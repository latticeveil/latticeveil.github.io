# The Ones Still Inside

Six days after Meridian's first field return, the west-path bell rang for one person.

Three had left beneath it.

Avery was above the cistern helping set the first lintel when the single low note crossed the village. He lowered his end of the stone before the sound had finished.

"Hold," he said.

The two village workers on the other end stopped with him. No one let go. Below them, the unfinished facility had reached shoulder height in one corner and nowhere useful in the others. Lime dust whitened the ground. A line of fitted stone waited beside the north wall.

The bell rang once because someone was returning.

The seed-store bell answered with one hard pull.

Help.

Avery looked toward the upper path.

A woman stumbled around the last crop wall with one hand pressed to her forehead. Dust covered her coat to the shoulders. Blood had dried along the side of her face. She crossed the watch mark alone.

Mara reached her first.

"Nema," Mara said, taking the woman's wrist. "Where are the others?"

Nema tried to answer before she had enough breath.

"The cut," she managed. "It put us back inside."

Avery left the lintel supported and went down.

Orin came from the seed store carrying the inspection slate. Eli followed her with Pebble already ahead of him. The Wayhound stopped before Nema and smelled the air around her without touching.

Orin looked at Avery. "Count."

"Nema returned," Avery said. "Corvin and Tavin missing."

Mara's grip tightened around Nema's wrist.

"Alive when I left," Nema said. "Both of them. Corvin made me go first."

"Which cut?" Avery asked.

"Old north loading cut," Nema answered. "Above the abandoned lime shelf. We were checking the bracewood before bringing any of it home."

Avery knew the entry. He and Corvin had marked it two days earlier from the outside and agreed that no one would remove material until a three-person inspection passed.

Three people had gone.

One had returned.

"What changed?" Orin asked.

Nema closed her eyes.

"Tavin found an iron brace behind the fallen screen," she said. "We cleared enough stone to see whether it still held weight. We did not pull it. Corvin touched the wall beside it, and the loading room came back behind us."

"Came back?" Eli asked.

"We walked for the entrance," Nema said. "We reached the loading room. We took the other passage. We reached it again. Third time, there were two of Corvin."

No one interrupted her.

Nema swallowed. "One told us to follow. The other told us not to move."

"Which did you choose?" Avery asked.

"The one who insulted Tavin's knot," Nema said.

Eli looked toward the upper route. "Reasonable authentication."

"It worked once," Nema replied. "Corvin found a narrow edge where the wall did not meet itself. He pushed me through. Tavin was behind me. The stone closed between us."

"How long?" Orin asked.

Mara answered before Nema could. "They were due at middle bell."

The sun had moved well past it.

Orin put the inspection slate into Avery's hand.

"Field authority is yours," Orin said.

"You stay with containment," Avery said.

"And with the return point," Orin answered. "A rescue needs somewhere honest to come back to."

Avery looked at Eli.

Eli had one hand near his flask. Not holding it. Measuring the distance.

"Can you work?" Avery asked.

"Yes," Eli answered.

"That was not the whole question," Avery said.

"It is the whole answer I have," Eli said. "If it changes, I report it."

Avery nodded. "Pebble?"

The Wayhound faced the north path. His ears had gone forward. He did not move.

"Pebble disapproves," Eli said.

"Useful," Avery replied.

Orin turned to Nema. "Show us the boundary. You do not cross it again."

Nema opened her mouth.

"You returned injured," Orin continued. "That makes you a witness, not another person to recover."

Nema looked at Mara.

Mara said, "Listen to her."

That settled it.

They packed in the cistern yard.

Two coils of doubled cord.

Black route chalk.

White witness chalk.

Three salt packets.

Four wooden pins.

One quiet locking brace from the seed store.

Water.

Bandages.

No active Veilglass.

No workshop tools.

Nothing Meridian could not leave behind.

Orin wrote the names herself.

Avery.

Eli.

Pebble.

Nema, boundary witness only.

Corvin and Tavin, recovery.

She drew a box around the last two.

"Living first," Orin said.

"Then close what caught them," Avery answered.

"Only if closing it does not take the living back," Orin said.

Eli tightened his pack. "And the bracewood?"

Mara looked toward the unfinished north wall.

The old loading cut held enough fitted bracewood and iron to finish it. More than enough. The village had no spare iron of that weight. Leaving it would cost weeks.

"The wall can remain short," Mara said.

Avery looked at her.

"People object to rain," Mara continued. "They object more strongly to being folded into stone."

The answer went onto the field slate.

Material secondary.

They left before the lime dust had settled from the interrupted work.

Nema led them north above the Rootvine shelves. She moved carefully but did not ask to slow. Mara had wrapped her forehead and made her drink. The blood had stopped. Her right hand still shook whenever the route narrowed.

Avery kept her between himself and Eli.

"Tell it once," Avery said.

Nema looked at him.

"Everything from the last honest marker to the place you came out," Avery said. "Tell it once. Eli records it. After that, we do not let the route teach us a different version."

Eli took out a slate.

Nema began.

The old north loading cut had once served three lime shelves. Workers lowered shaped stone from the upper cut, moved it through a covered gallery, and sent it down by rope platform. A collapse had closed the highest shelf years ago. The lower two remained reachable but were no longer worth maintaining.

Corvin knew the stone.

Tavin knew where the village had stored the old work marks.

Nema knew bracewood well enough to tell rot from weather.

They had crossed six honest markers. They had entered beneath a roofless arch. They had found the fallen screen and the iron brace behind it. Then the loading room began repeating.

"Roofless arch?" Avery asked.

Nema pointed ahead.

The arch stood against the sky.

It had no roof because it had never been a doorway. Two upright stones and one crosspiece marked where loaded platforms once changed hands. Beyond it, the path descended between cut walls pale with old lime.

Pebble stopped three paces before it.

Nema stopped behind him.

"This is where I stay," Nema said.

"This is where you witness," Avery corrected.

He gave her the end of the first cord.

"If it pulls once, hold," Avery said. "Twice, give one arm's length. Three times, cut it and return to the village."

Nema looked at the knife tied beside the coil.

"If I cut it, you lose the line," Nema said.

"If you do not cut it when told, the route may gain yours," Avery said.

Nema wrapped the cord around the honest marker, not around her body.

"One, hold," Nema said. "Two, length. Three, cut and return."

Avery drew a black boundary line before the arch.

Eli placed a white witness stroke beside it.

Nema read both aloud.

They crossed together.

The temperature changed first.

Not colder. Divided.

Avery's face felt sun from the open cut while his hands felt the wet chill of an underground room. Dust moved across his boots in two directions. Somewhere below, a wooden platform knocked against stone in a slow rhythm.

Pebble lowered his body and continued.

At the first turn, they found Nema's blood on the wall.

At the second, they found it again.

The marks were identical down to the thin place where her fingers had slipped.

Eli crouched between them.

"One happened," Eli said. "One remembers happening."

"Which?" Avery asked.

Eli did not answer immediately. He listened with his head slightly turned.

His fingers touched the flask.

He left it closed.

"The left mark has a body behind it," Eli said. "The right has the idea of one."

Avery marked the left wall.

"Your decision," Avery said.

Eli looked up.

"Mine?" Eli asked.

"You can tell them apart. I cannot," Avery said.

Eli glanced toward the repeating blood.

"Left passage," Eli said. "No one touches the right wall."

Avery wrote the choice and followed it.

The cord behind them remained slack.

The cut descended into the loading gallery. Half its old covering had fallen away. Sunlight entered through square gaps overhead, but the light did not reach the floor beneath the gaps. It continued across the empty air and ended against the far wall in bright, unsupported blocks.

Tavin's voice came from ahead.

"Avery?"

Avery stopped.

The voice came again from behind them.

"Avery?"

Then from beneath the floor.

"Avery?"

Eli closed his eyes.

"Do not answer yet," Eli said.

Avery waited.

Pebble turned toward the left wall. His nose pressed close to the stone.

Eli called, "Tavin, tell me what is wrong with the bell cord."

Silence.

The route repeated Avery's name twice more.

Then a faint voice answered from beyond the left wall.

"Which bell cord?"

Eli opened his eyes. "That one."

Avery put his palm against the stone.

It felt hollow.

"Corvin?" Avery called.

Three Corvins answered.

"Here."

"Do not follow that."

"Took you long enough."

The last voice sounded closest.

That did not make it true.

Eli leaned toward the wall. "Corvin, what did Tavin do to his first store knot?"

The nearest voice said, "Tied it."

The voice behind them said, "Badly."

The quietest voice, somewhere below the others, answered, "He made a personal insult out of cord."

Avery felt Eli go still beside him.

It was Eli's phrase from the cistern yard.

Corvin had been there.

"Third voice," Eli said. "Low and left."

Pebble scratched once at the base of the wall.

Avery tested the fitted stones. Most had been cut from pale shelf rock. One near the floor was darker, older, and marked with three short strokes beneath a single arrow.

He did not recognize the mark.

Eli did.

His breath caught.

"Later," Avery said.

Eli nodded too quickly.

Avery drove one wooden pin into the floor beside the dark stone. Eli tied the second cord to it, then passed the free end around his waist without knotting it.

"No," Avery said.

"I need both hands," Eli replied.

"Then loop it through your belt. No knot," Avery said.

Eli changed it.

Avery took the remaining slack. "You decide which layer is carrying them. I decide whether we move."

"That sounds suspiciously fair," Eli said.

"Report it when we return," Avery replied.

Eli put his left palm against the dark stone.

The loading gallery moved.

For one breath, Avery saw every version at once.

The roof whole.

The roof gone.

Workers hauling pale blocks where Pebble stood.

Tavin crouched in a narrow room with blood across one sleeve.

Corvin braced beside him, one shoulder driven against a sagging beam.

An iron brace lay between them and the exit.

Behind that, deeper than sight, rows of clean bracewood waited in a dry chamber.

Enough to finish the facility.

Enough to build another.

Then the layers closed.

Eli staggered.

Avery caught his coat.

"Report," Avery said.

"Two living," Eli answered. "One room. The room is inside the wall and farther down than it should be."

"Limiter?" Avery asked.

"Holding," Eli answered.

"Flask?" Avery asked.

Eli swallowed. "Closed."

"Keep it that way if you can. Open it if you need," Avery said.

Eli looked offended. "A brilliantly narrow range of options."

"Can you make the room answer one place?" Avery asked.

"Not make," Eli said. "Ask loudly."

Avery planted the quiet locking brace against the dark stone. It did not click.

Eli moved his palm from stone to metal.

The brace clicked once.

The wall answered below them.

Pebble barked.

The stone opened sideways.

Not like a door. Like two memories disagreeing about which one occupied the space.

A narrow gap appeared.

Tavin was on the other side.

His left sleeve was torn and dark at the elbow. He sat with both boots pressed against the iron brace on the floor. Corvin stood behind him under the sagging beam. A length of cord crossed Corvin's chest and held the beam against his remaining shoulder.

"Boundary," Avery said.

Tavin stared at him.

"Say it," Avery ordered.

The boy looked down. A white chalk line crossed the floor before his boots.

"Here," Tavin said. "The line is here."

"Witness?" Avery asked.

"Corvin," Tavin answered.

Corvin's face tightened under the beam. "Regrettably."

"Record?" Avery asked.

Tavin lifted a small slate from the floor.

Even trapped, he had written on it.

"Return?" Avery asked.

Tavin looked through the gap at him. "With both of us."

"Correct," Avery said.

The gap narrowed.

Eli pressed both hands against the locking brace.

It held.

"Tavin first," Avery said.

"Corvin cannot hold that alone," Tavin answered.

"I was holding it before you began helping," Corvin said.

"You were making noises," Tavin replied.

"Structural noises," Corvin said.

Avery fed the rescue cord through the gap.

"Tavin, loop it through your belt," Avery said. "Do not tie it. Corvin, on my count, let the beam settle onto the iron brace."

Corvin looked at the brace beneath Tavin's boots.

"That is what opened the room," Corvin said.

"Did you remove it?" Avery asked.

"No," Corvin answered. "The wall offered it. Tavin accepted the invitation with his foot."

"It moved when I stepped near it," Tavin said.

"Then we leave it where the room wants it until you are out," Avery said.

Beyond Corvin, the dry chamber of salvaged bracewood appeared through another false opening.

Cut beams.

Iron collars.

Old lifting plates.

All clean.

All waiting.

Tavin saw Avery looking.

"The north wall," Tavin said. "There is enough."

"There is enough wall at home to wait for us," Avery answered.

"It may disappear when we move the brace," Tavin said.

"Then it disappears," Avery replied.

Tavin's eyes moved to the fitted iron and timber.

"We came for it," Tavin said.

"You came back from it," Avery said. "Move."

Tavin looped the cord through his belt.

Corvin shifted his feet.

Avery counted.

On three, Corvin let the beam descend.

The iron brace caught it.

The whole pocket made a sound like a room inhaling.

Tavin came through the gap on his hands and knees. Avery pulled only when the boy moved. Pebble caught the back of Tavin's coat and dragged for the final pace.

The moment Tavin cleared the white line, the gap tried to close.

Eli cried out.

His arms shook against the locking brace.

Avery caught the metal beside his hands.

"Corvin," Avery called.

The one-armed man was visible only in pieces now. Shoulder. Face. One boot.

"The beam?" Avery asked.

"Held," Corvin answered.

"Can you reach the cord?" Avery asked.

Corvin looked down.

"Yes," Corvin said.

Avery pushed the free end through.

Corvin caught it with his one hand.

He did not tie it around himself.

Instead, he wrapped it once around the iron brace beneath the beam.

"What are you doing?" Avery asked.

"Bringing back the thing that caused the argument," Corvin answered.

"Leave it," Avery said.

"If I leave it, your opening closes before I reach it," Corvin replied.

Eli's knees struck the floor.

The gap widened and narrowed with his breathing.

"He is right," Eli said.

Avery looked at him.

"I dislike it too," Eli added.

Avery made the decision.

"Bring only the brace," Avery told Corvin. "Nothing behind you."

Corvin looked once toward the clean bracewood.

Then he pulled.

The old iron came free.

The beam dropped.

Corvin ran.

Not gracefully.

He ran like an old man who knew exactly how much falling stone he could survive and had no interest in testing the estimate.

He crossed the white line as the pocket collapsed behind him.

Avery and Tavin pulled the cord. Pebble caught Corvin's coat. Eli held the opening for one impossible second longer.

Then all of them landed in the loading gallery.

The dark fitted stone slammed back into place.

Silence followed.

Real silence.

Corvin lay on his back with the old iron brace across his chest.

Tavin crawled to him. "Are you alive?"

Corvin opened one eye. "Do not sound so surprised."

Eli slid down the wall.

Avery was beside him before the flask cleared his belt.

"Report," Avery said.

"Still me," Eli answered.

"Limiter?" Avery asked.

"Still there," Eli answered.

"Need?" Avery asked.

Eli looked at the flask in his hand.

The metal trembled against his fingers.

"Yes," Eli said.

He drank once.

No one pretended not to watch.

The tension left his jaw first. Then his shoulders. He capped the flask and gave it to Avery long enough to prove he could.

"Record one dose," Eli said.

Avery did.

Tavin sat against the wall while Nema's bandage packet became useful. His elbow was cut but not broken. Corvin's shoulder had swollen beneath the old cord. He refused to call it an injury until Mara could insult it properly.

The old iron brace lay quiet between them.

Past the dark stone, the clean salvage chamber was gone.

The wrong pressure was not.

Dust still moved in two directions.

The unsupported blocks of sunlight remained fixed against the far wall.

Pebble stared at the floor.

"People first," Avery said.

Eli took his flask back. "People recovered."

"Now we decide whether this can follow them home," Avery said.

Corvin pushed himself upright.

"It wanted the brace," Corvin said.

Tavin looked at the iron. "It moved before I touched it."

"Toward you?" Avery asked.

"Toward the wall," Tavin answered. "Like it had fallen out and wanted back."

Avery studied the dark fitted stone. The three short strokes and one-way arrow remained visible near its base.

"The brace was not salvage," Avery said. "It was part of the closure."

Eli leaned his head against the wall. "The pocket showed us a room full of useful things so we would carry the one thing it needed farther away."

"Or so we would stay while deciding," Avery said.

Corvin nudged the iron with one boot. "Rooms designed for one person to understand."

Avery looked at him.

The words were Avery's, spoken six days earlier in the village.

Corvin had heard them.

That was all.

"We return the brace," Avery said.

Tavin started to stand.

"You return to Nema," Avery told him.

"I can witness," Tavin said.

"You already did," Avery replied.

Tavin looked ready to argue.

Corvin spoke first. "Go tell Nema I am alive. She will not believe me without a less reliable witness."

Tavin glared at him.

Then he took the first cord and followed it back toward the roofless arch.

Corvin remained seated.

"You too," Avery said.

"One arm does not prevent observation," Corvin replied.

"No. The shoulder does," Avery said.

Corvin considered that.

"Mara has been teaching you," Corvin said.

"Go," Avery replied.

Corvin went.

Avery, Eli, and Pebble stayed with the iron brace.

They did not reopen the pocket.

They cleansed its hold on the local layer instead.

Avery marked the original socket by its wear. Eli listened for the version of the wall carrying weight rather than offering it. Pebble stood at the one place where the dust moved only toward the exit.

Boundary.

Witness.

Record.

Return.

Eli held the wrong pressure quiet.

Avery seated the old brace.

Pebble barked when the angle was true.

Avery drove it home with the wrapped heel of the quiet Meridian brace.

The old iron rang once.

Every false patch of sunlight fell to the floor.

Dust settled.

The cold left Avery's hands.

Somewhere inside the wall, a loading platform struck stone one final time and stopped.

Eli removed his palm.

"Closed?" Avery asked.

"Closed enough to mark," Eli answered. "Not safe enough to forget."

Avery drew the warning.

No salvage.

No entry.

Pocket stabilized.

Village authority required before further work.

They left the Meridian locking brace seated against the wall as a monitor. It was worth more than anything they carried home.

That made leaving it easier.

At the roofless arch, Nema counted them aloud.

Tavin.

Corvin.

Avery.

Eli.

Pebble.

Then herself.

The cord was recovered last.

Everyone sent had returned.

On the walk home, Tavin stayed beside Nema and gave her three different explanations for why his elbow did not hurt. Corvin followed behind them carrying nothing heavier than his own injured shoulder. Mara would still object to that much.

Eli walked beside Avery.

"You gave me the route decision," Eli said.

"You were the only one who could make it," Avery answered.

"That has not stopped you from trying before," Eli replied.

"I am becoming tolerable under controlled conditions," Avery said.

Eli looked back toward the sealed cut. "If I had chosen the wrong blood mark, we would still be in there."

"If you had refused to choose, they would still be in there," Avery replied.

Eli was quiet for several steps.

"You know you are my best friend," Eli said. "That makes your confidence unusually irritating."

Avery checked the path ahead because looking directly at Eli would have made the answer more difficult than it needed to be.

"I had reached the first conclusion," Avery said.

"And the second?" Eli asked.

"Still gathering evidence," Avery answered.

Eli struck Avery's arm lightly with the back of one hand.

They continued shoulder to shoulder.

They reached the village near sunset.

The upper-path bell sounded one low note when the returning group came into view.

The watch counted all six across the outer mark.

Mara met Corvin with both hands on her hips.

"Shoulder," she said.

"Still attached," Corvin answered.

"That was not the question," Mara said.

"It was the useful part," Corvin replied.

Mara directed him toward the cistern bench. Nema sat beside him while Tavin gave the rescue report too quickly and had to begin again.

Orin listened without interrupting.

When Tavin finished, she looked at Corvin.

"Your name has entered three reports," Orin said. "I would prefer to know whether it is the one you use."

"Most days," Corvin answered.

Tavin frowned. "What does that mean?"

Mara unwrapped Corvin's shoulder.

"It means Corvin was the only name he thought might be his when we found him," Mara said. "He was alone beyond the lower crop wall. Hurt badly. One arm gone. No memory of where he came from or how he reached us."

Tavin stopped fidgeting with the report slate.

"He never remembered?" Tavin asked.

"Not where he came from," Mara answered. "Not who he had been."

Corvin looked at the half-built facility above the yard.

"I remembered stone," Corvin said. "People needed walls. It seemed impolite to wait for the rest."

Tavin studied him.

"Corvin," Tavin repeated. "Like corvid?"

Eli looked up from cleaning white chalk off the rescue cord.

"A bird family," Eli said. "Crows. Ravens."

His hands stopped.

"Rooks," Eli finished.

The yard continued around the word.

Water moved in the cistern lift.

Someone stacked the unused north-wall stone.

The hauler worried at an empty feed rope.

Avery saw Rook at the workshop threshold, already moving toward the bad idea before anyone could finish naming it. He saw him carrying Elijah through a collapsing route. He saw the corridor afterward.

Beside him, Eli lowered his eyes to the cord.

For one breath, neither of them moved on.

Corvin broke the silence.

"Then we have established I may once have been a bird," Corvin said. "That explains the missing memories but raises serious questions about the arm."

Tavin laughed first.

Eli did not laugh, but his hands began moving again.

Avery let the breath out.

Coincidence was allowed to hurt.

It did not have to become an answer.

Orin set the rescue slate beside Avery's field record.

"The pocket?" Orin asked.

"Living recovered before stabilization," Avery said. "All visible salvage left in place. Original closure brace reseated. One Meridian brace left as a pressure monitor."

"Contamination?" Orin asked.

Eli answered, "Local pressure settled. The internal layer still exists, but it is no longer folding the work route around itself."

"Then it is contained, not cured," Orin said.

"Contained and marked," Avery replied.

Mara tied Corvin's shoulder wrap hard enough to make him reconsider several opinions.

"And closed to village work," Mara said.

Avery nodded. "Until you authorize another inspection."

Mara looked at the unfinished facility wall.

"It can wait," Mara said.

Orin turned to Tavin. "Your record."

The boy handed her the slate he had kept inside the pocket.

Most of it held times, repeated voices, and the number of false exits. At the bottom he had copied the mark from the dark fitted stone: three short strokes beneath a single arrow pointing one way.

Orin studied it.

Then she took an older route book from the seed-store shelf.

"This was not a loading mark," Orin said.

Avery came to the table.

Orin turned three pages, then four more.

The same three strokes and single arrow appeared beside a faded route designation.

RIDGE FIELD NODE.

SURVEY CLOSED AFTER LOSS.

Below it, one instruction remained legible.

IF THE ROOM GOES QUIET, STOP.

IF THE QUIET STOPS, LEAVE.

Avery knew those words.

He had knelt over them in the temporary survey chamber during the first route he and Eli crossed together. At the time, Avery had known he had read them somewhere before without knowing where.

Eli stood so quickly the bench scraped stone.

Pebble was awake beneath the table before the sound ended.

Avery looked at Eli. "What?"

Eli stared at the old mark.

"We know that," Eli said.

Orin kept one hand on the route book. "From where?"

Eli touched the edge of the page without touching the ink. "The ridge field node. Our first route."

"We survived it," Avery said.

Eli shook his head.

"I remember more than that," Eli said.

His face had gone pale.

"The natural arch," Eli continued. "The fork that made both passages feel like the same passage. The missing limiter. The field node."

"That is the route we crossed," Avery said.

"I crossed it before," Eli replied.

Pebble pressed against his leg.

Eli looked down at him, then back at the mark.

"I was small," Eli said. "The room went quiet. Then the quiet broke. There was light in the ceiling and something moving where the shadow should have ended."

Orin did not touch him. "What happened after that?"

Eli closed his eyes.

"I was on the wrong side of a line," Eli said. "Someone caught my wrist. There was chalk ground into the side of their thumb."

Avery looked at his own hand before he could stop himself.

Old chalk sat in the creases. Nothing more.

"Did you see who it was?" Avery asked.

"No," Eli answered. "I remember the hand. I remember being told not to let the place finish the thought."

Avery felt the words settle badly.

Eli had used them during their first crossing of the ridge field node. Avery had used them after him. Neither had ever explained where the sentence came from.

"Then I remember daylight," Eli said. "I was outside. I don't remember crossing the doorway. I don't remember who pulled me through it."

"But you survived," Tavin said.

Eli opened his eyes.

"Yes," Eli answered. "That is the part I have never known how to explain."

No one asked the next question quickly.

That was another Meridian rule, though they had not written it yet.

Orin closed the route book.

"No one follows this tonight," Orin said.

Eli's hand found the flask.

"I was not going to," Eli replied.

"Good," Orin answered. "Tomorrow, we compare the mark, the pocket record, and every route account the village has. If the evidence holds, we authorize one controlled follow-up."

Avery looked toward the dark north path.

The rescue had succeeded.

The pocket had been contained.

The people Meridian sent had come home.

For the first time, that success had left them with somewhere worse to go.
