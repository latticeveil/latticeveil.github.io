# FRAME 12
# SOUTH CROP WALL

Orin went first.

That was not discussed. It happened the way necessary things happened around her: without announcement, without invitation, and with very little room left for complaint.

Rook watched her step onto the narrow lower path and made a small thoughtful sound.

Avery looked at him. "No."

Rook's eyebrows lifted. "I have not said anything."

Avery kept his eyes on him. "You were arranging to."

Rook put a hand to his chest. "That is an ugly accusation from a man who needs my road."

"I need a road," Avery said. "The distinction may become important."

Eli moved past both of them with one hand near the wall and the other loose beside his flask. He did not smile. The scraped stone below them had gone quiet, which Avery disliked more than he had disliked the sound.

Quiet could mean distance.

Quiet could mean patience.

Behind them, the field medic had already turned back toward the workers. She had two people hauling rope and one person stripping clean cloth into narrow lengths with her teeth. The dead man still lay below, not yet recovered. The smoke from the lower cook pits bent hard westward in the wind.

Food and medicine first.

Then chalk and salt.

Then cord.

Then whatever the east road chose to be.

Avery adjusted the strap across his shoulder and stepped after Orin.

The road Rook knew was not a road.

It had been a road once, in the same way a bone was a person once. The idea remained. The useful shape had been argued down by weather, neglect, and the cliff's long preference for taking things back.

It dropped along the outer face of Anchorfall in broken turns. Old hand slots cut the left wall. Low anchor posts leaned at intervals, each marked with red cord that had faded brown where sun and salt wind had worried it thin. Below, terraces stepped down the stone in uneven shelves: crop walls, cistern roofs, storage sheds pressed against ledges, rope frames without platforms, and paths narrow enough that Avery felt his knees make opinions.

Rook moved with irritating ease.

He did not hurry. That made it worse.

He put one boot where a crack widened, one hand where a rootvine had thickened around an old bracket, and his weight seemed to arrive after the decision had already been proven correct.

Orin noticed too. She said nothing.

Eli noticed and said, "You could at least look worried."

Rook glanced back. "I am worried."

Eli looked over Rook's very calm expression. "Your face forgot."

Rook touched his cheek as if checking. "My face is conserving strength."

"That is not where you keep it," Orin said.

Rook's mouth opened.

Avery said, "Think carefully."

Rook closed it again and looked deeply wronged.

The path narrowed near a dry runnel where water had once crossed from an upper cistern to the lower terraces. Stone channels cut through the wall, no wider than Avery's hand, each angled to send overflow from one shelf to the next. Most were empty. One was not.

Water seeped from a split above them, thin and bright, sliding over the path and vanishing beneath a scatter of grit. At first it looked harmless. A wet place on stone. An annoyance.

Then Avery saw the way the grit had arranged itself.

Too evenly.

"Stop," Avery said.

Orin had already stopped.

Eli's boot hovered half a breath above the wet stone. He held there, one hand flat to the wall, body suddenly still in a way that made the cliff seem to move around him.

Rook stopped behind Avery. "That is inconvenient."

"Water usually is," Avery said.

Rook looked at the wet grit. "Not usually with intent."

Eli lowered his boot back to the dry side of the path. His fingers pressed into the stone.

"It is not the water," Eli said.

Orin turned her head slightly. "Then what?"

Eli did not answer at once. His mouth tightened. His thumb moved once over the flask at his hip and stopped before touching the cap.

Avery waited.

He had learned that pushing Eli too quickly rarely produced the answer. It only produced noise around the answer.

Eli looked down the slope, past the water line, past the next two terraces, to a lower crop wall where gray-green Rootvine grew in vertical rows against lattice frames.

"Something under the cistern wants the road to think it has already broken," Eli said.

Rook blinked. "Roads think?"

"No," Avery said.

Eli looked at him.

Avery amended, "Not helpfully."

Orin crouched near the wet stone without touching it. The pale cord at her wrist hung still, square weights resting against the back of her hand.

"Old overflow channel," Orin said. "Bad repair under it."

"How bad?" Avery asked.

Orin took one of the square weights between two fingers and set it gently against the wet grit.

The path clicked.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. A small stone sound, exact enough to be worse than a crack.

The wet section dropped half an inch.

Eli exhaled through his teeth.

Rook leaned a little to see past Avery. "I withdraw my earlier confidence in the road."

"You had confidence?" Avery asked.

Rook straightened. "I had momentum."

"Keep less of it," Avery said.

Orin lifted the weight away. The path held where it had settled, but the grit shifted into a shape Avery recognized from damaged route edges: a false invitation. The wet stone looked smoother after the drop. Easier. Safer than the dry traverse along the outer lip.

That was the lie.

"No one steps there," Orin said.

"We can cut above," Rook said.

Orin looked at him.

Rook lifted both hands. "A road above. A worse one. Honest terrible, as previously recommended."

Eli gave him a quick look. "You remembered."

Rook smiled. "I am full of surprises."

"Most cracked things are," Orin said.

They went above.

The worse road began behind a cistern house built into the cliff. Its roof was flat stone, its sides patched with old seal paste, and three narrow channels ran from its lip into the terraces below. Someone had painted measure marks beside the channels years ago. Most had faded. The lowest mark remained dark, a black line at knee height.

This season had not been generous.

The climb above the cistern forced them through a maintenance cut barely wide enough for shoulders. Avery went sideways twice. Rook went through without touching either wall, which Avery chose not to dignify by noticing aloud. Eli scraped his knuckles on a bracket and hissed something under his breath.

"Bleeding?" Avery asked.

"Pride," Eli said.

"That is rarely fatal," Avery said.

Eli flexed his scraped hand. "You have not met mine properly."

At the far side of the cut, the path widened enough for all four of them to stand on a ledge above the south crop wall.

The terrace village lay below.

It did not look like a village at first. It looked like the cliff had learned to store hands.

Rows of Rootvine climbed lattice frames in gray-green ropes. Low sheds clung beneath overhangs, their roofs weighted with flat stones against the wind. Drying racks stood under patched cloth awnings, hung with strips of root, dark leaves, and pale bandage herb tied in bundles. Cistern mouths broke the ground at intervals, each ringed in chalk marks and foot-polished stone. A narrow yard held seed jars under a slanted roof. Beyond it, three field kitchens smoked low and careful.

People moved through it with the sharp economy of those who had more work than day.

No one looked up for long.

That told Avery enough.

Places that lived below a damaged route learned not to waste fear on every shadow.

They saved it for when the shadow stayed.

Rook stopped beside a rope ladder tied to two anchor rings and pointed down. "South crop wall."

"That is a ladder," Avery said.

Rook looked pleased with himself. "Yes."

Avery looked from the ladder to him. "You promised a road."

Rook touched the side rope. "I promised I knew one."

Avery folded his arms. "This is not one."

"It leads where we are going," Rook said.

Orin stepped onto the ladder first.

Avery watched the knots take her weight. They held.

Rook smiled at him.

Avery said, "If you say anything about trust, I will cut the ladder after you."

Rook put one boot on the top rung. "I would never."

Eli put a hand on the rope and looked down. "He would."

"I would," Rook said.

The ladder descended three terraces and ended beside a shed full of empty baskets. A boy with a carrying strap across his chest froze when Orin stepped off. He looked at the pale cord on her wrist, then at Rook, then at Avery's torn sleeve, then at Eli.

His eyes stopped on Eli's flask.

Eli noticed.

He always noticed when someone noticed.

"It is not medicine," Eli said.

The boy swallowed. "I did not ask."

"Good," Eli said.

Orin said, "Where is your grower head?"

The boy pointed toward the field kitchens without taking his eyes off them.

"Alive?" Orin asked.

The boy hesitated.

That was answer enough to make Avery's shoulders settle badly.

"For now," the boy said.

They followed him through the terrace yard.

The village smelled of wet stone, root starch, smoke, bruised leaves, and tired people. A woman at a drying rack stopped tying bundles long enough to count them. An old man with one arm kept stirring a pot with a paddle tied to his wrist. Two girls carried water skins between them, each walking with knees bent against the slant of the path.

Rook's face changed as they passed the field kitchens.

Not much. Enough.

Orin saw it.

"You have been here," Orin said.

Rook kept walking. "I have been many places."

Orin matched his pace. "With permission?"

"That narrows the list," Rook said.

Orin's mouth thinned.

Rook looked toward a shed with a cracked lintel. "I carried a man down from the rope road once. He had decided gravity was negotiable."

"It is not," Orin said.

Rook's mouth tilted without much humor. "He learned."

Orin's voice changed by almost nothing. "Did he live?"

Rook's eyes stayed on the shed. "Long enough to complain that I had carried him badly."

Orin said nothing.

For a moment, neither did Rook.

Avery filed that away with the rest of the things Rook tried to turn sideways before anyone could see their full shape.

The grower head was not in the field kitchens.

She was in a medic niche behind them, sitting on an overturned seed crate while someone wrapped her ribs. She had white hair cut short against her skull, forearms corded from field work, and a bruise darkening along the side of her jaw. When the boy brought them in, she pushed the wrapping hand away and stood.

She made it halfway before pain took the rest.

Avery stepped forward before deciding to.

She held up one hand.

"If you touch me before you say what you need," Mara said, "I will assume you came to steal."

Avery stopped.

Rook murmured, "Warm village."

"Quiet," Orin said.

The grower head looked at Orin's cord. "Sister."

Orin inclined her head. "Mara."

Rook's eyebrows moved.

Avery noticed that too.

Mara noticed Avery noticing and looked unimpressed by all of them.

"Anchorfall shelf is damaged," Orin said. "One dead. Several wounded. The medic is low on bandage herbs, clean cloth, field food, and anything that keeps people standing long enough to move."

Mara's jaw worked once.

Mara's jaw tightened. "How many wounded?"

"Enough," Avery said.

Her eyes came to him. "That is a field answer."

"Yes," Avery said.

Mara looked him over. "I dislike field answers."

Avery held her look. "So do I."

That earned him the smallest possible approval.

Mara looked past him to Eli. "And what does that one hear?"

Eli went still.

The room went with him, not because anyone understood the question, but because everyone felt the shape of it.

Avery turned his head toward Orin.

Orin's expression gave him nothing.

Eli said, "Today?"

Mara's face did not soften. "Yes."

Eli looked toward the outer wall, toward the terraces and the broken road above them.

"The cliff is holding its breath," Eli said.

The person wrapping Mara's ribs made a small sign with two fingers against her own sleeve.

Mara closed her eyes for one breath.

Mara opened her eyes. "Then we do not argue over roots. Tavin, open the second shed. Not the first. The second. Give them dried Rootvine, travel loaves, bandage herb, ghostleaf if any of it has not browned, and two water skins."

The boy who had led them down nodded too quickly and ran.

"Not ghostleaf," Orin said.

Mara looked at her. "You asked for medicine."

Orin's hand tightened on her cord. "For wounds. Not sleep."

"People in pain need sleep," Mara said.

"People on a damaged shelf need to wake when the stone moves," Orin said.

Mara held her stare. Then she looked to the woman at her side. "Half bundle. For later. Mark it."

The woman nodded and went after the boy.

Rook leaned slightly toward Avery. "They like each other."

"I heard you," Mara said.

Rook straightened. "I hoped so. I hate wasting charm."

Mara's attention stayed on Rook. "I remember."

That shut him up.

Avery looked from Mara to Rook.

Rook looked away first.

Interesting.

Not immediately useful.

But interesting.

Mara sat back down because standing had become too expensive. "You will not get chalk here."

"We know," Avery said.

Mara leaned back against the crate. "Or salt enough for a damaged route."

Avery nodded. "We know that too."

"Then you know you still have to take the east road," Mara said.

Eli's hand moved toward the flask. This time he touched it. Not the cap. The dented side.

"Yes," Eli said.

Mara watched him. "Do not take the lower rope bend."

Rook said, "That was my plan."

Four people looked at him.

He sighed. "Formerly."

Mara pointed one thick finger at him. "The bend has been taking carts."

"People?" Avery asked.

"Not unless they are foolish enough to hold on," Mara said.

"That will be comforting to the carts," Orin said.

Mara's mouth twitched and then stopped because her ribs objected.

The supplies came in three baskets and two wrapped bundles. Dried Rootvine packed in cloth. Travel loaves hard enough to survive a fall. Bandage herb tied with red thread. A smaller packet marked twice in charcoal. Clean cloth. Two water skins. A jar of paste that smelled bitter enough to be useful.

Avery checked the bundles by habit.

Mara watched.

Mara watched his hands. "You think I would cheat wounded workers?"

"No," Avery said. "I think knots fail when people are tired."

Mara accepted that.

Eli took one basket and adjusted its strap across his shoulder. He winced when the weight settled, then hid it poorly.

Avery saw.

Eli saw him seeing.

"Do not," Eli said.

Avery did not move closer. "I have not."

Eli shifted the basket again. "You were arranging to."

Rook made a pleased sound. "It spreads."

Orin took the heaviest medicine bundle before anyone could offer it to someone else. "We move."

Mara caught her sleeve as she turned.

Orin looked down at the hand.

"The thing below?" Mara asked.

Orin did not answer quickly.

That, too, was an answer.

"Gone for now," Orin said.

Mara's grip tightened. "That is what people say when a thing is not gone."

"Yes," Orin said.

The grower head let go.

Mara let go of Orin's sleeve. "Then take the upper shelf out. Not the lower. If anyone stops you, do not let them draw blood near the cistern marks."

Avery looked at her. "Why?"

"Because blood makes people stupid," Mara said. "And because the marks remember poorly."

Orin nodded once.

Avery did not ask whether that was belief, route-law, or experience.

In Anchorfall, those categories were beginning to look like the same tool held by different hands.

They left by the upper shelf.

The village did not watch them leave openly. That would have been rude, or fearful, or an admission that the village had given away more than it could spare. People found work that let them look sideways. A woman shook out cloth near the path. The one-armed man carried an empty pot farther than necessary. The boy Tavin stood beside the second shed and pretended to count jars.

Rook lifted two fingers to him.

The boy did not wave back.

"Popular," Eli said.

"Children are difficult audiences," Rook said.

Eli adjusted the basket strap. "He is old enough to know better."

"Exactly," Rook said.

The upper shelf curved behind three rain cisterns and a wall of Rootvine frames. The plants climbed in braided columns, leaves narrow and dark, roots thickened around the lower slats. Water ticked somewhere inside the cistern stone. The path here was wide enough for two people to pass if both had been raised without fear of death.

Avery kept the medicine bundle on his inside shoulder.

Orin walked ahead.

Eli walked behind Avery.

Rook had drifted last, which Avery disliked because it seemed intentional.

"You are watching the village," Avery said.

Rook did not pretend otherwise. "I am watching the road behind the village."

"Difference?" Avery asked.

"Village roads apologize when they betray you," Rook said.

"No, they do not," Orin said.

"Some do," Rook said.

"You hear apologies from stone often?" Avery asked.

Rook glanced at Eli. "I try not to compete."

Eli's reply did not come.

Avery turned.

Eli had stopped near the last cistern.

His hand was not on the flask now. It was against the stone ring of the cistern, two fingers set near a chalk measure mark that had been scrubbed nearly away.

"Eli," Avery said.

Eli blinked once.

"Someone is waiting where the path narrows," Eli said.

Rook's easy posture vanished.

Orin did not look back. "How many?"

Eli's eyes shifted toward the bend ahead. The path went between a storage wall and an outer drop where wind lifted loose dust in pale strips.

"More than four," Eli said. "Less than brave."

Rook breathed out through his nose. "That is a very specific number."

"It is a very specific feeling," Eli said.

Avery lowered his voice. "Can we go around?"

Rook looked over the outer edge. Then up toward the terrace above. Then toward the village behind them.

"Around means back through Mara's yard," Rook said. "Or down a ladder that no one has loved in a decade."

"So no," Avery said.

"So not well," Rook said.

Orin turned then.

"No blades unless they draw blood first," Orin said.

Rook looked faintly insulted. "I was raised with manners."

"You were raised with witnesses," Orin said.

"Also true," Rook said.

Avery shifted the basket strap higher. "We are carrying medicine."

"They know," Eli said.

That settled coldly into the space between them.

They walked on.

The path narrowed exactly where Eli had said it would. A low storage wall pressed close on the left, built from flat stone and sealed with gray paste. On the right, the terrace dropped to a lower frame of Rootvine and wind-twisted stakes. Farther back, the village moved in fragments: cloth, smoke, a shoulder at a doorway, someone turning away too late.

Close enough to know.

Not close enough to see cleanly.

Six people waited at the bend.

Not villagers. Not workers.

They wore road layers patched from too many places: waxed cloth, torn climbing wraps, old anchor-crew belts without the marks that made them honest. One had a hook tool held low at his thigh. One carried a short club wrapped in cord. A woman near the wall had a field knife angled backward along her forearm. Another had two rope weights looped through one hand. The youngest held nothing Avery could see, which made him watch the boy more closely.

The man in front smiled without warmth.

"Long way from the shelf," the man in front said.

Rook leaned slightly toward Avery. "Do you want to do the talking, or should I make it worse first?"

Avery did not look away from the bandits. "Neither."

Orin stepped forward one pace. "Move."

The man looked her over. His eyes touched the pale cord at her wrist, paused, and decided badly.

The man shifted the hook tool against his shoulder. "Road is closed."

"No," Orin said. "It is occupied."

The woman by the wall laughed once.

The man lifted the hook tool and let it rest against his shoulder. It was not a farming hook. Too short. Too thick near the grip. A route-clearing tool, probably stolen, edge filed fresh.

"Food and medicine pass here," the man said. "People carrying them do not always need to."

Avery felt Eli change behind him.

Not move.

Change.

Avery set his free hand slightly outward, palm low. Stay.

Eli saw it. His jaw tightened. He stayed.

"There are wounded people above," Avery said. "We are taking this to them."

"There are hungry people everywhere," the man said.

"Then ask the village," Avery said.

The woman near the wall smiled around the edge of her knife. "We did."

Orin's voice cut flat. "You took."

The man's smile thinned.

Rook sighed. "This is a poor road for robbery."

The rope-weight bandit looked at him. "You offering a better one?"

"Several," Rook said. "Most involve you leaving."

The rope-weight bandit did not smile. "Funny."

"Occasionally," Rook said.

The bandit's grip tightened around the weights.

Avery shifted the medicine bundle off his shoulder and set it carefully against the storage wall where a torn strap would not spill half the village's mercy into the dust. Eli watched the bundle go down. The woman with the knife watched Eli.

Orin saw all of it.

"Last warning," Orin said.

The front man lowered the hook tool from his shoulder. "That was ours."

"No," Orin said. "It was generous before you reached for it."

The youngest bandit moved his empty hand closer to his belt.

Rook clicked his tongue softly. "I would not."

The boy froze.

For one breath, no one moved.

Wind slipped between the storage wall and the drop. Somewhere behind them, a cistern ticked water into stone.

The front man looked from Orin to Avery, from Avery to Eli, from Eli to Rook, and found whatever answer he wanted badly enough to mistake for truth.

"Step aside," the front man said. "Leave the baskets."

Avery looked once toward the village.

A woman at the nearest drying rack had gone still with both hands full of leaves. Tavin stood half behind a cistern pier, eyes wide, not hidden enough. Mara was not visible. Good. That meant someone had sense.

Not enough sense to look away.

Enough to live with what they saw.

"No one needs to be hurt," Avery said.

The man with the hook smiled again.

"Then stop standing where hungry men need to walk," the man with the hook said.

Eli's hand left the flask.

Not toward it.

Away.

Empty.

Avery saw the effort it took.

Orin stepped into the center of the path.

Rook rolled one shoulder, loose as rope coming off a hook.

The front bandit set his feet.

Orin's voice stayed level.

"Then we go through you," Orin said.
