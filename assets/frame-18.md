# Afterimage

The split held for less than a minute before it started becoming four different emergencies.

Eli disappeared north under old survey marks that looked too patient to trust.

Rook went east with another man's habits waking in his hands.

Sister Orin stayed where she had promised, one hand near the frame, the other never far from the Frame Jack. The Anchor Maw Core burned in Malrec's unfinished work like a heart that had not agreed to belong to anyone.

Avery took west.

He moved alone through a cut of worked stone where old brace slots ran in careful lines and pale seam-light gathered along the ceiling. Kaden's voice came through in pieces, naming positions that sounded less like a map with every breath: Rook east, Elijah moving, Dr. Avery Chen alive but stopped, Sarah closing on a signal that should not have had Avery's name on it.

Avery paused at the edge of a broken chamber.

Someone was already inside.

She stood with a black tablet half-raised in one hand, shoulders held too still, face caught between recognition and refusal. The screen lit her from below with clean, cold certainty.

For one breath, Avery understood exactly what mistake she had made.

Sarah did not lower the tablet.

Not because it could help her. Because her hands had not yet agreed that they were empty.

The man across the chamber waited where he was, balanced and watchful in the pale seam-light. Up close, the resemblance held only in pieces. The shape of the face. The mouth when still. The dark concentration in the eyes before speech changed it into something else. He was not her husband. The certainty had already landed. Her body had simply taken longer than her mind to obey it.

The device in her hand still read:

Avery.
Exact.

The stranger glanced at the screen, then back at her. "You know me?"

"No," Sarah said.

It came out too quickly to sound convincing.

His expression shifted by a fraction. Not offense. Assessment.

Avery kept his voice careful. "You said the name like you did."

Sarah forced air into her lungs and tried again. "I know someone with your face badly enough to have made a mistake for one second."

That answer seemed to satisfy him more than the first. Not because it was good. Because it was honest.

He took one careful step nearer, enough for light to catch the edge of a survey brace at his hip and the chalk dust ground into one sleeve. "Avery," the stranger said. "Just Avery."

Sarah almost laughed at the cruelty of that.

"Dr. Sarah Chen," Sarah said. "And yes. I know."

He watched her over the tablet. "You thought I was someone else."

Sarah's answer came flat with effort. "My husband."

Avery absorbed that without visible discomfort, which made him immediately unlike the men she had expected to meet in this world and far too much like the one she had left behind.

"You don't think that now," Avery said.

Sarah shook her head once. "No."

The tablet buzzed once against her palm as if objecting.

Sarah looked down at it, then held the display out a little so he could see without needing to touch it. "This is the problem."

He studied the screen, not understanding the interface but understanding enough to recognize certainty when it was rendered in a machine.

"Our tracking systems don't identify people by clothing or movement," Sarah said. "Not even by voice. They go deeper than that. Tissue. chemistry. identity markers that are supposed to belong to one body and one body only."

His eyes moved from the screen to her face.

Sarah heard the thinness in her own voice and pressed through it anyway. "It isn't confusing you with him because you look alike. It's reading you as if you and my Avery share the same underlying answer. As if this place has... another version of us written into it."

The chamber went very still.

Not because anything changed in the stone. Because the sentence had settled between them and refused to pretend it was smaller than it was.

Avery looked past her for a moment, toward the dark route she had taken in. "We've seen places where the Continuum repeats a choice until it becomes a habit," Avery said. "And thin places where one path lays over another badly. But a person..." He shook his head once. "If that's true, I don't know the whole of it."

Sarah let out a breath she had not realized she was holding.

The admission did more for her trust than certainty would have.

Static burst in her ear.

Kaden's voice arrived torn almost flat by interference, but present. "Sarah?"

She touched the comm at once. "Still here."

The relief on the other side was brief and tightly managed. "Your signal did something ugly. I lost you for twelve seconds."

"I found the reason," Sarah said, eyes still on Avery. "And it isn't my Avery."

Silence.

Then Kaden, dry even through static: "That is specific in exactly the wrong direction."

For the first time since stepping into the chamber, Sarah felt the edge of a laugh.

"I'm with someone local," Sarah said. "His name is Avery."

That bought her another beat of silence.

"Of course it is," Kaden said.

Avery looked faintly offended by the idea that his name might be a tactical inconvenience.

"Can you still see the others?" Sarah asked.

"In pieces," Kaden said. "Rook's still east. Elijah is moving. Dr. Avery Chen is still alive, which I realize is not the same thing as useful. Eli went north and then stopped being cooperative."

Avery's attention sharpened at that. "Stopped how?"

Sarah relayed the line.

He frowned toward the corridor behind him. "That likely means north stopped deserving him."

Sarah looked from him to the chamber exit. "Can you get me to the portal?"

"Probably," Avery said.

The answer was too measured to be comforting, which made it trustworthy.

He took another step, slower this time, giving her room to refuse. "If we're moving together, put that thing away before you let it convince you I'm your husband again."

Sarah slipped the tablet down, not because she liked being told to, but because he was right.

"I only made the mistake once," Sarah said.

Avery glanced at her as he turned toward the route. "Then we can both try not to improve on it."

She followed him out of the chamber.

*

At the portal, Sister Orin heard Kaden lose Sarah before he admitted it.

The small relay on the stone did not go silent. Silence would have been kinder. It thinned instead, turning his voice into a wire pulled too tight between worlds.

Orin kept one hand on the Frame Jack and one on the old brace stone. The Anchor Maw Core burned in the center of Malrec's frame, white in its heart and amber at the edges, bright enough to make the chalk marks look freshly cut. The opening looked calm from the wrong angle.

That was how unfinished things survived long enough to kill people. They learned to look finished.

Kaden came back with Sarah's voice somewhere behind him and tried to make relief sound like procedure.

Orin tightened the jack by a fraction.

The frame complained under her hands.

"You are not only carrying voices," Orin said.

Kaden did not answer at first.

Static moved around him. In it, Orin heard distance, not empty space, but a place that had been made to hold a man open.

"No," Kaden said at last. "Not only voices."

Orin looked at the frame, not the relay. "Then spend carefully."

Kaden's answer came after a pause. "I am familiar with being spent."

That was too much truth for a stranger and not enough for the wound underneath it.

Orin set her jaw and turned the Frame Jack again.

*

Eli went north for eleven minutes before deciding the corridor was lying to him.

It had all the right pieces. Older survey cuts at shoulder height. Brace slots in the walls. A dry mineral smell instead of active seam-burn. Nothing obviously wrong.

That was the problem.

The passage felt archived.

Not dead. Worse. Finished. A route with its decision already behind it.

Eli stopped beneath a low beam and listened to the rock.

Nothing.

Not silence exactly. The Continuum was never generous enough for real silence. But no pull. No live correction in the seams. No pressure at the back of the teeth saying left, idiot, unless you enjoy becoming an example.

He hated corridors that behaved too well. They always turned out to be proud of it.

His hand found the flask in the outer pocket of his coat without looking. The metal was cool against his palm, worn smooth where his thumb always landed. He unscrewed it, took a short pull, and let the burn settle him back into proportions he trusted.

"North is excellent," he muttered to the empty passage. "If your goal is disappointing me in a structurally sound way."

He capped the flask again and stood there long enough to admit the second problem.

He was thinking about Rook.

Not abstractly. Practically. The route had bent around the man. Not obeyed him. Not welcomed him. Bent. Like stone trying to remember a weight it had once carried and resenting how close he came to fitting.

Eli had seen enough unstable places to know the difference between danger and a live thread.

North had danger.

Rook had thread.

And beneath that, another thing Eli did not yet have a clean word for: interest sharpened by familiarity he had not earned. Every time Rook looked at a mark before understanding why it mattered, something in Eli's spine answered as if the route had tapped the wrong surveyor and gotten close enough anyway.

He glanced back the way he had come, toward the portal and the sensible plan he was now in the process of insulting.

"Avery can be right at me later," Eli said.

The eastward pull was faint, but it existed. Enough.

Eli turned off the north path and started cutting across broken side-work toward the route Rook had taken, following disturbance more than footprints.

The flask tapped once against his ribs as he climbed over a collapsed brace.

Comforting.

Annoying.

Both usually meant the same thing.

*

Rook heard the scrape of a boot behind him and moved on reflex, half-turning with one hand already on the wall for balance.

"If this is another memory," Rook said into the dim seam-light, "I would prefer one with less walking."

Eli's answer came from behind the fractured support frame. "Disappointing start, then."

Eli stepped out from behind a fractured support frame, one hand lifted in a vague gesture of peace or apology. With him, both seemed likely.

Rook stared. "Weren't you north?"

Eli gave the chamber a look. "North turned out to be tedious."

Eli came farther into the chamber and looked around at the chalk stubs, broken shelves, and disturbed dust as if confirming something for himself. His gaze landed on the fresh prints near the frame, then on Rook, then stayed there.

"You changed routes," Rook said.

"I improved routes," Eli said. "There's a difference."

Rook did not move from where he stood. "Why?"

Eli shrugged one shoulder. "Because the corridor I was on had already made up its mind, and yours hadn't. Because Kaden said Elijah was trending toward your region. Because you looked like the sort of man who would either find something useful or die in a way that became everyone's paperwork."

That almost got a laugh out of Rook.

"And," Eli added, more quietly, "I wanted to see what the route was doing around you."

There it was. No performance on it. Just fact.

Rook let his hand fall away from the wall. "Found an answer?"

"Not a complete one." Eli glanced at the cut mark Rook had been staring at before the interruption. "But I think you're the real thread."

The words landed harder than Rook expected.

"That is either flattering or terrible," Rook said.

Eli did not soften it. "Probably both."

Eli moved to the far side of the chamber and crouched beside the repaired support frame. He touched one finger to the urgent later work, not the careful older joinery. "This was patched by someone who knew the place would move before he could finish being careful." He looked up. "That feel familiar?"

Rook shut his eyes for half a beat.

Lamplight.
A rough breath.
Someone saying, not unkindly, You can rewrite the braces later if you survive now.

When he opened his eyes again, Eli was still watching.

"Yes," Rook said.

Eli stood. "Then let's stop wasting the one useful thing that's happened since the split."

They left the chamber together.

The route tested them twice before it let them settle into a rhythm. Rook would name a turn from a flash he did not trust, Eli would reject or confirm it by pressure, and the corridor would prove one of them right a breath later. Once, a clean-looking left branch folded inward behind them after Eli chose right. Once, Rook ducked before he knew why and a rotten brace cracked where his head had been.

Neither called it teamwork.

Both began using it that way.

Rook was the one who broke the silence first. "Avery is going to be furious with you."

"Yes," Eli said.

Rook watched him for another step. "And you're still here."

Eli glanced at him. "You say that like I don't know him."

Rook let the corner of his mouth lift once. "Fair."

The flask knocked lightly against Eli's coat when he ducked under a fractured brace. Rook noticed the sound and then, a second later, the movement of Eli's hand checking that it was still there.

Eli noticed him noticing.

"Medicinal," Eli said.

"Against what?" Rook asked.

Eli tapped the flask back into place. "The possibility of hearing one more person tell me to be sensible."

Rook looked ahead again. "Does it work?"

Eli considered that. "Not especially."

Static burst through both their comms at once.

"Rook."

Kaden's voice, thin and failing.

Rook hit the receiver on instinct. "Still here."

"Eli too?" Kaden asked.

Eli blinked once, surprised enough to show it. "Am now."

"Good," Kaden said, though he sounded as if goodness had become expensive. "Sarah found local Avery. Not her husband. Repeat, not her husband."

Eli let out a short breath through his nose. "That sentence should not make sense, but somehow it does."

Rook's attention sharpened. "Is she stable?"

"As a concept? Hard to say," Kaden said. "As a person, yes."

Even through the distortion, Rook could hear the strain under the line. It put a hard edge in him almost at once.

"Kaden," Rook said, quieter, "how much is this costing you now?"

There was a pause.

"We are not doing that in front of company," Kaden said.

Eli's eyes flicked from the corridor to Rook.

Rook did not look back. "That's not an answer."

Kaden did not dignify that as an accusation. "It wasn't meant to be." Another break of static. Then Kaden again, flatter. "Elijah's signal is still moving into your sector. The route around you keeps shearing and then correcting. If either of you starts seeing repeats, trust the oldest version first."

Rook filed the warning away and still did not let the earlier thing go. "You sound worse."

"You've said that before," Kaden said.

"You lied before," Rook said.

Silence.

Kaden's answer came quiet enough that Eli should not have been able to hear it and probably did anyway. "Practice helps."

The channel broke.

For a few steps the only sound was boots on old stone.

Eli kept his eyes ahead. "That sounded private."

"It is," Rook said.

"All right," Eli said.

Rook glanced over. "You're not going to ask?"

"I just did," Eli said.

Rook almost smiled despite himself. "That's a terrible technicality."

"It's an excellent one." Eli stepped over a crack where seam-light pooled too cleanly. "If you want to tell me later, you can. If not, the corridor will invent something ruder than the truth and I'll work with that instead."

Rook looked at him for a moment too long.

There was an ease in Eli that did not come from innocence. It came from surviving a world that punished people for demanding clarity before it existed.

"Something was done to him," Rook said at last.

Eli's face changed, not with surprise but with a more focused kind of attention.

"By your people?" Eli asked.

Rook nodded once.

"Do the others know?" Eli asked.

"Not the whole of it," Rook said.

Eli let that sit without pressing. "And you do."

"Enough to know I should have stopped more than I did," Rook said.

That was apparently the amount of confession the corridor would get from him.

"Project Chimera was the clean name on the door," Rook said. "The useful name. The one you could put on a badge and say in a briefing without anyone asking what had been buried under it."

Eli did not interrupt.

"There were older files," Rook continued. "Older rooms. Other attempts that were not supposed to have happened because nobody alive wanted responsibility for the failures. The people above the project kept changing the labels and preserving the appetite."

The corridor clicked under them.

Eli watched the seam before answering. "That sounds like bandits with better paper."

Rook almost laughed.

It came out badly.

"Yes," Rook said. "And locks. And lawyers. And medical language for things that should have stayed crimes."

Eli looked at him once, then forward again. "Do they know where this place is?"

"Not well enough," Rook said. "That is the only comforting answer I have."

Eli's voice went quiet. "Then we should make sure they do not learn better."

Rook looked at him.

Eli kept walking. "I said should. I did not say I knew how."

"That may be the most honest strategic plan anyone has offered today," Rook said.

"Good," Eli said. "It can be Avery's problem later."

Rook let the corridor have the almost-smile.

Eli accepted it like a field condition. "Then when we get out of here," Eli said, "you can decide whether guilt is a useful hobby. For now I'd prefer you keep doing whatever it is that lets dead routes talk to you."

The bluntness of it hit Rook somewhere unexpectedly human. He looked away before Eli could see too much of that.

They moved on, faster now. At the next crossing node, Rook felt the middle passage pull at him too cleanly. Eli crouched near the left mouth instead and brushed two fingers over fresh disturbance in the dust.

Eli brushed two fingers over the fresh disturbance. "And someone lighter than you took this one recently."

Rook saw the narrow mark, then the smaller prints beside it.

Not a boot print.
Too clean-footed for vermin.

A fragment answered before he could stop it: warm fur brushing a hand, an impatient living shape waiting just long enough to be followed.

Rook looked toward the left corridor.

"What?" Eli asked.

Rook shook his head once, already moving. "I think he isn't alone."

Eli was beside him in a breath. "Now that is a sentence worth following."

*

Sarah had expected local Avery to move like her husband.

She stopped expecting it after the third turn.

Dr. Avery Chen navigated hard spaces the way engineers negotiated failure: by reducing variables, forcing sequence, and carrying purpose visibly in the body. This Avery moved with attention distributed outward, as if he were not solving the corridor so much as staying inside a conversation it had started before she arrived.

It was not mystical. Sarah distrusted that word on principle.

It was practiced.

He paused at a narrow break where the stone pinched close and touched the wall with the backs of his fingers.

"Active?" Sarah asked.

"Thinking about it," Avery said.

"That's a horrible answer," Sarah said.

"It's also the accurate one," Avery said.

He waited another two breaths, then continued. Sarah followed through and felt the delayed shiver in the seam a second after they passed, like a trap regretting missed timing.

"You do this often?" Sarah asked.

"Survive routes?" Avery said. "Often enough."

Sarah checked the tracker again, this time with the names stripped away. It helped less than she had hoped. One signal still threaded dangerously close to the man walking in front of her, and Rook's presence kept feathering at the edges as if the system couldn't hold him in one outline.

Kaden, by contrast, remained one hard point and nothing else.

Too clean.
Too wrong.

"You have someone here who looks like me," Sarah said.

Avery did not turn around. "Probably."

"That was evasive," Sarah said.

"That was cautious," Avery said.

He stepped over a cracked brace and finally glanced back at her. "You have Sister Orin's habit of asking dangerous questions while pretending you've already decided the answer."

Sarah stared.

"I don't know whether that was an insult," Sarah said.

"Neither do I," Avery said.

The comparison landed hard enough that neither of them filled the silence. Sarah looked again at the tablet. Avery watched the corridor instead.

Sarah looked back to the tablet. "The machine thinks your people and mine share identity below the surface. Not resemblance. Something deeper."

"And you think that means counterparts," Avery said.

"I think it means the idea is no longer stupid enough to dismiss," Sarah said.

Avery considered that for several steps.

"Rook's signal behaves wrong?" Avery asked.

Sarah frowned. "How do you know his name?"

Avery kept moving. "Eli used it enough near the portal to become a problem."

"Fine. Yes. Rook doubles. Not constantly. But when the interference clears, he doesn't register like one body." She hesitated. "Kaden does the opposite. One signal. Singular to the point of absurdity."

Avery's expression tightened, though not with recognition. With work.

"The doubled one," Avery said slowly, "feels like a route trying to place a man where someone else already was."

Sarah stared at him. "You can tell that from a sentence?"

"No," Avery said. "From several years of bad places and one sentence."

She almost pushed. Didn't.

"And Kaden?" Sarah asked.

Avery was quiet too long.

"I don't know yet," Avery said at last. "Only that singular isn't the same as simple. From what you're describing, the route doesn't fail to find his counterpart. It fails to decide where all of him is."

That was not an answer either.

But it was honest enough to move with.

The comm crackled suddenly.

"Sarah," Kaden said. "Voice check."

"Here," Sarah said.

"Avery with you?" Kaden asked.

She looked at the man beside her and felt the old jolt of wrongness all over again.

"One of them," Sarah said.

Kaden made a sound that might have been exhausted approval. "Eli crossed onto Rook's route."

Avery stopped. "He did what?"

Sarah repeated it.

Something like relief passed quickly through Avery's face.

"Good," Avery said.

"Good?" Sarah said. "You objected to him going alone at all."

"Yes." Avery resumed walking, faster now. "And if he abandoned north on purpose, then he finally noticed what the route was actually doing."

"Which is?" Sarah asked.

Avery looked into the dark ahead. "Following Rook first."

*

At the portal, Orin caught only pieces of the westward conversation.

Machine.
Counterparts.
Singular.
Not simple.

She did not know the strangers' instruments well enough to judge their names for things. That did not matter. The shape underneath the words was old enough.

Rook doubled because the road remembered another man.

Kaden did not double because something had taught him to be a wound without edges.

Orin did not say that aloud.

The frame tried to narrow.

She drove the Frame Jack down another tooth and held it there until the brace stopped shivering.

"Sister Orin?" Kaden said through the relay.

"Here," Orin said.

"If I drop one of them, I may not find them again," Kaden said.

"If you spend yourself empty, no one finds anyone," Orin said.

His breath caught once in the line.

Then Kaden answered, too controlled. "That is also familiar."

Orin closed her hand harder around the jack.

The people above Project Chimera had not given the Continuum the word they used for themselves.

They had given it enough of their shape.

*

By the time Eli and Rook found the chalk line, neither of them argued with it.

It sat low on the left wall just past a slant of broken stone, a fresh mark dragged by a tired hand that had still cared enough to be deliberate. Next to it, half lost in the dust, ran a set of narrow animal prints and one faint smear where a tail or flank had brushed the mineral drift.

Eli crouched first.

"Not local vermin," Eli said. "Too clean-footed."

Rook felt the fragment before he understood it.

Warmth.
Movement ahead.
An impatient living shape pausing long enough to make sure it was being followed.

"Elijah found something," Rook said.

Eli glanced up. "Or something found him."

There was no fear in the line. Only calculation.

Rook looked deeper into the corridor. The seam-light there felt thinner, stretched over distance that wanted to behave strangely but had not committed yet. A live route. A dangerous one. The kind that punished hesitation more efficiently than bad judgment.

Eli rose and unscrewed his flask.

Rook gave him a look.

"What?" Eli asked.

Rook kept his eyes on the corridor. "We may be close."

"Exactly." Eli took one measured swallow, capped the flask again, and slid it back into place. "I'd like to meet your missing analyst while shaped correctly."

Rook should not have found that reassuring.

He did.

They moved on through the narrowing passage, not bothering anymore with the fiction that one of them was leading and the other following. The route wanted different things from each of them. Eli heard where the seams were becoming impatient. Rook knew which turn would matter before the evidence caught up. Between those failures, something almost efficient emerged.

At the next break, the corridor offered three shadows.

Rook opened his mouth.

Eli cut him off. "Middle if we're being watched. Right if we're being tested. Left if your analyst has found anything even slightly friendly."

Rook blinked. "How did you get all of that that fast?"

Eli's mouth twitched. "Practice helps."

The echo of Kaden's line landed between them for half a second and did not entirely hurt.

Rook looked left. "You were right."

Eli started down the left passage. "About which part?"

Rook followed at once. "That I was the real thread."

Eli did not look back, but Rook could hear the grin in his voice.

"Try not to get sentimental about it," Eli said. "We're only using you as bait for something useful."

*

The corridor bent once, then again, tightening toward a darker throat of stone where the seam-light thinned enough to feel withheld.

Rook heard it first as distance.

Not footsteps. Not another bad echo pretending to be helpful. A single bark from far enough ahead to be uncertain and near enough to matter.

Eli stopped so fast the motion looked like recognition before thought.

"That's him," Eli said.

Rook glanced over. "Elijah?"

Eli shook his head once, already listening harder. "No. The bark."

Another sound came down the corridor. Shorter this time. Impatient in a way no wild thing had any right to sound.

Eli's whole face changed.

Not much. Too little for anyone who did not know him to call it softness. But enough.

"Pebble," Eli said, and there was almost relief in it now. "I know that bark."

The Wayhound came around the bend in a low pale rush a second later, seam-marked fur catching the weak corridor light. He did not hesitate on seeing them. He ran straight to Eli and stopped hard in front of him as if arriving at the obvious answer to a problem everybody else had been slow enough to complicate.

"There you are," Eli said, dropping one hand at once to the rough fur of his neck. "You found him. Good."

Pebble huffed once, impatient and satisfied both, shoved his narrow head briefly into Eli's hand, then turned back down the corridor. He did not go far. He moved three paces, stopped, looked back.

Come on.

Rook saw Elijah before Eli did.

Not because the corridor gave him a cleaner line of sight. Because there was something wrong now about the way familiarity arrived. A shape in the dim. One shoulder lower than the other from fatigue. One hand on the wall. Pebble pausing just ahead of him like a guide making sure the person being rescued had not mistaken the arrangement for dignity.

"There," Rook said quietly.

Elijah came around the bend with the look of someone who had not been alone long enough to enjoy it and had still managed to be injured by the attempt. Dust streaked one sleeve. Blood had dried brown along the cuff where he had scraped his hand somewhere earlier and failed to care about it. He opened his mouth, likely to say Rook's name first.

Then Eli stepped half a pace forward, and the moment changed.

Elijah stopped.

Eli did too.

The resemblance was not exact. It was worse than exact. Exact could have been filed under anomaly and argued with later. This was the same answer written by two different hands. Face, build, the set of the mouth when neither was speaking, the same tired intelligence looking out through damage that had not happened in the same order.

Pebble gave an annoyed little bark, as if human beings were once again failing basic route discipline.

Elijah looked down at him, then back up at Eli. "How do you know I named him that?"

Eli frowned. Not defensive. Simply thrown by the premise. "I named him that."

Elijah stared.

Rook watched the sentence land and keep falling.

"What do you mean," Elijah said, more fascinated than alarmed now, "you named him?"

Eli's hand stayed on Pebble's neck, fingers sunk lightly into the rough fur there. "I mean I named him when I first met him." He glanced at Pebble as if expecting support and getting only old patience in return. "He kept it."

That should have gone somewhere loud.

Instead Elijah took one slow breath, then another, and Rook saw confusion begin to sharpen into effort rather than panic. Not disbelief. Measurement. He looked at Eli as if the right angle might make the explanation present itself.

It did not.

So Rook said the thing the corridor had already decided all of them were going to have to admit.

"You two look enough alike to ruin everyone's day."

Elijah let out a short disbelieving breath that almost counted as a laugh.

Eli looked him over with the bluntness of a man checking a wall for structural failure. "You look worse."

"Good," Elijah said. "I worked at it."

That was close enough to normal human speech that Rook felt his own shoulders loosen by a degree.

Not safety.

Only contact.

Pebble circled back and settled against Eli's leg with proprietary confidence, as if there had never been any question where he belonged once the route had finished delivering what he had gone to fetch.

Elijah noticed.

His eyes narrowed just slightly, not with resentment but with recalculation. "He knows you."

"Yes," Eli said.

Elijah absorbed that in silence. Then: "Right."

He took one step nearer.

Pain hit him immediately.

It crossed his face fast enough that another person might have missed it. Rook did not. Elijah's hand came up hard to his temple. At the same instant Eli flinched, two fingers pressing briefly above one brow as if some narrow bright spike had passed through him and gone on without asking permission.

The corridor was still there.

Pebble was still leaning against Eli's leg.

And over that, for less than a heartbeat, Elijah was somewhere else.

Stone corridor.
A seam in the air.
A hand clamped hard to another sleeve.
Eli's voice, low and clear in a way Elijah had not yet heard from the man standing in front of him now:

Avery. That's a thin place.

Then it was gone.

Elijah blinked once, sharply, and the corridor came back around him in full. Eli was still standing there, jaw tight now, eyes narrowed with the private irritation of pain he had not invited.

Rook moved before either of them decided to pretend nothing had happened.

"Give each other a little room," Rook said, casual enough to pass for field sense if no one looked at him too closely. "You both look worse when you're standing on top of it."

Elijah stared at him.

"On top of what?" Elijah asked.

Rook gave him a thin look. "My patience, mainly."

That bought him just enough compliance to be useful. Elijah stepped back half a pace. Eli did the same without needing the instruction repeated. The pressure in the corridor eased by fractions at once.

Rook filed that away.

Elijah lowered his hand from his temple. "I'm fine."

The lie was not especially convincing.

Eli's answering look suggested he knew that as well as anyone, but he let it pass. Maybe because his own head was still hurting. Maybe because people with certain kinds of damage recognized the hierarchy of available dignities and tried not to interfere with the smaller ones.

Rook touched his comm. "Kaden."

Static answered first.

Then Kaden, thinner than before: "Still here."

Elijah's expression shifted at the sound of a known human voice not attached to the people currently damaging his understanding of reality. "Where are the others?"

"Moving," Kaden said. "Sarah's with local Avery. Sister Orin's still holding at the portal. The rest are moving west."

"Four," Eli said.

There was a brief crackle of silence. "Right," Kaden said. "Pebble counts."

That got another almost-laugh out of Elijah. Less from humor than relief. Enough.

Rook looked down the corridor beyond them. "How far?"

"To everyone being a single problem?" Kaden asked. "Not far enough."

Elijah frowned. "Sister Orin stayed with the portal?"

"That was the plan," Kaden said. The static around his voice roughened, then thinned again. "If the gate fails while we're all congratulating ourselves elsewhere, it becomes a much shorter book."

Elijah pushed one shoulder off the wall and forced himself straighter. "Pebble led me through two bad splits and one chamber that kept trying to put the same turn in front of me twice. Portal's west of here. Or where west has been pretending to be useful."

Eli's attention sharpened. "You can read route drift now?"

"Only when it makes my head feel like someone is filing the inside of it," Elijah said. He looked at Eli once more, quick and involuntary. "That got worse when you got close."

Rook chose not to dignify that with analysis.

"Fine," Rook said. "You stay with Pebble. Eli, take seam-side. We move before the corridor decides reunion scenes are a luxury item."

They did.

*

Orin heard the word gate and almost corrected it.

She did not. Kaden needed fewer corrections, not more.

The frame was not a gate. Not properly. It was a frame and a conduit without the thing that told the opening when to end. Malrec had known enough to build the shape and not enough to stop the shape from wanting a body to complete it.

The people above Project Chimera had made the same mistake in cleaner rooms. Orin could hear that now in how Kaden spoke around pain. They had found a person useful near the places between worlds and decided usefulness was permission.

Different language.
Same harm.

She turned the Frame Jack again.

"Hold," Orin told the stone.

The stone did, for now.

*

The westward route got meaner by design.

Pebble kept them off the worst options, darting ahead and returning with the impatient certainty of a creature who had no patience for human doubt. Eli trusted him without ceremony. Elijah kept glancing at the Wayhound as if trying to reconcile the animal he had named with the animal who already belonged to someone else's history.

Rook stayed between Eli and Elijah whenever the corridor allowed it. When proximity made both counterparts flinch, he shifted them apart and said, "Walk staggered. Whatever this place is doing, it likes proximity too much."

Neither argued.

Kaden's voice came and went in thinning threads.

"Portal line still holds," Kaden said once.

Later: "Sarah's close. Avery too."

Rook did not ask which Avery. The route answered by dragging both matching signatures closer.

Later still, after a bad shear forced them through a sloping cut where Pebble found purchase faster than any human boot: "Try not to make me track you one by one."

Rook recognized the strain because he had heard it before in cleaner rooms, under cleaner words. People above Project Chimera had called it tolerance and exposure and useful adaptation. They had made harm sound like equipment behaving within expected limits.

Rook touched the comm during one of the longer lulls. "Kaden."

Static.

Kaden came back low enough to suggest the others were no longer meant to hear. "You pick your timing badly."

Rook dropped his voice. "You sound terrible."

Kaden sounded almost amused, which made it worse. "I've sounded terrible for years. You're only promoting it because we're in a ruin."

Rook let the others gain several paces ahead. Eli noticed and did not slow.

"How much is it costing you?" Rook asked.

Silence stretched. Decision, not absence.

"Holding the connection open feels," Kaden said, then stopped. "It feels too much like the tests."

Rook's hand tightened around the receiver.

"Every time I force the line stable it feels like they're doing it again. Like they found a way to turn my nervous system into equipment and never finished putting it back." Kaden's laugh had no humor in it. "I can feel where they taught me to stay open."

Rook shut his eyes once.

White rooms. Clean language. Kaden half-upright in the chair, past screaming by then, which had been worse. Somebody saying procedure as if the word could make cruelty clinical.

Kaden did not let him look away from it. "You still think if you'd fought harder, you'd have gotten me out before any of it stayed. I know."

Rook drew one shallow breath. "I don't."

Rook had no answer worth giving.

Kaden's voice softened around the damage. "You don't owe me a better version of what I already know."

The line went still.

"I should have ended it before they got that far," Rook said.

Kaden made a tired sound on the other end that might, in gentler circumstances, have been affection.

"You tried to slow it," Kaden said.

Rook did not soften that either. "I failed."

"Yes," Kaden said.

There was no cruelty in it. Only fact.

Rook opened his eyes. The corridor was still there, waiting to be crossed rather than admired.

"Stay alive long enough for me to be useful about this later," Rook said.

"Sentimental for you," Kaden said.

Rook let the corridor have the almost-smile. "Ruinous, honestly."

When the line broke, it did not feel terminal.

Only expensive.

Rook caught up just as Pebble stopped so abruptly that Eli nearly stepped into him.

The corridor ahead widened around a ruined post. Stone shelf on the left. Broken anchor irons on the right. Deep scratch marks across the wall at hip height, too long to be tool work and too deliberate to be collapse.

Eli went still.

"That's not route damage," Eli said.

Pebble lowered himself, fur lifting along the spine, mouth closed.

Rook looked at the torn anchor irons. "Name?"

Eli's expression went thin. "Braceback."

Rook looked back toward the chamber. "That's a terrible name."

Eli did not blink. "It's a terrible animal."

Elijah looked from one to the other. "How terrible?"

Then the answer stood up.

It had been half-hidden against the far wall, broad enough and still enough to pass briefly for collapsed ruin. When it moved, the chamber seemed to recoil from its certainty. Stone plating ran from skull to spine in overlapping slabs. Coarse dark hair rose between the plates. Pale seam-lines glimmered when it breathed.

The Braceback lowered its head.

Pebble gave a low sound that was not fear and not challenge.

Warning.

Eli did not take his eyes off the creature. "Territorial. Fast in bursts. Don't let it pin the corridor."

Rook looked once to the side routes, once to the broken post, and understood the shape of the problem almost at the same instant Eli did.

Rook looked toward the side route. "It wants us into the right cut."

"Yes," Eli said.

"Why?" Rook asked.

Eli's mouth twitched without humor. "Because it lives here and isn't eager for discussion."

The Braceback charged.

"Left!" Eli snapped.

They moved as one body and still barely made it.

Pebble cut low across the creature's front, turning its attention by inches. Rook shoved Elijah toward the left passage. Eli read the support geometry before anyone else had time to understand it.

"Rook," Eli said, already angling for the wall. "If it commits here, the shelf goes under its left side. Keep it high. Pebble, off."

For three breaths, the plan worked.

The shelf dropped. One forelimb punched through. Stone gave. The Braceback snarled, a sound like rockfall taught to hate.

"Go!" Eli snapped.

Then the damaged brace tore farther than Eli had asked it to.

The dropped shelf collapsed entirely under the Braceback's weight, and the beast came through in a spray of stone and rage, bloodied on one side and faster for it.

Eli hit the side wall hard. Something metal knocked free from his coat, rang once against stone, and vanished under sliding debris. No one had time to look.

Elijah turned at the wrong time.

Not from stupidity.

From the human error of checking whether everyone else had made the gap.

The Braceback hit the last stretch in a single violent burst and caught him across the leg.

Rook saw claw first.
Then cloth.
Then blood.

Elijah went down hard. Blood sheeted down his trouser leg at once.

Too fast.

Eli reached him first, stopped a pace short as the feedback problem struck both counterparts, then rerouted into usefulness with brutal speed.

"Left wall!" Eli shouted. "Rook, up. Pebble, keep it off the mouth."

"Can you stand?" Rook asked.

Elijah laughed once, wild and thin. "That seems optimistic."

Blood was running through Rook's fingers now, hot and immediate.

Too much blood. Too little time.

Rook hauled him up across his shoulders, adjusting when the leg made any gentler carry impossible. Elijah made a raw sound Rook was going to remember later in worse hours than this one.

"Sorry," Rook said.

Elijah's answer came through his teeth. "You are deeply forgiven if we live."

Eli backed into the lead position at once. "This way. Don't crowd me."

The Braceback came again.

Rook ran.
