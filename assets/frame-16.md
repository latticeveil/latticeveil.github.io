# The Frame Looks Back

Orin started with the floor.

Not the frame.

Not the wrapped core in Avery's pack.

The floor.

She crouched beside the first chalk line and followed it with two fingers held a breath above the stone. Dust had gathered in the shallow cut. The line ran from the half-built doorway toward the eastern wall, broke around a rusted pin, and continued beneath a workbench whose legs had settled unevenly over the years.

"No one touches the frame," Orin said.

Rook looked toward the frame. "You have delivered that instruction often enough that I am beginning to suspect it includes me."

Orin did not look up. "It began with you."

Rook nodded. "Good. I dislike uncertainty in matters of reputation."

Avery lowered his pack beside the wall, far enough from the doorway that the core stopped making the nearby chalk lose color. He kept one hand on the flap anyway.

Eli leaned against the workbench and took a small drink from his flask. His one-handed staff rested loosely in his other hand. The pale inlay along the grip had not stopped twitching since they entered the hidden chamber. It clicked against the floor once, corrected itself, and settled.

Pebble sat against Eli's boot.

The animal watched the doorway with his ears low.

Orin glanced at him, then at Eli's staff. "If that brightens, you move away from the frame."

Eli capped the flask. "It has been trying to leave without me since we came in."

Orin returned to the chalk line. "Then do not encourage it."

Eli slipped the flask back into his coat. "I rarely encourage anything before breakfast."

Avery looked over. "You drank through breakfast."

Eli patted the flask through the fabric. "Then I am ahead of schedule."

Orin ignored them with the patience of someone who had decided the room was more dangerous than the conversation.

They searched outward from the doorway.

The workshop had been built in layers. The central chamber held the frame, the diagram, and the worst of Malrec's failed measurements. Narrow storage cuts opened behind the workbench and along the north wall. Their doors had rotted crooked in their hinges. One stood open by a handspan. Another had swollen shut against damp stone.

Avery worked the open one wider with the flat of his axe.

Inside, old tools lay where someone had left them: brace hammers with split handles, pin tongs stained dark around the jaws, coils of cord hardened by age, jars of mineral dust gone gray with damp. Most were useless. Some were only waiting for a patient hand.

Orin picked through them carefully.

She stopped when she found the cane.

It had fallen behind a narrow stool and rested beneath a collapsed strip of shelving. Avery almost missed the change in her posture because nothing about the object demanded attention. It was plain wood from end to end. No pale inlay. No salt grooves. No weighted cap. Its length was practical, its lower end worn flat on one side, its grip darkened by years of pressure from the same hand.

Orin lifted it free.

Her thumb settled into the worn place at the handle without searching.

"Malrec's?" Avery asked.

Orin nodded.

Rook leaned against the doorframe. "Not decorative."

Orin ran her thumb over the worn grip. "His left leg never healed correctly. Cold routes made it worse."

She tested the cane once against the floor. The wood held.

For a moment Orin stood with it the way another person had stood in this room for years, weight angled through one arm, body making an old correction without thought.

Then she laid the cane beside the salvage worth keeping.

Carefully.

Eli watched her do it and said nothing.

The next storage cut held paper, cloth, and old route slates stacked in shallow shelves. Damp had taken most of them. Avery lifted each one from below while Orin checked the surviving marks.

Rook found the margin note folded into the back of a narrow ledger.

"This one was hidden badly enough to be intentional," Rook said.

Orin held out her hand.

Rook gave it to her without a joke.

The writing was smaller than the note they had found beneath the diagram. The ink had browned. One edge had been thumbed soft by someone returning to the same words more often than the cloth deserved.

Orin read in silence.

Avery watched her jaw tighten.

"What is it?" Avery asked.

Orin looked at the old writing for another breath before she answered.

"Something he did not send."

Her thumb moved down the cloth.

Then Orin read aloud.

"I waited too long after Neris. I called regret a wound because wounds ask less of a man than repair. Orindessa left before I learned the difference."

The room seemed to settle inward.

Eli's hand stopped on the cap of his flask.

Orin continued.

"Years later, a route worker told me a town had given her a name with trust inside it. Sister Orin. I was glad the road found a better word for her than the ones we left behind."

Orin lowered the cloth.

Rook looked away first.

"He heard," Avery said quietly.

"Yes," Orin answered.

The word came out level.

It did not sound light.

Orin folded the note along its oldest crease and placed it with the first one. "Keep searching."

Avery did.

The west storage door resisted the axe twice before the swollen wood tore free around the latch.

Something scraped inside.

Avery froze.

The sound came again.

Small.

Dry.

Busy.

Rook shifted the longstaff into both hands. "That sounds industrious."

"Back," Orin said.

The first creature came through the gap beneath the broken door.

It was roughly the size of a market basket, low and jointed, with six short legs working in quick uneven pairs. Its back was covered by overlapping mineral plates the color of old brace stone. Pale dust clung between them. The head was little more than a wedge beneath the front shell, split by two dark teeth that worried at the torn wood as if the door remained an insult requiring correction.

The creature stopped.

Its legs clicked against the floor.

It turned toward the rusted pin nearest the frame.

Orin swore softly.

"Brace Mite," she said.

Rook glanced at her. "Singular?"

The storage cut answered him.

Three more shapes scraped against one another inside the dark.

Then six.

Orin drew her bow. "Temporarily."

The first Brace Mite darted forward.

Avery brought the flat of his axe down across its shell.

The impact cracked through the workshop. The creature folded against the stone, legs kicking twice before going still. A shard of shell skipped beneath the workbench.

The others came through the broken door all at once.

They did not move like hunters.

They moved like hunger with a specific trade.

Two rushed the nearest floor pins. One climbed the leg of the workbench and began biting through a cord spool before Avery could turn. Another ignored all of them and ran straight toward the unfinished doorway.

"Keep them off the frame," Orin shouted.

Rook was already moving.

He planted the longstaff against the floor and vaulted over the first cluster. The nimble working caught his weight and returned it at an angle no ordinary pole should have allowed. He landed on the edge of the workbench, kicked the cord-biting mite into open floor, and struck the stone beside another hard enough to turn it away from the doorway.

"Avery," Rook called. "The rude one."

Avery shifted his stance. "They are all rude."

Rook pointed with the end of his staff. "The ambitious one."

Avery stepped between the mite and the frame. His axe came down edge-first this time. Mineral shell split. Pale powder burst across his boots.

Orin cast her weighted cord across the west doorway. One square weight struck the stone beyond it and caught in a seam. She snapped the line low. Two Brace Mites hit the cord and tumbled over one another. Orin pinned the first through a shell gap with an arrow before it could right itself.

The second scrambled sideways.

Pebble barked once.

Eli lifted his staff.

The pale lines along its grip brightened.

He did not strike the mite.

He pressed the staff forward by a finger's width and made the route in front of the creature feel farther away than it was.

The Brace Mite ran.

Its six legs clicked furiously against the same square of floor.

Eli exhaled through his teeth. "That is unpleasant."

"Small work only," Orin warned.

Eli kept the staff extended. "It is small."

The west storage cut cracked.

Something inside gave way with a damp wooden snap.

The dark behind the door moved.

Not one creature.

A nest.

Brace Mites poured through the broken opening in a scrape of shell and stone teeth, climbing over the cord, under it, and across one another. They spilled around the workbench legs and scattered toward every worked piece of the chamber: pins, cords, brace slots, mineral seams, the half-finished doorway.

Rook stared at them for half a breath.

"I withdraw my admiration for industry."

He jumped from the bench.

The staff struck floor, caught, and threw him sideways across the room. Rook dragged its weighted end along the stone as he landed, making the pale inlay flare. Three mites turned toward the moving pressure and followed him away from the frame.

"That is right," Rook said, backing toward the southern wall. "I am expensive and poorly stored."

Avery crushed another near the conduit depression.

The shell resisted the first blow and split under the second. He shifted his grip, using the axe less like a weapon than a heavy tool. Every strike had to land where the creature would be before it reached the hardware. Every miss cost stone, cord, or time.

One mite climbed the frame.

Orin fired.

The arrow punched through the narrow gap below its upper shell and pinned it against the left upright. Its legs scraped wildly against stone. Orin crossed the chamber, seized the shaft, and tore the creature free before it could chew through the pale cord behind it.

"Eli," Orin called.

Eli turned toward the frame. "I see it."

Another mite had reached the base of the frame.

Its teeth closed around an old tension line.

Eli drew the staff across his body with one hand. The air near the floor folded wrong. Not visibly. Avery only felt the change when his next step landed too early.

The mite jerked sideways as if it had been shoved by a route deciding it preferred another answer. Its teeth slipped from the cord.

Pebble lunged in, caught the creature by the edge of its shell, and dragged it backward with a growl far larger than his body.

The Brace Mite twisted.

Avery struck once.

The shell broke.

Pebble released it and sneezed pale dust across Avery's boot.

"Good work," Avery said.

Pebble moved immediately back to Eli.

Not Avery.

Eli braced the staff against the floor and drank again. His hand shook once against the flask. He swallowed, capped it, and kept the metal clenched in his free hand.

"How many?" Avery asked.

Rook ran along the southern wall, planted one boot against a brace slot, and vaulted back over the cluster following him.

"Fewer than before," Rook answered.

Avery struck another shell. "Useful number."

Rook landed beside the southern wall. "I am busy improving it."

The cord across the west doorway jerked.

Orin saw the larger shape behind it before Avery did.

Not larger by much.

Only wider.

An old Brace Mite forced itself through the broken storage door with shell plates thick enough to grind against both sides. Rusted fragments clung around its mouth. One of Malrec's pins had been bitten halfway through and remained caught between its teeth.

It did not rush the frame.

It turned toward the floor beneath it.

Toward a low iron assembly almost buried under the collapsed shelf.

The creature bit down.

Metal screamed.

Orin's expression changed.

"That," she said.

Avery understood.

He ran.

The old mite tore backward with the iron assembly caught in its mouth. A hand crank unfolded from beneath the shelf. Two hinged brace arms dragged after it, still tangled in hardened cord.

Rook landed beside the creature and struck its shell with the side of his staff.

The mite turned.

Its head snapped toward him.

Rook moved a fraction too slowly.

One dark tooth scored along the staff and threw him against the workbench hard enough to rattle every jar on it.

"Rook!" Eli shouted.

"Working on it," Rook answered from the floor.

Avery drove his axe into the shell seam behind the creature's head.

The first strike lodged.

The mite dragged him half a pace.

Avery planted his boots and twisted the haft.

The shell opened by a finger's width.

Orin's arrow entered that width and buried itself deep.

The Brace Mite collapsed onto the iron assembly.

For a few breaths, the only movement in the workshop came from the last smaller mites trying to reach the cords. Avery finished one. Orin pinned another. Eli sent the final creature running in place long enough for Rook to rise, limp across the floor, and bring the end of his staff down through its shell.

Silence returned badly.

Stone dust drifted through the seam-light.

Rook leaned on the longstaff and looked at the dead mite beneath it.

"Qualified success," he said.

Avery pulled his axe free. "You were thrown into a table."

"The table was defeated."

Eli drank again.

Orin crouched beside the iron assembly the old mite had tried to carry away.

She lifted one hinged arm, cleared old cord from the joint, and turned the hand crank by a quarter rotation. The two brace arms drew inward with a low mechanical complaint. A narrow pin cradle tightened between them.

Recognition moved across her face slowly.

"Frame jack," Orin said.

Avery knelt beside her. "For the doorway?"

"For worked frames generally. Seat a pin. Restore tension. Hold stone in argument with itself while you repair what failed."

Rook looked at the dead mites. "They ate the argument."

Orin tested the crank again. "Part of it."

Orin checked the frame jack's teeth. One had bent. Two were still sound.

"Can it repair the door?" Eli asked.

Orin looked toward the unfinished frame. "It can repair the part of the door made by hands."

That was not the same as yes.

Avery began clearing shell fragments from the floor. Most had cracked into chalky layers under the axe. A few remained intact along the edges, thin curved plates with a mineral hardness that had survived years in the hidden room.

Orin gathered three and wrapped them in scrap cloth.

"Useful?" Avery asked.

"For small brace repairs," Orin answered. "Clean them first. Mites spend their lives chewing places we would rather keep honest."

Rook nudged a broken shell with his boot. "A touching contribution to the route economy."

They dragged the dead mites into the west storage cut and blocked the doorway with the torn shelf. Orin salted the nest opening lightly, not enough for a burial rite, only enough to discourage anything deeper inside from deciding the workshop remained worth eating.

Then they returned to the frame.

The swarm had exposed damage Avery had not understood before.

Behind the left upright, one old tension line had been gnawed nearly through. Beneath the frame, a row of pin sockets leaned unevenly around the central depression. The frame had shape, as Malrec's note promised.

It did not have discipline.

Orin fitted the frame jack across the lower stone brace. She set one arm against the left upright and the other into a pin socket beneath the conduit depression. When she turned the crank, the doorway answered with a long scrape from deep inside the wall.

Eli stepped back.

His staff brightened.

Pebble pressed harder against his boot.

"Away from it," Orin said.

Eli moved toward the workbench without argument.

Avery watched the frame settle by increments as Orin worked. The jack drew the lower pins into alignment and restored tension to the surviving cords. It was not graceful. It looked like a person holding a cracked rib together long enough to keep breathing.

Rook found the second note inside the frame jack's storage case.

He read the first line and lost the little humor left in his face.

"Orin."

She kept one hand on the crank. "Read it."

Rook unfolded the cloth.

"The anchor is not the limiter," he read. "It may wake the conduit. It may give false distance enough weight to answer. It cannot tell the answer where to remain."

The jack creaked under Orin's hand.

Rook continued.

"I mistook force for instruction for too many years. A core may open the road and still leave the road without a wall. If the frame answers before a limiter is found, do not mistake an opening for a route. Do not mistake a route for a promise."

Rook lowered the note.

Eli looked at the central depression. "He knew it could open wrong."

"He knew enough to fear it," Orin answered.

Avery rested one hand against his pack. "And he never found the limiter."

Orin kept her eyes on the frame. "No."

Avery nodded toward the jack. "Can the jack hold it?"

Orin looked at Avery as if deciding how honest the answer needed to be.

"The jack can hold stone," she said. "It cannot teach the Echo where to stop."

Rook folded Malrec's note. "Strong objection to experimentation."

"Yes," Orin answered.

No one moved toward the frame.

The hidden workshop had become quieter after the fight. Not empty. Listening. Chalk dust lay pale across the floor. Dead Brace Mites scraped faintly against one another behind the blocked storage door whenever the draft changed.

Eli lifted his flask.

He drank, lowered it, and watched the doorway over the metal rim.

"Something is already near it," Eli said.

Avery looked at him. "The frame?"

Eli looked toward the doorway. "The other side."

Eli rubbed two fingers against the grip of his staff. The pale inlay answered with a faint pulse.

"It feels closer than it did before the mites," Eli continued. "Not open. Waiting."

Orin's attention stayed on him. "Can you tell what waits?"

Eli shook his head. "No."

Eli considered the answer.

Eli tightened his grip on the staff. "Only that it feels like a place with its hand against the same wall."

Rook looked toward the frame. "That is almost comforting."

Eli glanced at Rook. "It should not be."

Rook looked back at the frame. "Then it is behaving consistently."

Avery took the wrapped core from his pack.

The cloth tightened around its hard irregular shape. The chalk nearest the frame paled again as he stepped forward.

Orin watched the core.

Then the frame.

Then the three people waiting for her to say no.

"One attempt," Orin said. "No one enters. No one touches the opening. We learn whether Malrec's work can wake, and then we put it back to sleep."

Rook lifted one hand. "I appreciate rules established before the disaster."

"You will appreciate following them more."

Orin braced both hands around the frame jack's crank.

Avery knelt before the central depression.

The Anchor Maw Core fit too well.

He felt that before he seated it. The shape in his hands seemed to find the socket while he was still lowering it, one broken route-anchor answering another. The wrapped cloth slipped free. Dark mineral facets caught the seam-light. Pale lines deep inside the core brightened and arranged themselves around a hollow center.

"Ready?" Avery asked.

"No," Orin answered.

She drew one slow breath.

Orin tightened her hands around the crank. "Seat it."

Avery pressed the core into the conduit.

The workshop kicked.

Not like the Anchor Maw's foot striking ground.

Like distance had struck the other side of the wall.

The frame jack snapped tight beneath Orin's hands. Every surviving cord around the doorway drew taut at once. Rusted pins seated themselves deeper into stone with a series of hard metallic knocks.

The core brightened.

The empty space inside the doorway lost depth.

Avery stumbled backward.

For one breath, the frame held nothing except a flat gray surface that reflected no one in the room.

Then the gray surface opened.

There was a chamber beyond it.

Not this chamber.

It carried the same broad answer badly translated. A flat floor where Malrec's stone had settled unevenly. Upright surfaces too clean to be walls made by any mason Avery knew. Hard white light without lantern, seam, or flame. Tall narrow shapes with dark faces. Tables built from smooth material without grain or hammer mark. Lines that went where cords might have gone, but thinner, colored, and bundled with impossible neatness.

The place looked like the workshop after someone had removed every forgiving thing from it.

Rook stepped closer.

"No," Orin said.

He stopped.

Across the opening, movement answered.

A person stood in the clean wrong room.

Only one.

The figure was difficult to hold in sight. The doorway shivered around the edges whenever Avery tried to understand him as a whole. But when Rook shifted his weight, the person beyond shifted too. When Rook lifted his head, the other man did the same.

Not reflection.

Too much around him was wrong.

His clothing sat differently. Smooth where Rook's coat was patched. The light fell cleanly across a face held in angles the Continuum had never taught Avery to expect. Strange equipment rested near one hand.

But the movement matched.

Rook stared at him.

The figure stared back.

"That is not me," Rook said.

His voice had gone quiet.

Eli made a sharp sound behind Avery.

Not pain.

Recognition arriving where it had no right to be.

Avery turned.

Eli stood near the workbench with the flask clenched in one hand and the one-handed staff lifted in the other. Every pale line along the staff had ignited. Light crowded between his fingers and ran upward through the wood in quick branching paths.

"Eli," Avery said.

Eli did not look at him.

His eyes stayed on the doorway.

Not on Rook's wrong reflection.

Somewhere past it.

"There is someone else," Eli whispered.

Orin released one hand from the jack. "Do not reach."

"He feels-"

Eli stopped.

His face tightened as if the unfinished sentence had entered his skull and found resistance.

"Like what?" Avery asked.

Eli's grip closed harder around the staff.

"Like me."

The staff screamed.

The sound did not come from wood.

It came from the shape around it.

Pale light burst outward in a hard ring. Avery threw one arm across his face. The frame jack jumped against the floor. Loose chalk lifted in a white cloud. Eli left his feet and struck the workbench hard enough to send the remaining jars crashing sideways.

His staff broke in the middle.

Not snapped.

Opened.

For one impossible breath, the hollow inside the wood contained too much light and no wood at all.

Then both halves hit the floor.

Dark.

Eli lay on his side beside them, breathing hard.

The flask had rolled from his hand but stopped against the leg of the workbench within reach.

Pebble lunged toward him.

Avery crossed the room and dropped beside Eli. "Look at me."

Eli blinked.

His eyes found Avery slowly.

"I saw him," Eli said.

Avery kept one hand near Eli's shoulder. "Who?"

Eli swallowed. His hand found the flask and closed around it.

"My reflection."

Avery looked toward the open doorway.

Only Rook's answering figure remained visible in the hard white room.

"Where?" Avery asked.

Eli pressed the heel of one hand against his forehead. "Inside."

Orin cranked the frame jack another quarter turn.

The portal surface shuddered.

"It is failing," Orin said.

Rook did not turn from the figure beyond the threshold.

Pebble did.

The animal left Eli's side so quickly Avery nearly reached after him. Pebble crossed the chamber in a gray rush, caught the edge of Rook's coat in his teeth, and pulled.

Rook looked down.

Pebble pulled again.

Harder.

"That is new," Rook said.

The animal planted all four feet and growled through the cloth.

Orin watched him. "Step away from it."

Rook looked at the doorway.

The figure in the clean room looked back.

The frame shivered again. One surviving cord snapped and whipped against stone. The open surface buckled inward, recovered, and narrowed by a finger's width around the edges.

"If it closes, we learn nothing," Rook said.

"We learned it opens without knowing where to hold," Orin answered. "That is enough."

Rook looked back at the unstable opening. "For Malrec."

Orin's face hardened. "Do not make his mistake sound like caution failed him."

Rook flinched.

Not visibly enough for anyone except Orin to notice.

His hand moved down to Pebble's head.

The animal did not release his coat.

"I am the only one trained to move with this staff," Rook said. "The working keeps my footing where a bad route tries to take it. If someone is going to test whether that is a floor on the other side, it should be me."

Avery rose from beside Eli. "Or no one."

Rook pointed toward the narrowing surface. "The opening is collapsing."

Avery stepped between Rook and the frame. "That is an argument for no one."

Rook glanced toward Avery. "You say that because you are sensible."

"I say it because the door has already thrown Eli across the room."

From the floor, Eli lifted his head. "I would prefer not to be treated as a measurement."

Rook nodded. "Fair. You are a warning."

Eli let his head rest against the workbench. "Also fair."

The doorway contracted again.

Hard white light trembled across Rook's face.

On the other side, his counterpart stood at the same distance.

Waiting.

Orin's hand remained locked around the frame jack.

"Rook," she said.

He looked at her.

"We do not know if you will live."

Orin looked from Rook to the narrowing doorway. "And if it cannot find the limiter Malrec never gave it, it may try to make one from the first living thing that crosses. You may not pass through it. You may become the piece holding it open."

Pebble made a low desperate sound and pulled again.

Rook crouched enough to loosen the fabric from the animal's teeth. He rested one hand briefly against the side of Pebble's neck.

Rook eased the cloth from Pebble's teeth. "I know."

He rose.

The longstaff settled into his hand with the easy correction of old training.

Rook tested its weight once and looked toward the doorway.

"It has been a while since the staff qualified me for a truly terrible idea," he said. "I was concerned the training had gone to waste."

No one laughed.

Rook looked back at Orin.

Some of the humor remained in his face.

Not enough to hide behind.

"If this is where it ends," Rook said, "it has been a good life working under Sister Orin."

Orin's grip tightened on the crank.

Orin said his name once more. "Rook."

He stepped toward the doorway.

Pebble barked once.

The figure in the clean wrong room stepped forward at the same time.

For a breath, the two movements met perfectly.

Rook planted the longstaff against stone, trusted the working beneath his hand, and entered the threshold.

The frame looked back.

Then it took him.
