# FRAME 11
# ANCHORFALL

Avery's answer had barely stopped moving through the room before the route-hold let them go reluctantly.

Avery felt the release in the soles of his boots first. The floor lines dimmed under old chalk and fresh thread. The threshold marks stopped leaning toward his attention. Wind moved through the outer slits again and became only wind, cold and mineral and large enough to make the room feel smaller than it had a moment before.

Not safe.

Never safe.

Only no longer the center of the argument.

Sister Orin stood at the route board with one hand near the strip that had pointed toward Anchorfall. She did not touch it. The strip stayed face-down, held by a single black thread that had not been there when they entered the hold and that Avery had not seen her tie.

He decided not to ask.

He was learning.

Eli sat on a low brace crate near the wind slit, flask in one hand, thumb moving over the dented metal without opening it. He watched the cliff beyond the slit as if distance could be read with enough stubbornness.

"Drink or put it away," Avery said.

Eli's eyes shifted toward him. "That is an intimate command from a man who spent the morning refusing a wall."

Avery kept his attention on the flask. "I refused a mark."

Eli's mouth twitched. "You refused it aloud."

"It was persuasive."

Eli looked down at the flask. Avery waited for the familiar deflection. Eli uncapped it instead, took one measured swallow, and put it back at his hip.

Maintenance, then.

Not panic.

Avery accepted that without comment.

Orin took the pale cord from the table and wrapped it once around her wrist. The square weights knocked softly together, each one marked on four faces, the fifth scraped smooth.

"Anchorfall is not a ruin," she said.

That was not the warning Avery had expected.

Eli stood. "That sounds worse than if it were."

"It is," Orin said.

She crossed to the doorway and stopped before the threshold. Old places had rules. Orin gave this one time to recognize hers.

"There are working shelves below the cliff road," she said. "Lift frames, rope sheds, field kitchens, small stores, medic niches, anchor crews. People who live too close to broken routes because everyone else prefers the benefits at a distance."

"Route labor," Avery said.

"Route lives," Orin corrected.

The correction landed cleanly enough that Avery did not argue with it.

At the board, the hidden strip clicked once, the black thread trembling toward Anchorfall.

Not loudly.

Enough.

Orin's mouth thinned. "No names offered to the wind. No answering calls from below. No stepping where the path looks easier than it should."

"What about where it looks terrible?" Eli asked.

Orin answered without turning. "That is often honest."

Eli looked unconvinced. "Comforting."

Orin did not soften. "It was not meant to be."

Avery checked his chalk, his field knife, and the small wedge he had taken from the hold's tool shelf. Orin had seen him take it and had neither objected nor approved. He disliked how often those were beginning to feel like the same thing.

They stepped out onto the cliff shelf.

The sky opened hard above them.

*

Anchorfall Cliffs did not announce themselves with height.

Height was ordinary.

The Continuum had always been generous with ways to fall.

Anchorfall announced itself with work.

The road from the route-hold rose along the escarpment, cut into steps where the stone permitted shape and repaired with bracewood where it did not. Anchor posts lined the outer edge at irregular intervals. Some stood sound and recently wrapped. Some leaned outward, iron cores exposed through old stone. Some were only square scars where posts had been torn out and not yet replaced.

Across the ravine, matching anchors marked the opposite cliff.

Between them hung almost nothing.

Avery stopped at the first overlook despite Orin continuing three steps ahead.

Old bridges had crossed here once. Not one span but many, layered at different heights: freight routes wide enough for carts, narrow walking lines, covered ropeways, scaffold paths, service bridges built beneath other bridges for hands brave enough or poor enough to repair passage from below. Most were gone now. Their broken ends jutted from both cliffs like unfinished sentences.

Below, the ravine kept what had fallen.

Bridge sections lay in the depths, blocky and huge, some split clean through, some lodged across lower terraces where rain had gathered in square pockets. Pale seam-light crawled through cracks in the debris and vanished under water. Rootvine grew in sheltered shelves, narrow green stems climbing the stone in vertical runs where damp had survived the wind.

And people lived among it.

Not many. Enough.

Smoke rose from covered cook pits on a lower shelf. Bright cloth strips snapped from a rope shed. Two figures carried a coil of line between them along a path so narrow Avery's knees objected on their behalf. Farther down, a lift frame stood over open air, its wheel turning slowly, though no platform hung from it.

Eli came to stand beside him.

"This place should be louder," Eli said.

Avery looked again.

He was right.

There was movement, but not the amount the structures promised. A working cliff settlement had rhythms: call and answer, rope creak, tool knock, warning bells, the irritated efficiency of people doing dangerous jobs they had done yesterday and intended to do tomorrow.

Anchorfall had smoke.

It had motion.

It did not have enough sound.

Orin had stopped ahead of them.

She had noticed too.

"Hands near tools," she said.

Avery obeyed.

Eli's hand drifted toward the flask, stopped, then moved instead to the wall. He set two fingers against the stone and frowned.

"There is a route below us," he said.

"There are many routes below us," Avery said.

"No." Eli's head tilted. "One that wants to be above us."

Orin turned.

That got her attention.

Then the first scream came from the lower shelf.

It did not travel cleanly. The ravine caught it, broke it, and threw it back in pieces. By the time it reached the cliff road, it sounded like several people at several distances, all arriving too late.

Avery was moving before Orin ordered it.

That was probably a mistake.

He made it anyway.

The path down to the lower working shelf began beside a pair of anchor posts wrapped in red cord. It looked serviceable from above. Halfway down, Avery saw the problem. The steps were good. Too good. Recently swept. Recently edged. A practical route, inviting speed.

"Stop," he said.

Eli stopped behind him with a boot already lifted.

Orin stopped behind Eli.

Below them, someone shouted, "This way!"

Avery looked.

A woman in a field medic's gray wrap stood beside the lower rope shed, one hand pressed to the shoulder of a bleeding man, the other waving them down. Behind her, three workers clustered near a lift frame whose platform had jammed halfway between shelves. Two more people were trapped on the platform, gripping the rail while it swung slightly in the wind.

Past them, at the far edge of the shelf, a dark shape moved behind stacked bracewood.

Not large enough to be the worst thing in the world.

Large enough.

Eli whispered, "That is not the honest way down."

Avery looked again at the clean steps.

They did not lead to the medic.

They led to the gap behind her.

The route had laid itself half a pace wrong, guiding the eye to the shelf while placing the body toward open air. A person running hard enough to help would reach the final turn, trust the last three steps, and go straight over the edge.

Below, the field medic waved again.

"No!" Avery called down. "Stay where you are."

She froze.

Good.

Then the dark shape came around the bracewood.

The workers saw it and scattered badly.

The creature moved low to the stone, not with the lunging force of a Braceback, not with the careless hunger of ordinary predators. It placed each foot as if testing whether the shelf deserved weight. Dark stone plates covered its shoulders and flanks, too smooth in some places and split in others. Pale route seams glowed between them with each breath. Its head was wedge-shaped and wrong, the jaw divided down the front into two hard halves that opened slightly apart before meeting again.

An anchor-biting mouth, Avery thought.

Then refused the thought before it became a name.

The thing did not charge.

It stepped left.

The workers stepped right, away from it, toward the false-clean stair.

It stepped again.

Herding.

"It is moving them," Avery said.

"Yes," Orin said.

Her voice had gone colder than the wind.

The field medic understood a breath after Avery did. She grabbed the bleeding man's collar and hauled him back from the false route with admirable violence.

"Not the stairs!" she shouted.

One of the workers on the lift platform panicked anyway.

He climbed the rail.

The platform swung.

The creature's split mouth opened.

No sound came out.

The anchor post beside the false stair answered instead, giving a low metallic hum that pulled every eye toward it.

The climbing worker looked at the hum.

His foot slipped.

He fell between platform and shelf without even enough time to scream properly.

The sound he made when he hit the lower brace beams stripped away the last of Avery's hope that this was only a route problem.

Eli flinched hard.

Avery did not look at him.

Could not.

"Orin," Avery said.

"Left wall," Orin said. "Old maintenance cut. Not the stair."

Avery found it a second later: a narrow descent half-hidden by hanging rootvine and old repair cloth, miserable footing, irregular handholds, no invitation anywhere in its shape.

Honest, then.

They went down fast and badly.

Orin first, because she knew where danger preferred to stand. Avery after. Eli last, muttering under his breath without sound becoming words.

Halfway down, the creature lifted its head.

It saw them.

Or felt them.

The pale seams along its shoulders brightened once, then dimmed.

On the lower shelf, the field medic dragged the bleeding man behind the rope shed and shouted at the remaining workers with the kind of authority that came from having kept fools alive for years and resenting how often the work repeated.

"Back wall! Backs to stone! Do not run the clean line!"

Avery almost liked her.

The creature did not.

It moved toward the rope shed.

Not directly. Never directly. It angled between the workers and the safe wall, forcing them to choose between its mouth and the false route. The split jaw clicked once. The nearest anchor post answered. Another clean path shimmered into suggestion along the shelf edge where no path had been a moment before.

One worker turned toward it.

Eli shouted, "Don't!"

The worker stopped because fear sometimes had enough room to obey volume.

The creature turned toward Eli.

Avery saw the opening and hated it.

Avery kept his eyes on the creature. "Eli, stay above the lower line."

Eli did not look away. "I know."

"Do you?" Avery asked.

"No," Eli said. "But I heard you."

That would have to do.

They reached the shelf.

Orin moved first, pale cord already in hand. She cast one weighted end across the false-clean stair. The weight hit stone, skipped, and stuck at the edge where the final step had tried to become air. The false route shivered.

The creature swung toward her.

Its mouth opened wider.

Inside, there were no teeth. Only dark ridges like broken anchor clamps.

"Eyes low," Orin said.

Avery lowered his gaze to the creature's feet.

They were worse.

Each step left a faint square impression that did not match the shape of its paws. The shelf remembered anchor points where the creature placed itself. Temporary ones. Wrong ones.

It was not only walking on the route.

It was making the route answer.

"That thing is laying claims," Avery said.

"Small ones," Orin said.

Avery kept his gaze on the wrong square marks. "Small is relative."

Orin's answer was immediate. "Move the workers."

Avery moved.

He reached the field medic as she was binding the bleeding man's shoulder with one hand and holding a route knife in the other. Her face had gone gray beneath windburn and dust, but her grip was steady.

"How many?" Avery asked.

"Alive?" she snapped.

Avery decided not to parse that. "Useful category."

"Six here. Two on the lift. One below." Her mouth tightened. "Not alive below."

He shifted his attention to the lift. "Can it be lowered?"

The field medic tightened the binding with her teeth. "Not while that thing keeps the anchor singing."

The anchor post beside the jammed lift hummed again, a deep metal note that made the hanging platform tilt toward the gap. The two trapped workers clung to the rail.

The creature stepped toward them.

Avery saw the whole shape then.

Not hunger first.

Completion.

The false stair. The humming anchor. The lift suspended between shelves. The workers placed like weights in a bad diagram. The creature did not need to kill them all by tooth or claw. It needed them to choose the places Anchorfall had already lost.

The open-door debt had found a local animal clever enough or damaged enough to make use of it.

Or the animal had found the debt.

Either answer was bad.

"We need its attention off the lift," Avery said.

"That is not difficult," Eli said behind him. "That is the part I dislike."

He stood near the lower line Avery had warned him about, not crossing it. His face had gone sharp and pale. His hand was not on the flask. It was pressed against the stone at his side.

Listening.

Eli's fingers pressed harder against the stone. "It wants the moving thing. The platform. Not the people exactly. Their weight."

Avery looked from Eli to the swinging platform. "Can you make it want something else?"

Eli's mouth tightened. "I can make myself interesting."

"No," Avery said.

Eli gave him a quick look. "You asked."

"I regretted it immediately," Avery said.

Orin's cord snapped tight across the false stair. The creature lunged.

Not at Orin.

At the cord.

It caught one square weight in its split mouth and bit down. The marked face cracked.

Orin's hand jerked, but she did not let go.

Avery started toward her.

A voice from above said, "Sister Orin, that looks expensive."

Orin did not look up.

"Rook," she said, with the weary precision of a person naming a predictable problem. "If you are about to do something clever, choose useful instead."

"I resent the implication that I distinguish those under pressure."

The man came down the service rig as if gravity had only advisory authority.

He slid along an old rope, boots striking the wall twice, one hand catching a brace pin, the other holding a coil of climbing line. Dark hair whipped into his eyes. His coat was patched, dusty, and cut close for movement. A route knife flashed at his hip. He landed on the lower shelf between the false stair and the lift, exactly where no careful person should have stood and exactly where the creature looked next.

Rook smiled at it.

"No," Orin said.

"See?" Rook said. "Useful already. It understands me."

"It understands movement," Avery said.

Rook glanced at him, quick and bright. "Then I'll try to be profound later."

The creature lunged.

Rook was no longer there.

Not vanished. Moved. Fast enough that Avery's eye caught the second half of it first: Rook sliding under the lift chain, hooking his climbing line around the jammed guide brace, and throwing the free end toward the field medic.

"Tie that to something with a conscience," he called.

The field medic caught the line, stared for half a breath, then wrapped it around the rope shed's main post.

"That post is cracked!" she shouted.

Rook called back, "Then tie it well!"

Avery understood the plan just late enough to be irritated by it.

The jammed lift platform swung toward the drop. Rook had not tried to lower it. He had given it a second anchor, a bad one, temporary and angled through the rope shed. If the platform slipped, the line might hold long enough to keep the workers from falling.

Might.

Rook ran along the shelf edge toward the anchor post, not away from it.

The creature followed.

"He is using himself as the wrong route," Eli said.

There was awe in it.

And fear.

Avery disliked both.

"Of course he is," Orin said.

She snapped the broken weight free from the creature's mouth with one hard pull. The cord came loose, shorter now by one square weight and therefore less trustworthy.

The creature recoiled. Pale seams flared along its jaw.

Rook reached the humming anchor post and slapped one palm against it.

"Bad idea," Avery said.

"Yes," Orin said.

Neither of them had time to stop him.

Rook drove his route knife into the old cord wrapped around the anchor post and cut downward.

The hum broke.

Not stopped.

Broke.

It came apart into several ugly notes, each one catching in a different anchor along the shelf. The lift platform dropped half a body length, then jerked hard against Rook's line. The rope shed post cracked with a sound like a bone deciding against loyalty.

The trapped workers screamed.

The field medic threw herself against the tied line and held it with both hands.

Avery got there a heartbeat later, bracing the line around his forearm and shoulder. Pain bit deep. He set his boots and leaned back.

"Now!" he shouted.

Eli moved.

No fire. No light. Only Eli, the stone, and the pressure between them.

He moved to the shelf edge where the false route kept trying to become a path and pressed both hands flat to the stone.

"There," he said through his teeth. "That is the lie. That one."

Orin was already crossing toward him.

The creature turned from Rook to Eli.

Rook saw it.

Avery saw Rook see it.

"Don't," Avery said, because he already knew the shape of the man's thought.

Rook did it anyway.

He ran at the creature from the side, not attacking its body but throwing his shoulder into the line of its attention. At the same time he snapped the loose end of his coil around one of the dark plates along its flank.

The plate caught.

The creature twisted.

Rook let the line run through his gloved hand, then planted one boot against a brace stump and pulled with everything he had.

He was not strong enough to move it.

He only needed it to correct for him.

The creature shifted one foot to keep balance. Its square wrong-print landed exactly where Eli had marked the lie with his hands.

Orin dropped the shortened cord across that spot.

"Keth," she said.

The shelf answered.

Not kindly.

Effectively.

The false route collapsed into ordinary broken edge. The creature's foot lost its claim. Stone failed under it, not much, not enough to send the whole body over, but enough to make the thing stagger toward the ravine instead of the workers.

"Pull!" Avery shouted.

The field medic pulled the shed line.

Avery pulled.

The lift platform swung inward, slammed against the shelf, and stayed there long enough for the two workers to scramble off and fall hard onto stone.

Alive.

Good enough.

The creature recovered with a movement so fast it erased any comfort the word stagger had offered. Its split mouth snapped at Rook's line and cut through it. Rook fell backward, rolled once, and came up too close to the edge.

Eli took one step toward him.

The creature saw.

Orin's hand shot out and caught Eli by the back of his coat.

Orin snapped, "No."

Eli stopped.

Rook did not wait to be rescued.

He threw his cut line straight into the creature's face.

It did no damage.

It bought one flinch.

The creature flinched at the motion. Avery crossed the last distance with the survey wedge in hand and drove it into the cracked anchor cord Rook had cut. Not a repair. A refusal. A blunt piece of field judgment shoved where the route wanted to keep singing.

The humming anchor post went silent.

Every false-clean edge on the shelf went dull.

For one breath the creature stood exposed as only a body. Stone plates. Pale seams. Wrong mouth. No borrowed route beneath it.

It hated that.

Avery did not need a name to know hatred when a thing turned its whole body toward him.

Then Orin's cord struck the shelf in front of it, cutting the creature off from Avery, the lift, and the workers.

Not binding it.

Drawing a line it did not want to cross without its borrowed anchor-song.

The creature backed one step.

Then another.

Toward the ravine.

Away from them.

Rook lifted one hand, palm out, calm enough to look insulting.

"Yes," he said softly. "That way."

The creature's split mouth opened.

For a moment Avery thought it would try again.

Instead it gave ground to the only route left and dropped from the shelf.

Not falling.

Climbing.

It went over the edge backward, dark claws finding seams in the vertical stone. Pale lines flickered down its body as it descended into the ravine. Within seconds it was only motion between bridge wreckage. Then only a dark interruption in pale light.

Then nothing.

No one moved.

The rope shed post cracked once more, late and resentful, but held.

One of the rescued workers vomited over the side of the shelf.

No one commented.

Eli lowered himself to sit on the stone exactly where he had been standing. His hands shook once. He hid it badly and with great determination.

Rook looked at Orin.

Orin looked at the cut cord, the cracked weight, the damaged anchor, the workers, the dead man below, then finally at Rook.

"You were told to wait," she said.

Rook brushed dust from his sleeve. "I did. Then the situation became rude."

Orin's gaze dropped briefly to the damaged post. "You cut a live anchor."

Rook lifted one shoulder. "A lying live anchor."

"That distinction will comfort the repair crew," Orin said.

Rook looked toward the cracked cord. "I will phrase the apology beautifully."

Orin stepped close enough that Rook had to tilt his chin down to meet her eyes.

Rook did not.

Avery watched the two of them and understood the shape at once. Old friction, yes. Not strangers. Orin knew exactly how Rook broke rules. Rook knew exactly which of her silences meant anger and which meant fear.

He respected her.

He also expected to survive her disapproval.

That was dangerous in its own way.

"Next time," Orin said, "choose useful before clever."

Rook's smile softened by a fraction. "I heard you the first time."

Orin studied him. "You rarely do."

Rook's smile returned. "I rarely admit it."

The field medic came toward them, one sleeve dark with someone else's blood and both hands still shaking from the line she had held. She looked past Avery toward the lower beams where the fallen worker lay broken and still.

The living did not erase him.

It only made the accounting worse.

"That thing took three before you came," she said.

No one answered quickly.

"Two from the lower ledge yesterday," she continued. "We thought bad rope. Then one this morning by the salt bins. We thought panic. Then this." Her voice hardened around the word because grief needed something to grip. "That was no rope."

"No," Avery said. "It was not."

She looked at Orin then, and some of the anger changed shape. Not less. More disciplined.

The field medic asked, "Can you close the shelf?"

Orin looked at the damaged anchor post. "Temporarily."

The field medic accepted that with a short nod. "Temporarily is what we have."

"Yes," Orin said.

That seemed to satisfy the field medic because it was not dressed as comfort.

Avery went to the edge and looked down once. The fallen man lay across the lower brace beams, one arm hanging loose over open air. A recovery would require rope, time, and exhausted people with no choice but to do the work.

Orin's voice carried from the damaged anchor post. "Salt him before the burn. Dead flesh answers badly in places like this."

Avery looked back.

Eli had gone still.

The field medic closed her eyes once. "We know."

"Know it twice," Orin said.

Anchorfall was inhabited.

That meant every failure had witnesses.

Rook came to stand beside him.

"You are Avery, then," he said.

Avery looked at him.

Rook's eyes were sharp, alert, already reading exits before faces. Lean build. Travel-worn coat. Hair in his eyes. Youth in the face, road-dust in every practiced movement.

"Sister Orin said you would be unpleasant," Rook said.

Avery looked over at Orin.

She had moved to the damaged anchor post and was already setting her shortened cord into a temporary hold.

"She used my name?" Avery asked.

"No," Rook said. "That was the clue."

Eli laughed once from the ground.

Small.

Real.

Avery disliked how much he was relieved by it.

"And you are Rook," Avery said.

Rook offered a small bow. "Usually."

Avery repeated, "Usually?"

Rook spread his hands. "There are forms for the exceptions."

Avery stared at him.

Rook looked delighted.

"You do safety reports, don't you?" Rook asked.

Avery answered before he could decide not to. "Reluctantly."

Rook brightened. "I knew we would get along."

Avery added, "You cut a live anchor."

Rook looked pleased. "There it is."

Eli stood slowly and tried to hide the effort. Rook noticed. His expression shifted, not into concern exactly, but into attention without mockery.

"You heard the shelf," Rook said.

Eli shrugged one shoulder. "It was loud."

Rook's attention sharpened. "No, it wasn't."

Eli's mouth tightened.

Avery looked for the joke in Eli's face and did not find it.

"Then I heard it anyway," Eli said.

Rook considered him for a moment, then gave a short nod.

"Useful," Rook decided.

Eli looked toward Orin. "He says it less terribly than you."

"He has had less practice being right," Orin said from the anchor post.

Rook placed one hand over his heart. "Wounded."

"Not yet," Avery said.

Rook pointed at him. "You do safety reports and threats."

Avery folded his arms. "Field efficiency."

The field medic cut through them by pointing to the rope shed, the damaged anchor, the injured worker, and the shelf beyond, all with the same blood-marked hand.

"If everyone is finished establishing whose bad habits are historic," she said, "we are low on bandage herbs, out of hard chalk, and the salt bin is cracked. We cannot hold this shelf if that thing comes back tonight."

That ended the conversation cleanly.

Orin tied off the last temporary line and stepped back from the anchor post. The hum did not return. The false stair remained only broken stone.

Orin turned from the repaired line. "Where is the nearest supply?"

"Which kind?" the field medic asked.

Orin's expression did not change.

The field medic gave a short humorless breath and pointed with two fingers, first along the lower shelf, then farther east where the cliff road vanished behind broken bridge anchors.

"Food and medicine are close," she said. "Terrace village below the south crop wall. Rootvine plots, rain cisterns, drying sheds, enough bandage herb if the growers have not stripped it bare already."

"And chalk?" Avery asked.

The field medic shook her head. "Not there."

"Anchoring salt?" Orin asked.

The field medic shook her head again. "Not enough to matter."

Rook glanced at Avery's sleeve, then at the field medic's hands. "That seems optimistic for present company."

"The route equipment is farther," the medic said. "Sealed storehouse east of the old rope road. Hard chalk, anchoring salt, spare cord, pins, wedges, proper brace clamps. If the road has held."

Avery heard the if.

So did Orin.

Eli looked toward the eastward cliffs where smoke thinned into wind and the broken bridge lines vanished around a curve of stone.

"It hasn't," he said.

The field medic turned toward him.

Eli swallowed. "Not fully. Something below keeps pulling at it."

Orin studied him for one breath, then looked to Avery.

Avery understood the question without liking it.

They could stay and mend a single damaged shelf with insufficient materials. They could retreat to the route-hold and let Anchorfall spend the night discovering how little their temporary work had bought. They could take the east road for equipment and arrive tired at a place already willing to lie.

Or they could keep the wounded alive first.

There were no good choices.

Only timed ones.

"Food and medicine first," Avery said. "Then chalk and salt."

"And cord," Orin said.

The field medic gave her a grim look. "Everyone needs cord."

Rook picked up the severed end of his climbing line from the ground, inspected it, and held it up.

"I can contribute less cord than I had this morning."

"You can contribute quiet," Orin said.

Rook sighed. "No one ever asks for what I have in abundance."

Avery looked at him, then at Eli, then at Orin's shortened cord and the dead man below and the workers still shaking beside the lift.

The team shape was not comfortable.

That did not mean it was wrong.

"You know the terrace village?" Avery asked Rook.

Rook smiled.

Avery regretted the question immediately.

"I know a road down," Rook said.

Avery narrowed his eyes. "That is not the same answer."

"No," Rook said. "But it is the one we have."

Orin closed her hand around the cracked square weight she had taken back from the creature's mouth.

"Then we go down," she said. "Food. Medicine. No heroics with the equipment road until people stop bleeding."

Rook looked faintly offended. "You say that like heroics are a limited resource."

"They are when I am forced to spend them on you," Orin said.

The field medic nodded once, sharp and grateful in a way that had no softness in it.

Below them, wind moved through the broken bridge bones of Anchorfall.

Somewhere in the ravine, stone scraped against stone.

The creature was gone.

For now.

Avery did not say that aloud.

Some truths did not need help finding them.
