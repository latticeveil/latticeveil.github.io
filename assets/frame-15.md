Morning did not clean the road.

It only made the damage easier to count.

The route-hold stood in sections now. One outer wall remained because it had fallen inward against a brace post and become too stubborn to finish dying. The supply shed had lost half its roof. The Underhold entrance was still open, but the stone lip around it had split into three pieces, and every person who climbed out of it did so with one hand on the wall and the other over their mouth.

Ash lay in the seams.

Salt lay in the ash.

The place where the Anchor Maw had fallen was not a body. That made it worse in a way Avery did not have a word for. There was no carcass to drag away, no hide to cut, no bones to mark the size of the thing that had nearly erased them. The creature had come apart after the core was struck, slowly at first, then all at once, stone and plate and pale seam collapsing into ash-gray dust until the wind took what it could and left the rest in the low parts of the road.

Where the Anchor Maw had been, there was only a hollow and a ring of scraped stone.

Dain's burial fire had burned down before dawn.

Orin had not slept near it.

She had sat where the smoke could pass her without touching the wounded, one knee raised, her bow across her lap, and watched until the last coal went black. No one had asked whether she wanted company. Avery had thought about it twice and decided both times that asking would force her to answer when silence was the only mercy left to her.

Before the evacuation carts left for the terrace town, Orin called over the two young bandits who had followed Dain through the breach. The youngest still looked sick whenever his eyes drifted toward the burial shelf. The knife woman stood beside him with her arms folded tight, trying to look old enough that fear would stop finding her.

"You do not go back to taking from people because the road taught you it was easier than asking," Orin told them.

The youngest bandit stared at the ash between his boots. "What do we do instead?"

"You work," Orin answered. "You learn a trade that leaves the next person with as much as they had before you arrived. You eat because you earned a place at a table, not because someone smaller than you could not stop you."

The knife woman looked toward the carts. "They will not want us."

"No," Orin replied. "Not yet."

The answer made the young woman look up.

Orin held her gaze. "They will take you because I asked them to. I will answer for you until you can answer for yourselves. Do not make that promise a lie."

The knife woman swallowed. Some of the stiffness went out of her shoulders.

"I know they have reasons not to want us," she said. "We took things. Food. Tools. Things people needed. I do not know what I am supposed to say when they look at me and remember that."

"Do not ask them to forget," Orin replied. "Give them something else to remember as well."

It had taken Orin most of an hour to convince the terrace town's supply steward. Avery had heard only pieces while he carried bandages between the wounded: Orin offering route labor, repair work, and her own return visits; the steward naming every sensible reason to refuse; Orin agreeing with each one and asking again anyway.

When the carts finally left, the two young bandits rode near the rear beneath clean blankets. The youngest sat with his hands folded in his lap. The knife woman kept watching the townsfolk around them, tense whenever someone looked back, but she did not reach for a weapon or look away. They were watched by people who were not ready to trust them and protected by Orin's word until they could earn something better.

As the rear cart started down the road, the youngest bandit rubbed one sleeve across his face and failed to make the motion look casual. "Thank you, Sister Orin."

The knife woman looked embarrassed by how quickly the words reached her eyes. She nodded to Orin once. "Thank you."

Orin watched until the cart passed the broken wall and the road carried it out of sight. Then she turned back toward the people who were still too wounded to travel.

Eli was among them. He slept badly, which was still better than not sleeping at all.

The field medic had stitched the wound under his ribs and wrapped him tight enough that he complained only in small, breathless pieces. His flask stayed in his right hand even when he was asleep. The new staff lay beside him on the blanket, light enough to be carried one-handed, pale wood bound in worked cord, the head of it capped with a dull metal ring that never quite stopped catching the light.

Rook was supposed to be sitting still.

Rook was not sitting still.

He had found a cracked wallstone and made a seat of it at the edge of the hollow. His ribs were wrapped. One cheek was bruised. The knuckles of his left hand were scraped raw. None of that stopped him from tossing a dark, palm-sized object into the air and catching it with the careless rhythm of a man who had never once in his life been warned not to play with something dangerous.

Up.

Catch.

Up.

Catch.

Avery watched the object turn in the gray light.

It was not stone. Not exactly.

The thing was black at its outer edge, but the center held a muted amber depth like fire seen through old bottle glass. Hair-thin pale lines ran through it in branching patterns. One face held a narrow dark slit where the pale lines refused to meet. Every time it rose, Avery saw the lines shift, not moving, but refusing to be remembered in the same arrangement twice.

Up.

Orin called across the hollow. "Rook."

Rook closed his fist around the object before it fell. "Yes, Sister Orin?"

"Where did you get that?" Orin asked.

Rook looked down at his hand as if surprised to find it attached to him. "This?"

Orin stood.

The motion was quiet.

That was enough to make three nearby workers stop sorting broken brace pins.

Rook's smile thinned. "From the road."

"The road is wide," Orin replied.

Avery stepped closer.

Rook opened his hand.

The pale lines inside the core bent toward Avery for half a breath, then settled.

"That is the thing the Anchor Maw dropped, isn't it?" Avery asked.

Rook glanced at him. "You say that like I stole it."

"Did you?" Avery asked.

"It is not dead," Orin corrected him.

Rook's hand stopped moving.

Orin held out her palm. "It is ended. That is not the same."

Rook looked at the hollow, then at the object, then back at Orin. "I was going to say I improved the road by removing a tripping hazard."

Orin held her hand out farther. "Give it here."

Rook tossed it.

Orin caught it out of the air so fast Avery barely saw her hand move. Her fingers closed around the core, and for one sharp instant the amber center brightened against her skin.

She did not flinch.

That told Avery she wanted to.

Orin turned to Avery. "Avery."

He stepped forward.

She placed the core in his palm and folded his fingers over it. The thing was heavier than it looked. Not large-heavy. Meaning-heavy. It settled into his hand as if it expected to be carried.

"Hold onto this," Orin told him.

"Why me?" Avery asked.

"Because Rook will keep throwing it," Orin answered.

Rook lifted one finger. "In fairness, I was catching it as well."

Orin did not look at him. "And because I have seen it before."

Avery waited.

Orin's jaw tightened.

"Not the thing itself," Orin answered. "A drawing. I think."

"You think?" Avery asked.

Orin looked at the core in his hand as if it had answered a question she did not remember asking.

Orin looked back toward the road. "I was young. There were marks around it. A place my mother wanted me away from. I remember enough to know I do not like remembering it."

Something moved behind Avery's boot.

Pebble slipped out from between two broken supply crates with the soft, quick motion of a shadow remembering it had feet. The small creature paused when it saw Avery.

For a moment, all of Pebble went still.

Not afraid, exactly.

Alert.

Avery had learned the difference on the road. Fear moved backward. Alertness measured distance.

"Hello," Avery said softly.

Pebble's ears tucked. He shifted left, then right, as if deciding which version of the path had less Avery in it.

Rook noticed. "That is the politest insult I have ever seen."

Pebble avoided Avery's boot by a wide half-circle, then trotted straight to Eli and pressed against the blanket near his arm.

Eli opened one eye. "I have been replaced by a small professional nurse."

Pebble set his chin on the blanket.

The staff beside Eli gave a soft click.

Everyone looked at it.

The ring at its head brightened, then dimmed, then brightened again. The worked cord along the grip tightened as if a hand had closed around it from the inside. A thin line of pale light ran down the shaft and stopped near Eli's fingers.

Eli lifted his head a little.

"No," the field medic said from behind him.

Eli lowered his head again. "I was not sitting up."

"You were beginning to become the idea of sitting up," the field medic replied.

Eli settled back onto the blanket. "That feels legally different."

The staff clicked again.

Pebble sneezed.

The light inside the staff jumped.

Eli stared at Pebble. Exhaustion had hollowed his face, but a faint smile found its way through. "Wow. You seem special, Pebble."

Rook winced. "That sentence was wounded before it left you."

Eli closed his eye again. "So am I."

Avery expected Orin to correct the moment into something useful. She did not. Her eyes stayed on the staff, then on Pebble, then on the core in Avery's fist.

For a breath she looked almost afraid of how many old things had started speaking at once.

Then the expression closed.

Orin straightened. "We need information."

Rook glanced toward Avery's pack. "About the core?"

"About a place," Orin answered.

Avery tucked the core into an inner wrap of his pack, close enough to feel its weight through the cloth. "What place?"

Orin looked beyond the broken wall, toward the route that ran south and west and away from the hold.

Orin began carefully. "I saw the place when I was young."

No one spoke quickly enough to interrupt her.

Orin seemed to notice that and disliked it.

"Not clearly," she added. "Stone braces. Bad marks. Men my mother knew enough to fear. Somewhere inside, a drawing shaped like the thing Rook was throwing."

"A ruin?" Avery asked.

"A mistake," Orin answered.

Rook leaned forward. "Those are often the same building."

Orin gave him half a look. It was not a warning. Not quite.

Orin's gaze stayed on the distant route. "My mother pulled me away from it. Hard enough to bruise. She knew they were bandits. I remember her hand more clearly than the place."

The words settled badly.

Avery glanced at Eli. Eli was awake now, eyes narrowed, not with pain but with the particular focus he got when a road said something under hearing range.

Avery nodded toward the pack. "You think that place has something to do with this."

Orin looked at the pack where he had secured the core.

"I think I saw that shape there. I do not remember why it mattered. I do not remember whether anyone explained it to me. But I remember being told not to touch the wall beside it," Orin said.

"Do you know where the ruin is?" Avery asked.

Orin shook her head. "No."

Rook sighed. "I miss when answers were kinder."

"I know the direction. And I know the sort of people who keep memory longer than paper," Orin answered.

That was how they began to leave the route-hold without leaving it behind.

The survivors did not ask them to stay. No one had the strength for the argument, and everyone understood what the hollow meant. A place that had survived by being hidden underneath itself could not trust that trick anymore. The Underhold would be emptied in shifts. The route-hold would become a marker, a warning, maybe someday a rebuilt waystation if the road proved kind and people proved stubborn.

For now, it was a wound with doors.

The first answers came from people with soot in their hair.

Orin chose three survivors who had worked the outer roads before the hold took them in: a woman with a burned sleeve and a measuring cord around her neck, an old brace setter whose hands shook only when he stopped using them, and a boy too young to have his opinions treated with care but old enough to have carried water through the night.

They sat near the broken supply shed while Avery repacked medicine, rope, and clean cloth. Eli remained on a low cart with Pebble against his side and the staff across his knees. Rook hovered in the way of a man who had been ordered to rest and had interpreted rest as standing in different places.

Orin described what she remembered. "A doorway that never opened. Southwest of here. Old brace stone. Marks that do not match route law."

The woman with the measuring cord crossed herself in the local way, two fingers to wrist, then throat. "Dead gate."

The brace setter grunted. "There are six dead gates if you listen to people who want children home before dark."

Orin shook her head. "This one had pins. Metal set into stone. Not old temple work. Not natural seam."

The boy looked up. "My uncle said there was a door that made lamps forget which way was down."

Rook blinked. "Lamps have a down?"

"Oil does," Avery answered.

The boy bristled at Rook. "The flame leaned wrong. Even when there was no wind."

Orin crouched so she was not towering over him. "Where did your uncle see it?"

The boy looked down. "He didn't. He found a pack there. A good one. Took the buckles and left the rest."

"Why leave the rest?" Avery asked.

The boy swallowed.

"Because the rest still had a hand in it," the woman answered for him.

Rook stopped shifting.

The brace setter rubbed his thumb along the side of one finger. "Southwest, then south again when the road splits around the old cistern shelves. If it is the one I am thinking of, route-thread goes slack there. Not cut. Slack. Like the road forgot to pull back."

Orin nodded once. "That narrows it."

The brace setter frowned. "It narrows it to a stretch that eats careless feet. You should not go."

"I know," Orin replied.

The brace setter frowned at her. "That was me saying you should not go."

Orin held his gaze. "I heard you."

The old man studied her face.

Whatever he found there made him look away first.

The second answers came from paper, which was less frightened and more stubborn.

They kept Eli on the low cart between stops and made him walk only when a doorway or bad stretch of road left no kinder option.

The mapkeeper lived in a room that had once been part of a toll arch. The arch had cracked decades ago. The room remained because the mapkeeper had braced it, cursed it, and refused to move. Shelves leaned against shelves. Flat stones hung from cord loops. Scratched boards were stacked by region, then by age, then by the mapkeeper's opinion of the person who had drawn them.

The mapkeeper interrupted before Orin finished speaking. "No."

She was small, square, and old enough that age had stopped looking fragile on her and started looking like another tool she had sharpened.

Orin folded her hands. "You do not know what I am asking."

The mapkeeper tapped her stylus against the table. "You are asking about the dead gate south of the cistern shelves because the hold broke and now everyone thinks old mistakes are new solutions."

"I like her," Rook whispered to Avery.

The mapkeeper pointed a stylus at him. "If you touch anything, I will take a finger as a shelving fee."

Rook put both hands behind his back. "I admire her from here."

Avery set a clean bandage roll and two packets of dried feverleaf on the mapkeeper's table. "We can trade."

The mapkeeper looked at the supplies, then at Eli on the threshold. Eli leaned against the doorframe with his flask in one hand and the staff in the other, pretending not to need either as badly as he did.

The mapkeeper pointed her stylus toward Eli. "That one should be lying down."

Eli shifted against the doorframe. "That one agrees. But people keep putting plot-important ruins in walking distance."

The mapkeeper stared at him.

Eli looked to Avery. "Too much?"

Avery considered him. "A little."

The mapkeeper took the feverleaf.

The mapkeeper pulled a slate from the pile. "There were three survey lines south of the cistern shelves. One collapsed. One was eaten by a wet seam. One was marked abandoned after raiders started using it."

Orin went still.

Avery noticed because Orin was very good at not going still.

"Which raiders?" Orin asked.

The mapkeeper shuffled through a stack of thin slate pieces. "The kind who scratched ownership into things they did not own."

Orin studied the slate pile. "That does not narrow it."

"It does if you know the scratches," the mapkeeper answered.

She found a slate and turned it.

The mark had been copied in white chalk: three short cuts leaning inward over a longer line, like a broken roof over a road.

Orin's fingers curled once.

Rook saw it too. His joking expression faded and left the older face underneath.

Rook kept his voice low. "Sister?"

"Not here," Orin warned.

The mapkeeper's eyes sharpened. "You know it."

"I knew people who used it," Orin answered.

The mapkeeper leaned forward. "Then you know enough to stay away from whatever they hid."

Orin met the mapkeeper's stare. "No. I know enough to know hiding it did not make it harmless."

The mapkeeper held her gaze for a long moment.

Then she drew a line on a scrap of route cloth with a piece of charcoal. Cistern shelves. Broken survey path. A dry spill channel. Three standing stones that were not stones if one knew how to read brace work.

The mapkeeper pushed the route cloth across the table. "This gets you near. Not in."

"What gets us in?" Avery asked.

"A fool, a pry bar, and someone who knows old raider signs," the mapkeeper answered.

Rook raised a hand.

Everyone looked at him.

"I can be two of those," he said.

Orin took the cloth. "You will be neither until your ribs stop arguing with air."

Rook lowered his hand. "My ribs and I are in discussion."

Orin folded the route cloth. "Lose."

The third answer came from a man who smelled like wet stone and old rope.

They found him below the cistern shelves, where water had once been collected and measured and sent along narrow channels to gardens that no longer existed. The shelves rose in layered cuts of gray stone, each one stained by mineral lines. Broken pipes stuck from the walls like snapped bones. The salvager had made a camp beneath a slanted slab and hung bits of found metal from cord loops so they chimed when the wind changed.

He called himself Harn.

He had one eye, two pry bars, and very little interest in being helpful until Avery showed him the mapkeeper's cloth.

Then he spat into the dust.

Harn tapped one pry bar against the stone. "Bad place."

"So we have heard," Avery replied.

Harn shook his head. "No. Bad place is where someone falls and you say they should have watched their step. This is worse. This is a place that watches back."

Eli studied the shelves, pale under his bruises. "He is not wrong."

Orin glanced at him.

Eli touched the staff's head to the ground. The ring gave a faint pulse.

"It feels like the road is holding its breath under the stone," he said. "Not trapped. Waiting."

Harn's good eye went to Eli's flask, then to the staff, then away.

"I do not ask about other people's sickness," the salvager said. "Keeps me alive."

Rook nodded. "Wise."

Harn ignored him and pointed his pry bar toward the lower shelves. "There is a false fall where the spill channel broke. Looks blocked. Is not blocked. Someone set stones there to look like collapse. Behind it, old path. After that, I do not go."

Harn scratched at his beard.

"Found a mark."

Orin's voice was careful. "Three cuts over a road?"

Harn looked at her for the first time with real attention.

"You one of them?"

"I was," Orin answered.

The wind moved the hanging metal. The chimes were thin and uneven.

"Then you know the second mark."

Orin shut her eyes.

Long enough.

Halfway along the shelf, Orin stopped.

Avery stopped behind her. "Orin?"

She did not answer.

At the far bend, where the channel dropped into shadow, a little girl stood with bare feet on the stone.

Neris.

Avery knew her only from smoke and grief and Orin's voice saying the name as if it had weight. He should not have known her face. One hand closed around the edge of a sleeve too large for her wrist.

The girl looked at Orin.

Not through her.

At her.

Orin breathed the name. "Neris."

The child opened her mouth.

No sound came.

The air around her folded wrong. Her outline broke into thin pale seams, not light and not shadow, each one trying to put her in a different place. For one instant Avery saw her twice: the child on the shelf and the same child somewhere else, falling backward through a road that had no ground under it.

Orin reached for her.

Neris vanished.

The mineral dust at the bend lifted as if something had passed through it very quickly, then settled back onto stone.

No one moved.

Eli's staff clicked once against the wall.

Orin looked back at them. "Did you see her?"

Avery did not know whether lying would be kinder.

"Yes," Avery answered.

Rook's voice came quieter than usual. "A little girl."

Orin lowered her hand.

Harn made a warding sign over his chest. "That was not one of mine."

Orin shook her head. "No."

Her face had gone bloodless, but her voice stayed under command by force alone.

"No, she was not."

The false fall took an hour to open.

Avery did most of the lifting because Rook's ribs made him useless for heavy work and Eli's wound made him worse than useless. Rook objected to that description until Orin told him he could either hold the lantern or be tied to the cart. He chose the lantern with dignity and announced that he had always wanted command responsibility.

The stones had been set with care. Not mortared. Balanced. Each one shaped or chosen to look accidental from the outside and deliberate from the inside. Someone had built the lie well.

As Avery shifted the last slab, stale air breathed out of the gap.

It smelled of chalk, cold metal, and a dryness that had been kept too long.

Pebble pressed against Eli's boot.

The staff in Eli's hand gave a nervous little tremor.

Eli glanced at the staff. "I hate when the stick has an opinion."

"Do not argue with it," Orin replied.

Eli adjusted his grip on the staff. "I was planning to be emotionally available to it."

"Generous," Avery muttered.

Rook held the lantern higher. "Leadership is sacrifice."

Avery squeezed through the gap.

Behind him, Orin entered and stopped at the first bend.

Her hand went to the wall.

Avery turned. "What is it?"

Orin looked younger for half a second, which was impossible and still true.

Orin's hand left the wall.

No one moved.

Eli winced. "Sorry. That came from the road before it came from manners."

Orin looked at him for a long breath.

"Do not spend strength on memories that are not yours to carry," Orin answered.

Eli nodded once.

They kept moving.

The second mark waited where the narrow path widened into a low chamber.

It had been cut into the stone at knee height, nearly hidden behind mineral crust: the three inward cuts over the road line, and beneath it a smaller mark like a hooked crescent with a dot at its mouth.

Orin crouched.

Rook lowered the lantern. "Is that his?"

Orin did not answer immediately.

When she did, her voice had gone flat with effort.

"Yes," Orin answered.

Avery looked between them. "Whose?"

Orin brushed dust from the mark with the side of her thumb. "Dain's father."

The name made the chamber seem smaller.

Eli's hand tightened on the staff. Pebble pushed closer to his leg.

Avery looked from the mark to Orin. "The man who taught you?"

Orin nodded.

Rook's face shifted. "I thought you said Dain learned from you."

Orin kept her hand against the mark. "He learned violence from anyone who would give it shape. But his father taught me how not to die before I learned how not to become someone others needed to survive."

She stood too quickly.

For one moment, her face opened around an old hurt and did not know what to do with it. Not grief like Dain's burial. Not the raw thing she had given Neris in the smoke. This was different. Older. Complicated by gratitude, anger, and the shame of having loved someone who had stood in a bad place and called it shelter.

Then Orin breathed in.

The crack closed.

"His name was Malrec. He was gentle when he could afford it. He could afford it less often than he pretended," Orin said.

Rook lowered the lantern a little. "That is a hard kind of person to miss."

"Yes," Orin answered.

She turned toward the deeper dark.

"And a harder kind to forgive."

The passage opened after that.

Not into a temple.

Not into a shrine.

Into a workshop.

That was the first thing Avery understood, and it unsettled him more than grandeur would have.

The chamber had been carved and braced by hand. Four stone posts stood around a circular depression in the floor, each post drilled with metal pins and wrapped in old route cord. The cords crossed overhead through pulley hooks, sagging now, but arranged with purpose. Wedges held two leaning slabs in place. Thin strips of beaten metal ran from the floor depression to anchor sockets in the wall. Chalk diagrams covered every surface that could hold a line.

Measurements.

Ratios.

Failed corrections scratched out so hard the stone was scarred.

At the far side of the chamber, an unfinished frame rose from the floor.

It was shaped like a doorway only if one was generous. Two side braces leaned inward. A top piece rested unfinished between them, pinned but not sealed. The inner edge was lined with sockets, each one empty, each one marked by a different symbol. Some symbols Avery knew from route work. Some he had seen only in Orin's hands. Some made Eli look away.

Avery studied the frame. "That is not a gate."

"No," Orin agreed.

Avery looked up at the unfinished frame. "But it was meant to be."

"Yes," Orin answered.

Rook stepped into the chamber with the lantern raised high. "I was prepared for bones."

"There may still be bones," Avery replied.

Rook sighed. "Thank you. I had briefly found comfort."

Eli stayed near the entrance, one hand braced against the wall. The staff's head flickered, pale light catching in the chalk lines like water in cracks.

Eli kept his distance from the frame. "It is not asleep."

Orin turned. "What do you hear?"

Eli swallowed.

"Nothing. That is the problem." He pressed the staff harder against the floor. "Every route makes some kind of sound. Pressure, pull, complaint. This is a mouth held shut."

Pebble gave a low, unhappy chirr.

Avery crouched near the central depression. "Something was supposed to sit here."

Orin joined him.

She did not ask to see the core.

Avery took it from his pack anyway.

Not brightened.

Paled, as if the color had been pulled out of them.

Rook stopped smiling.

Rook raised one hand. "I vote we do not put the ominous object into the ominous hole."

"Seconded," Eli answered immediately.

Orin cut the discussion short. "No one is putting anything anywhere."

She said it to them.

She was looking at herself.

Avery wrapped the core again.

The chalk settled.

They searched with their hands behind their backs unless Orin gave permission. Avery read what he could by structure. Rook read the room by where a person would hide useful things if that person expected thieves. Eli read it by the places that made him nauseous and the places that made the staff hum. Orin read everything else.

They found the diagram under a weighted sheet of thin slate.

It had been protected from damp by oilcloth and from accident by a frame of rusted pins. The drawing showed the doorway in layers: stone brace, cord tension, anchor sockets, route lines, central depression, and a final shape Avery could not understand because it seemed to be drawn from the wrong side of the page.

At the bottom was a signature.

Most of it had been scraped away by time or intention.

Only four letters remained.

MALR

Orin touched the edge of the slate.

Her fingers shook once.

Only once.

Avery read the broken letters aloud. "Malrec."

Orin traced the edge of the slate. "He always left off the ending when he thought the work mattered more than the man."

Rook glanced at her. "That sounds like something he said until people repeated it back to him."

A small, pained sound left Orin.

It was not quite a laugh.

"He did," Orin answered.

They found the note behind the diagram.

It was written on layered route cloth in a cramped, slanted hand. Parts of it had faded. Parts were reinforced in darker ink, as if the writer had returned years later and still could not leave the thought alone.

Orin read silently first.

Avery watched her face and learned nothing except that the words hurt.

Then she read aloud because the room had earned witnesses.

"A stable crossing cannot be made from cord tension alone. Route law resists false distance. Echo pressure resists false return. The frame will hold shape but not passage."

"That is cheerful," Eli whispered.

Orin continued.

"An anchor is required. Not a pin. Not a stone. A living route-anchor broken from its own hunger and held at the center before the frame is woken."

Rook looked at Avery's pack.

Avery did not move.

Orin's voice tightened.

"If the old Maw stories are true, an Anchor Maw carries a core dense enough to convince the road that a place has weight where no place should be. If the stories are not true, then this remains a scholar's joke written by a thief who wanted to build a door out of punishment."

Orin stopped reading.

A final passage remained beneath the theory, written smaller than the rest.

Avery watched her thumb settle over the first word as if she could keep it inside the cloth by covering it.

When Orin continued, the steadiness had left her voice.

"Orindessa. If you found this, my hands did not outlast the waiting. I am sorry I kept this room from you. I wanted a road none of them knew how to follow. Somewhere you could take the younger ones who still knew the difference between surviving and becoming cruel. Somewhere beyond the reach of men who would turn any open door into another way to steal."

Orin drew in a breath that caught halfway.

"You should have told me," Orin said.

The words went into the old workshop and found no one left to answer them.

Orin looked down at the remaining lines.

"I thought I could finish it before secrecy became another harm I had done to you. I could not. The core remained a story, and my hands did not. Leave the frame sleeping unless you know what I did not. I am sorry, Orindessa. I wanted to give you one thing that was not a weapon."

Silence took the chamber.

The kind that had edges.

Orin lowered the note.

Avery looked toward his pack. "He thought it was a myth."

Orin looked down at the old note. "He hoped it was not."

Avery nodded toward the frame. "Did he finish it?"

Orin looked at the frame, the empty sockets, the central depression, the failed marks.

"Not before he died," Orin answered.

Rook let out a slow breath. "For once, I appreciate a man's lack of follow-through."

Eli's staff gave one last soft pulse.

Pebble pressed both front paws against Eli's boot as if holding him in place.

Avery looked at the wrapped core in his pack. It felt heavier now than when Orin had given it to him. He thought of the Anchor Maw turning to dust. The exposed core. The way the creature had ended without leaving a body behind. He thought of Dain's burial smoke, of Orin's final words to a dead girl, of Pebble stepping around him like some part of Avery had already become dangerous.

Avery rested a hand against the wrapped core. "So we have the thing he never had."

Orin folded the note along its old creases. "We have one thing he believed he needed. That is not the same as having an answer."

"But it is a door," Rook said. His voice had lost its humor. "Not a brace. Not a trick of the road. He meant to open it and go somewhere."

Eli kept his staff planted against the floor. "Somewhere else," he said. "I cannot hear where. I can only hear that this room thinks somewhere is waiting on the other side."

Avery looked at the unfinished frame. "Do we want to find out?"

Orin did not answer quickly.

The empty sockets waited around the central depression. The wrapped core sat against Avery's palm with the patient weight of a thing that had already survived being impossible.

"Yes," Orin answered at last. "But wanting to open a door is not a reason to wake it before we know what frightened the person who built it."

"What do we do?" Eli asked.

Orin looked at the unfinished door.

For a long time, no one interrupted her.

The frame stood in its hand-built silence, braced and pinned and incomplete. A dead theory waiting for the one impossible piece its maker had never found.

At last Orin put Malrec's note back against the diagram and rested her palm over the broken name.

"We learn what he did not," Orin answered.
