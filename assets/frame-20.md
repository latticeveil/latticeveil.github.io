# The First Meridian

The frame cooled by degrees.

That was the first lie it told them.

Stone could cool. Metal could stop complaining under tension. Chalk could fade back to chalk after spending too long pretending to be language. None of that meant the work was over. Orin had lived long enough in wrong places to know the difference between silence and safety, and Malrec's hidden workshop had gone silent in the exact way a trap did when it wanted the next hand to be careless.

She kept her injured palm wrapped around the Frame Jack anyway.

The cloth Avery had tied around her hand had already spotted through. Not badly. Enough to remind her that the body kept records even when the mind preferred ledgers with cleaner columns.

Across the workshop, Avery stood over Malrec's table with three pieces of failed crossing work laid in front of him: the spent Anchor Maw Core, a bent locking pin, and the survey brace he had used to help find Kaden before the line collapsed. He had arranged them without seeming to notice he had done it, each failure separated from the next by the width of two fingers.

The old habits of a man trying to make grief hold still.

Eli sat on the floor because standing had become a negotiation no one trusted him to win honestly yet. Pebble lay against his bad leg, not sleeping. The Wayhound had one ear turned toward the corridor and the other toward the dead frame. Eli's fingers rested on Pebble's rough fur, still and too careful.

The flask hung at Eli's belt.

Orin did not look at it often.

That was another lie.

She looked at it every time Eli breathed differently.

It was the same dented shape. The same worn cap. The same practical size for a hand that had learned to find it without thinking. Her holding-work circled the mouth exactly as she had made it, every plain useful line copied with a care that made anger feel like a small tool for a larger job.

Below her work, the deeper lines of the Infinite Well working waited in the metal like a second thought with teeth.

Eli had not touched the cap since he found it there.

That restraint frightened Orin almost as much as the flask.

"It needs to come out," Avery said.

Orin looked up.

Avery had his eyes on the Anchor Maw Core. It sat dark in the frame's central depression now, amber heart gone brown at the edges. It looked less like a power source than something that had been eaten and politely returned.

"Not yet," Orin said.

Avery did not reach for it. That was why she trusted him with sharp things.

"If it cools into the socket, we may have to break the seating to remove it," Avery said.

Orin looked back at the socket. "Then we break the seating."

Eli laughed once.

There was no humor in it. Humor required a certain amount of room. Eli had none left.

"Good," Eli said. "I was worried we would run out of things to break."

Pebble pressed harder against his leg.

Eli's jaw tightened. The movement was small, but pain found it. His body looked better than it had any right to look. The tremor had settled out of his hand. The cut on his cheek had sealed into a pale line. Even the blood under his nose had dried too cleanly. But when he shifted, he moved like every repaired place had been stitched to a second version of him that had not agreed to live.

Orin watched the limp before she watched his face.

The limp remained.

Good, some tired practical part of her thought.

Then she hated herself for the relief. A remaining injury proved he had not been remade completely. It did not prove he was safe.

"We are not breaking anything until we decide who is allowed to stand in this room again," Orin said.

Avery went still.

Eli looked at her then.

Not quickly. Nothing about him was quick unless anger helped. His eyes were too clear for a boy who had been half drunk on necessity for years and too sharp for a boy whose body had just been dragged away from a failed doorway.

"Allowed," Eli repeated.

Orin answered with no room in it. "Yes."

"That's a generous word for a room with a corpse in the corridor."

Orin let the sentence land. It deserved that much.

Pebble's head lifted.

"We are going back for him," Avery said.

Eli's face changed. Not softened. Changed. Like a blade turning enough to catch different light.

"I know," Eli said.

The words were controlled.

Too controlled.

Orin had heard men swear vengeance with less danger in them.

"We are going back correctly," Orin said. "With salt. Burn cloth. Cord. Tools. Enough hands to carry what can be carried and enough sense not to become useful to whatever is still listening."

"You think something is listening?" Avery asked.

"I think Malrec built a door and died before he learned what answered from the other side." Orin looked at the frame. "I think people from a clean room opened their own version of it and named harm until it sounded like work. I think Rook is dead in a corridor made sick by both mistakes. If listening is the worst thing this place does, I will take the bargain and complain about it later."

Eli's hand moved toward the flask and stopped before touching it.

Avery noticed.

He noticed everything. That was one of his more irritating moral qualities.

"Does it hurt?" Avery asked.

Eli's mouth twitched. "You will need to be less specific."

Avery nodded toward Eli's belt. "The flask."

Eli looked down.

For one breath, Orin thought he might uncap it. She shifted her grip on the Frame Jack without meaning to.

Eli saw that too.

"Comforting," he said.

"Answer him," Orin said.

Eli's fingers curled away from the metal. "It feels full."

Avery frowned. "Of liquor?"

"Of me," Eli said.

The workshop did not move.

Orin wished it had.

Movement would have given her something to strike.

Eli took a slow breath through his nose. "That is not a useful answer."

"It may be the only honest one," Avery said.

"Then honesty is overrated."

"Often," Orin said.

That got Eli to look at her.

She did not give him comfort. Comfort was not always kind, and she had no skill left for decoration.

"You will not drink from it until we understand more," Orin said.

Eli smiled without showing teeth. "And if I fall apart without it?"

Orin lifted one shoulder. "Then I will tell you you are falling apart loudly enough for everyone to benefit from the knowledge."

Pebble huffed.

Avery looked down at the Wayhound. "That may have been agreement."

"It was criticism," Eli said.

Avery asked the obvious question. "Of whom?"

"Everyone available."

The exchange should not have helped.

It did.

Not much. Not enough to make the room merciful. Enough to prove the four living things in it had not yet been reduced to only what the frame had done to them.

Orin set the Frame Jack on Malrec's table.

The sound was small. It made all three of them look at her.

"This room cannot belong to one person again," she said.

Avery's attention sharpened.

Eli looked back toward the corridor instead.

Orin continued anyway. "Not to me. Not to Avery. Not to Eli. Not to a village that wants useful secrets. Not to the next clever lonely fool who decides a warning is an invitation written by a coward."

Avery's mouth tightened at Malrec's table.

He had liked Malrec's work. Orin could see that. Not the harm. Not the secrecy. The craft of it. Avery respected good joints, good measurements, and the terrible patience required to build something that should not work until it nearly did.

That respect made him dangerous in the right way.

"Records," Avery said.

Orin nodded once.

"Witnesses," Avery added.

Orin nodded once. "More than one."

Avery looked toward the dead frame. "Rules for who can handle the frame."

Orin added the next fence. "Rules for who can be in the room while someone handles the frame."

Eli laughed again, quieter. "Rules for who gets to say the room is interesting."

"No one," Orin said.

Eli looked back at her.

"No one opens it because the question is interesting," Orin said. "No one studies it alone. No one sells it. No one hides it and calls secrecy safety. No one keeps a private door."

Avery took a piece of chalk from the edge of Malrec's table.

He did not write yet.

"That is a beginning," Avery said.

"It is a fence," Orin answered. "Beginnings wander."

Avery turned the chalk once between his fingers. "Fences fail."

Orin looked at the frame. "So do doors."

Eli shifted to stand.

Pebble rose with him.

"Sit," Avery said.

"No."

The word was flat enough to become furniture in the room.

Avery turned fully. "You nearly came apart inside a threshold."

Eli answered without looking away from him. "Yes."

Avery did not soften. "Your leg is bad."

Eli's mouth tightened. "It was bad before."

Avery kept counting. "Your body has been altered by a damaged frame."

Eli gave him that one. "That one is new."

"You have a copied flask with an unknown working attached to your belt."

Eli's eyes flicked down and away.

Avery saw it and softened exactly not at all. "You are not going into the corridor first."

Eli stood anyway.

Badly.

The limp dragged through him on the first step, ugly and honest. His hand went out to the wall. Pebble pressed against his knee, bracing without letting Eli pretend it was affection.

"I am not staying here," Eli said.

Orin heard the rest of it.

Not with words.

I left him there.

I called wrong.

I lived.

She had no answer that would not insult all three facts.

"No," Orin said. "You are not staying here."

Avery looked at her.

"He walks in the middle," Orin said. "Pebble stays on him. You watch the leg and the flask. I watch the route."

"I can watch my own flask," Eli said.

"You have had poor results."

His face did something almost living.

"Cruel woman."

"Efficient one."

"Both can be true."

"Often," Avery said.

Eli looked betrayed. "I liked you better when you were only annoying."

"You have never liked me better," Avery answered.

"I was leaving room for growth."

Pebble leaned into Eli hard enough to make him catch himself again.

The flicker of speech ended there.

They all knew what waited beyond it.

Orin began gathering what they would need.

Salt first.

Not table salt. Not cooking salt. Anchor salt, gray and heavy, stored in a sealed jar from the terrace supply run. It caught in the wrinkles of her palm when she tested a pinch and made the skin feel too aware of itself. Good salt did that. It reminded flesh that boundaries were not suggestions.

Burn cloth next. Old field linen soaked in oil and dried flat, folded into packets. Practical. Ugly. Reliable.

Cord. Two lengths.

Pins. Wedges.

A pry hook.

A narrow shovel.

A spare cloak.

Avery added seal-wrap, needle cord, and a clean knife.

Orin looked at him.

"If we are wrong about what is left," Avery said, "I would rather be wrong with options."

She accepted that.

Eli took one of the salt jars.

Avery reached for it at once.

Eli moved the jar away.

The motion was too smooth.

All of them noticed.

Eli noticed last, which made it worse.

He looked at his own hand with open dislike.

"I can carry salt," he said.

Orin held out her hand.

For a moment, Eli did not move.

Then he gave her the jar.

Not because he agreed.

Because some part of him understood that refusing would make the flask win another argument it had not been invited to attend.

"You carry cord," Orin said.

Eli took it badly. "Generous."

Orin held the cord out. "Cord is useful."

Eli looked at the clean knife. "So are knives."

Orin did not move the knife closer. "Cord is useful for living people."

That one landed.

Eli took the cord.

Avery marked Malrec's table before they left.

Four short lines.

Not a full diagram. Not a map. A warning in a language the room had already proved it could misunderstand.

Boundary.

Witness.

Record.

Return.

Orin watched him place the marks along the center of the table, not at the edges. He drew a straight line through them after a moment, measuring by eye against the frame's dead center and the old chalk track on the floor.

"What is that?" Eli asked.

"A meridian," Avery said.

Orin looked at him.

Avery kept his eyes on the mark. "A fixed line for orientation. Something you use so other measurements stop pretending they are alone."

Eli's expression tightened. "Sounds optimistic."

Avery tapped the chalk line once. "It is practical."

"That is what optimistic people say when they own tools."

Avery ignored that. "If this room becomes anything, it needs a centerline everyone can check against. Not a throne. Not a secret. A mark that says all work is measured from the same place."

Orin let the word settle.

Meridian.

It had a clean shape in the mouth. Not soft. Not grand. A line on a map. A way of saying here is where we measure from, and no one gets to move it quietly.

Malrec had hidden his center until it killed other people.

Project Chimera had hidden its center behind clean language and machines Orin would never see.

This could not be that.

"Meridian," Orin said.

Eli looked between them. "We are naming the fence now?"

"We are naming the rule," Orin said.

"That is worse."

Orin accepted that. "Yes."

Avery put the chalk down. "Meridian Group, if it grows beyond the room."

Eli blinked.

Orin almost smiled.

"Group," Eli said.

"People behave better when the name reminds them there are supposed to be more than one of them," Avery said.

Eli gave him a tired look. "Have you met people?"

Avery's answer was dry. "Unfortunately."

The old room held the name without reacting.

That did not make Orin trust it.

It made her decide to use it before fear could turn it ceremonial.

"Meridian Group," she said. "For now that means four fools, one damaged room, and rules written before anyone sleeps."

Eli looked toward the corridor. "Three living fools."

No one corrected him quickly enough.

He looked back at them, and the anger in his face was not a flame. It was a field after fire had passed, still hot under ash.

"Rook does not get counted as labor," Eli said.

Orin took the salt jar.

"No," she said. "He gets counted as the reason we do it properly."

That silenced him.

Not peacefully.

But silence had uses if no one mistook it for healing.

They left the workshop in the order Orin had chosen.

Orin first, because she knew how dead corridors lied.

Eli next, because if he moved faster than her she would trip him with the cord herself.

Pebble pressed against Eli's left side.

Avery came last with the burn cloth and the clean knife, because Avery trusted rear guards less than he trusted geometry and therefore watched both.

The corridor outside Malrec's workshop had changed while they were not looking.

Not much.

That was how it wanted to be underestimated.

The seam-light along the ceiling had thinned. The old brace slots looked darker, like missing teeth in stone. Dust had settled unevenly across the floor, caught in places where the route had breathed and left no wind to confess it.

Orin stopped at the first bend.

Eli nearly walked into her and caught himself on Pebble instead.

"What?" Eli asked.

Orin held up one hand. "Listen."

He did.

That was something the day had not taken from him. Eli could be reckless, furious, half undone, and still listen when the route made itself worth hearing.

The corridor held its breath.

No scrape.

No animal.

No wet drag.

No distant settling stone.

Only the faint high pressure left from the failed frame behind them and the soft sound of Eli trying not to make pain audible.

"Too quiet," Avery said from the rear.

"Yes," Orin answered.

Pebble's fur lifted along his spine.

Eli's hand went toward the flask.

"No," Avery said.

Eli's hand stopped.

Eli kept his hand still. "I wasn't going to drink."

Avery's eyes stayed on the flask. "You were going to touch it."

Eli looked offended by the precision. "That is a different crime."

Avery did not blink. "Today it may not be."

Eli gave him a look that would have been sharper if pain had not dulled the edge.

Orin moved again.

They found the first blood before they found the broken column.

It had dried darker than it should have, drawn into the cracks between fitted stones in thin branching lines. Orin crouched and held one hand above it without touching.

Rook's blood.

Kaden's too, maybe, from the fall and the struggle.

Eli made a sound behind her that did not become a word.

"Breathe," Avery said.

Eli breathed through his teeth. "I am."

Avery waited. "Better."

Eli gave him a flat look. "Greedy."

Orin pinched salt between two fingers and let it fall across the blood.

The grains struck stone and stayed grains.

No hiss.

No blackening.

No immediate wrongness.

That should have helped.

It did not.

They moved on.

The broken brace-column came into view by degrees: first the torn edge of the left wall, then the metal spar angled across the floor, then the column itself with its side split where Rook had struck it.

The impact mark remained.

The blood remained.

The body did not.

Eli stepped past Orin.

She caught him by the back of his coat.

He stopped because the cloth stopped him, not because sense had returned.

"No," Orin said.

Eli's voice came out thin. "Move your hand."

"No."

"Orin."

He did not call her Sister.

That told her more than shouting would have.

Avery moved up beside them, careful and pale.

Pebble went low.

Not crouched.

Low, as if gravity had become a threat he meant to bite.

Orin released Eli's coat only when Avery had one hand ready at the boy's shoulder.

Eli lurched forward two steps and stopped at the edge of the blood.

There was no body.

No coat folded over a dead chest.

No hand slack on stone.

No familiar shape made unfamiliar by stillness.

No Rook.

For one breath, the absence was so complete that Orin's mind refused it as a measurement.

Then training arrived.

Training was cruel enough to be useful.

"Do not step in the blood," Orin said.

Eli laughed.

It was the worst sound he had made all day.

"Wouldn't want to disturb the scene."

Orin kept her hand on his coat. "Correct."

He turned on her.

The anger in his face was easier to bear than the emptiness under it.

Eli stared at the empty place. "He's gone."

Orin made herself answer. "Yes."

Eli's voice sharpened. "So I think the scene has been disturbed."

Orin did not release him. "Not by us."

That landed where she meant it to.

Avery stepped around the outer edge, eyes moving. "There should be a drag trail."

Orin nodded.

"There isn't," Avery said.

Orin kept her eyes on the blood. "Check anyway."

Avery was already lowering himself. "I am."

He went down on one knee near the place Rook's shoulder had been. He did not touch the central blood. He checked the dust beyond it, the grit line beside the broken spar, the place where a body taken by hands would have smeared weight through drying blood.

Nothing honest answered.

Eli stood too still.

Pebble sniffed the ground once and jerked back like the stone had lied directly into his nose.

The Wayhound barked.

Short.

Furious.

Then he barked at the wall.

Not the broken left wall that had killed Rook.

The right wall.

The wall Eli had called wrong.

Eli flinched so hard Orin saw it in his bad leg.

"Pebble," Avery said.

Pebble ignored him and barked again at the right wall.

Orin crossed to it.

There was no opening. No seam wide enough for fingers. No fresh break in the mortar. Only old worked stone and a faint pressure against her teeth.

She laid two fingers on the surface.

Cold.

Not stone cold.

Deep-route cold.

"It wants us looking where we looked before," Orin said.

Eli's face drained.

"Don't," Avery said.

Eli looked at him. "I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"Convenient talent."

"Not convenient. Repetitive."

Orin kept her fingers against the stone. "The body was not dragged through here."

Avery stood. "No."

Orin asked the next necessary question. "Animal?"

"No." Avery's answer came at once. "No tooth marks in the blood edge. No track pattern. No disturbed dust consistent with feeding or carrying. Nothing large enough came through this corridor unless it learned to apologize to the floor."

Eli looked down at Pebble.

Pebble growled.

"He says no too," Eli said.

Orin looked at the impact site.

Rook had died there. That remained true. The blood was too much for a living escape. The wound had been too large. The last breath had gone out under Kaden's hand. Orin had not seen it, but she had heard enough through the broken relay and seen enough afterward in the people who returned without him.

Rook had died.

The route had not left the dead alone.

"Search the edges," Orin said.

Avery looked at her.

She heard the question he did not ask.

How much hope do we allow?

"Search for theft," Orin said. "Not survival."

Eli closed his eyes.

Only for a moment.

Then he opened them and became useful in a way that frightened her.

"Left shelf is gone," he said.

Avery's gaze moved to him.

Eli pointed without touching the wall. "Not that left. Under it. There was an undercut below the brace line when we ran through. Pebble, don't stand there."

Pebble moved before Avery could check.

The stone where the Wayhound had been shifted by a finger's width and settled again.

Orin did not praise Eli.

Praise would have broken something.

"Good," she said instead.

He gave her a look.

"You said no one takes route calls from me alone."

Orin nodded toward the Wayhound. "Pebble moved."

Eli's mouth pulled tight. "So my standard is dog agreement."

Orin let that stand as mercy. "Today that may be aspirational."

Avery made a sound that might have been a laugh if the corridor had been less full of blood.

They searched.

Not long.

Long enough to remove decency from hope.

Orin checked the left break with salt and the pry hook. The broken spar had not been lifted after the strike. Dust lay in the underside grooves unbroken. No body had been pulled beneath it.

Avery checked the impact column and the floor beyond it. He found one torn thread from Rook's sleeve caught on a jagged place in the stone. He removed it with the clean knife and wrapped it in a scrap of field cloth because none of them could bear to leave the only piece that had agreed to remain.

Eli checked nothing at first.

He stood at the edge of the blood with cord in one hand and the wrong flask at his belt, looking like a man trying to decide whether the world deserved more damage than he could currently provide.

Then Pebble nudged him.

Eli looked down.

Pebble nudged him again, harder, toward the far wall.

"Fine," Eli said. "Be strange with purpose."

He limped after the Wayhound.

The limp worsened as he moved. Orin saw it. Avery saw it. Eli pretended the wall had become fascinating enough to justify the way his breath caught.

Pebble stopped near a smear too high to be from dragging.

Blood on the wall.

Three fingers' width.

Not a handprint.

Not a splash.

More like the corridor had remembered where a hand should have touched and supplied the evidence afterward.

Eli stared at it.

"That wasn't there," he said.

Avery looked over. "When?"

Eli kept looking at the mark. "When we left."

Avery's expression stayed practical. "You were not in a condition to inventory the wall."

Eli's head turned.

Avery held his ground. "That is not an insult. It is a fact."

"It is both."

"Fine."

Orin crossed to the mark.

She salted below it.

The salt jumped.

Not scattered.

Jumped.

Every grain lifted the height of a breath and dropped again in a line too straight to be chance.

Meridian, Orin thought, and hated that the word came now.

A centerline.

A measurement.

Something had made a line out of salt without touching it.

Pebble backed into Eli's leg and snarled.

Eli's hand went to the flask.

This time his fingers closed around it.

"Eli," Avery said.

"I know."

He did not let go.

"Eli."

"I said I know."

His voice shook with pain, not temptation. Orin could hear the difference. The flask was not calling to thirst. It was answering the corridor.

That was worse.

"Take your hand off it," Orin said.

Eli swallowed.

For a moment, he could not.

Then Pebble turned and pressed his head under Eli's wrist.

The contact broke something small and necessary.

Eli's fingers opened.

The flask swung once against his belt and went still.

"I hate this," Eli said.

No one insulted the sentence by agreeing.

Orin studied the salt line.

It pointed neither to the workshop nor deeper into the corridor.

It pointed into the wall.

Not an opening.

A direction.

"He was not carried," Avery said quietly.

Orin nodded.

"He was not eaten."

Orin shook her head once. "No."

Eli's mouth twisted. "Excellent. We have ruled out the comforting explanations."

"Those were not comforting explanations," Avery said.

"They were explanations."

That was true enough to hurt.

Orin turned back toward the blood.

The burn cloth remained folded in Avery's pack. The salt jar remained stoppered. The spare cloak remained useless.

They had come prepared to do mercy work.

The Continuum had denied them even that.

"Rook died here," Orin said.

Eli's eyes snapped to her.

She made herself continue.

"Rook died here. We do not soften that because his body is gone."

Eli's face worked once.

Avery looked down.

Pebble stopped growling.

"But the route did not leave him where death should have ended," Orin said.

The corridor seemed to listen.

Orin let it.

Let every damaged stone hear the shape of the decision.

"That is not a miracle," she said. "It is a breach."

Eli looked at the blood. "Can we follow it?"

"Not today."

"That was not my question."

"Yes," Orin said. "It was."

He looked at her then, and for the first time since the portal took the strangers back, he looked young in a way she wished she had not seen.

"We don't leave him," Eli said.

"We already had to," Avery answered.

Eli flinched.

Avery did too, afterward, because the truth had cut both directions.

Orin stepped between them before either man could decide pain needed a target.

"We leave the blood salted," she said. "We mark the site. We close the corridor as far as it will let us. We go back to the room and write the first Meridian rule in a hand large enough for fear to read."

Eli's voice was scraped thin. "Which rule?"

Orin looked at the wall where the salt had jumped into a line.

"The dead are not materials."

Avery lifted his head.

Pebble gave one low sound.

Eli looked at the empty place where Rook should have been.

For a while, no one moved.

Then Orin opened the salt jar.

She poured a ring around the blood first. Not a burial. Not a ward strong enough to stop what had already happened. A statement of boundary. A refusal to let the corridor pretend the site was unclaimed.

Avery set the torn sleeve thread inside a folded scrap of cloth and tucked it away.

Eli tied cord across the broken approach with hands that only shook when he thought no one could see. Pebble stayed beside him and watched the right wall with murder in every line of his body.

Orin marked the column last.

Not with Malrec's symbols.

Not with Continuist shorthand.

With four plain cuts, shallow enough not to weaken the stone and deep enough not to be rubbed away by an ordinary sleeve.

Rook died here.

Body taken.

Do not open.

Report to Meridian.

Eli read the last line.

"That was fast," Eli said.

Orin answered without ceremony. "Yes."

Eli looked at the marked column. "We are four fools and a dead room."

"Three fools," Avery said quietly.

Eli's jaw tightened.

Avery did not take the correction back.

Orin put the knife away.

"Then three fools had better become enough people to make a group," she said.

The corridor gave them no answer.

That was not comfort.

It was permission to leave before it changed its mind.

They walked back slower than they had come.

Orin did not look over her shoulder until the last bend.

The salt line remained.

The blood remained.

The body did not.

In her chest, grief tried to become a shape too large for work.

Orin cut it down to the size of the next task.

Find a village willing to stand near danger without worshiping it.

Write rules.

Post guards.

Move Malrec's work without letting Malrec's mistakes travel with it.

Watch Eli.

Watch the flask.

Watch the places where the dead did not stay dead enough.

At the workshop door, Avery stopped.

"Orin," he said.

She did not correct the missing title.

Orin stopped beside him. "Yes."

"If this is Meridian's first record, what do we write?"

Orin looked past him into the workshop. The dead frame waited in its socket. The dark Core sat in the center. Malrec's table held the chalk line Avery had drawn, straight and pale as a promise that did not yet know how expensive it would become.

Eli leaned against the wall with Pebble at his side, one hand hovering near the wrong flask and not touching it.

Three fools.

One missing dead man.

A room full of reasons secrecy had failed.

Orin stepped inside.

"Write this," she said.

Avery took up the chalk.

Orin kept her eyes on the frame while she spoke.

"Rook is dead. His body is gone. The route took or moved what should have been burned. Until we know why, no one works alone, no one opens a door, and no dead thing touched by this crossing is to be treated as finished."

Avery wrote.

Eli said nothing.

Pebble watched the corridor.

When Avery finished, the chalk line down the center of Malrec's table looked less like a measurement and more like a wound someone had chosen to keep clean.

Orin set the salt jar beside it.

"First Meridian record," she said.

No one argued with the name this time.

Outside the workshop, the corridor held its quiet.

Not empty.

Never empty.

Only waiting, with Rook's blood drying where his body should have been.
