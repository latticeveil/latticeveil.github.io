# FRAME 07
# AFTERIMAGE

Sarah did not lower the tablet.

Not because it could help her. Because her hands had not yet agreed that they were empty.

The man across the chamber waited where he was, balanced and watchful in the pale seam-light. Up close, the resemblance held only in pieces. The shape of the face. The mouth when still. The dark concentration in the eyes before speech changed it into something else. He was not her husband. The certainty had already landed. Her body had simply taken longer than her mind to obey it.

The device in her hand still read:

Avery.
Exact.

The stranger glanced at the screen, then back at her. "You know me?"

"No," Sarah said.

It came out too quickly to sound convincing.

His expression shifted by a fraction. Not offense. Assessment.

"You said the name like you did."

Sarah forced air into her lungs and tried again. "I know someone with your face badly enough to have made a mistake for one second."

That answer seemed to satisfy him more than the first. Not because it was good. Because it was honest.

He took one careful step nearer, enough for light to catch the edge of a survey brace at his hip and the chalk dust ground into one sleeve. "Avery," he said. "Just Avery."

Sarah almost laughed at the cruelty of that.

"Dr. Sarah Chen," she said. "And yes. I know."

He watched her over the tablet. "You thought I was someone else."

"My husband."

Avery absorbed that without visible discomfort, which made him immediately unlike the men she had expected to meet in this world and far too much like the one she had left behind.

"You don't think that now," he said.

"No."

The tablet buzzed once against her palm as if objecting.

Sarah looked down at it, then held the display out a little so he could see without needing to touch it. "This is the problem."

He studied the screen, not understanding the interface but understanding enough to recognize certainty when it was rendered in a machine.

"Our tracking systems don't identify people by clothing or movement," she said. "Not even by voice. They go deeper than that. Tissue. chemistry. identity markers that are supposed to belong to one body and one body only."

His eyes moved from the screen to her face.

Sarah heard the thinness in her own voice and pressed through it anyway. "It isn't confusing you with him because you look alike. It's reading you as if you and my Avery share the same underlying answer. As if this place has... another version of us written into it."

The chamber went very still.

Not because anything changed in the stone. Because the sentence had settled between them and refused to pretend it was smaller than it was.

Avery looked past her for a moment, toward the dark route she had taken in. "We've seen places where the Continuum repeats a choice until it becomes a habit," he said. "And thin places where one path lays over another badly. But a person..." He shook his head once. "If that's true, I don't know the whole of it."

Sarah let out a breath she had not realized she was holding.

The admission did more for her trust than certainty would have.

Static burst in her ear.

"Sarah?"

Kaden's voice arrived torn almost flat by interference, but present.

She touched the comm at once. "Still here."

The relief on the other side was brief and tightly managed. "Your signal did something ugly. I lost you for twelve seconds."

"I found the reason," Sarah said, eyes still on Avery. "And it isn't my Avery."

Silence.

Then Kaden, dry even through static: "That is specific in exactly the wrong direction."

For the first time since stepping into the chamber, Sarah felt the edge of a laugh.

"I'm with someone local," she said. "His name is Avery."

That bought her another beat of silence.

"Of course it is," Kaden said.

Avery looked faintly offended by the idea that his name might be a tactical inconvenience.

"Can you still see the others?" Sarah asked.

"In pieces," Kaden said. "Rook's still east. Elijah is moving. Dr. Avery Chen is still alive, which I realize is not the same thing as useful. Eli went north and then stopped being cooperative."

Avery's attention sharpened at that. "Stopped how?"

Sarah relayed the line.

He frowned toward the corridor behind him. "That likely means north stopped deserving him."

Sarah looked from him to the chamber exit. "Can you get me to the portal?"

"Probably," he said.

The answer was too measured to be comforting, which made it trustworthy.

He took another step, slower this time, giving her room to refuse. "If we're moving together, put that thing away before you let it convince you I'm your husband again."

Sarah slipped the tablet down, not because she liked being told to, but because he was right.

"I only made the mistake once," she said.

Avery glanced at her as he turned toward the route. "Then we can both try not to improve on it."

She followed him out of the chamber.

*

Eli went north for eleven minutes before deciding the corridor was lying to him.

It had all the right pieces. Older survey cuts at shoulder height. Brace slots in the walls where a route crew had once worked carefully and with enough confidence to leave the clean lines visible. A dry mineral smell instead of active seam-burn. Nothing obviously wrong.

That was the problem.

The passage felt archived.

Not dead. Worse. Finished. A route with its decision already behind it.

Eli stopped beneath a low beam and listened to the rock.

Nothing.

Not silence exactly. The Continuum was never generous enough for real silence. But no pull. No live correction in the seams. No pressure at the back of the teeth saying left, idiot, unless you enjoy becoming an example.

He hated corridors that behaved too well. They always turned out to be proud of it.

His hand found the flask in the outer pocket of his coat without looking. The metal was cool against his palm, worn smooth where his thumb always landed. He unscrewed it, took a short pull, and let the burn settle him back into proportions he trusted.

"North is excellent," he muttered to the empty passage. "If your goal is disappointing me in a structurally sound way."

He capped the flask again and stood there long enough to admit the second problem.

He was thinking about Rook.

Not in the abstract sense of keeping track of a stranger who had arrived where no stranger should have been. In the practical sense. The route had bent around the man. Not obeyed him. Not welcomed him. Bent. Like stone trying to remember a weight it had once carried and resenting how close he came to fitting.

Eli had seen enough unstable places to know the difference between danger and a live thread.

North had danger.

Rook had thread.

And beneath that, another thing Eli did not yet have a clean word for: interest sharpened by familiarity he had not earned. Every time Rook looked at a mark before understanding why it mattered, something in Eli's spine answered as if the route had tapped the wrong surveyor and gotten close enough anyway.

He glanced back the way he had come, toward the portal and the sensible plan he was now in the process of insulting.

"Avery can be right at me later," he said.

The eastward pull was faint, but it existed. Enough.

Eli turned off the north path and started cutting across broken side-work toward the route Rook had taken, following not footprints but disturbance: a fresh edge in seam-dust, a support ticked wrong by recent contact, one place where the wall seemed to hold onto the shape of uncertainty a second longer than it should have.

The flask tapped once against his ribs as he climbed over a collapsed brace.

Comforting.

Annoying.

Both usually meant the same thing.

*

Rook heard the scrape of a boot behind him and moved on reflex, half-turning with one hand already on the wall for balance.

"If this is another memory," he said into the dim seam-light, "I would prefer one with less walking."

"Disappointing start, then."

Eli stepped out from behind a fractured support frame, one hand lifted in a vague gesture of peace or apology. With him, both seemed likely.

Rook stared. "Weren't you north?"

"North turned out to be tedious."

Eli came farther into the chamber and looked around at the chalk stubs, broken shelves, and disturbed dust as if confirming something for himself. His gaze landed on the fresh prints near the frame, then on Rook, then stayed there.

"You changed routes," Rook said.

"I improved routes," Eli said. "There's a difference."

Rook did not move from where he stood. "Why?"

Eli shrugged one shoulder. "Because the corridor I was on had already made up its mind, and yours hadn't. Because Kaden said Elijah was trending toward your region. Because you looked like the sort of man who would either find something useful or die in a way that became everyone's paperwork."

That almost got a laugh out of Rook.

"And," Eli added, more quietly, "I wanted to see what the route was doing around you."

There it was. No performance on it. Just fact.

Rook let his hand fall away from the wall. "Found an answer?"

"Not a complete one." Eli glanced at the cut mark Rook had been staring at before the interruption. "But I think you're the real thread."

The words landed harder than Rook expected.

"That is either flattering or terrible," he said.

"Probably both."

Eli moved to the far side of the chamber and crouched beside the repaired support frame. He touched one finger to the urgent later work, not the careful older joinery. "This was patched by someone who knew the place would move before he could finish being careful." He looked up. "That feel familiar?"

Rook shut his eyes for half a beat.

Lamplight.
A rough breath.
Someone saying, not unkindly, You can rewrite the braces later if you survive now.

When he opened his eyes again, Eli was still watching.

"Yes," Rook said.

Eli stood. "Then let's stop wasting the one useful thing that's happened since the split."

They left the chamber together.

The corridor beyond narrowed quickly, then forked under a sagging seam where pale light kept thinning and recovering as if it could not decide whether to exist. Rook felt the old pull immediately, a pressure low behind the eyes and under the breastbone both.

"Left," he said.

"Why?"

"No idea."

Eli nodded once. "Good enough."

He took the left branch first, but only three steps. Then he stopped so suddenly Rook nearly ran into him.

"No," Eli said softly.

Rook looked past him. The corridor ahead appeared ordinary. Wet stone. Old brace cut. Mineral drift in the corners.

"What?"

Eli tilted his head, listening to something Rook could not. "The wall's too pleased with itself."

"That is not a measurable criterion," Rook said.

"And yet," Eli said.

Rook stared at the stone. A fragment nudged loose in him.

Never where the wall wants you.

"Right branch," he said at once.

Eli looked back over one shoulder. "You get a flash?"

"A warning."

"Excellent. I also had a warning. See? Teamwork."

They took the right path together. Five paces in, the left corridor folded inward behind them with a clipped grinding sound, neat as a trap closing in a story told by someone with no respect for ankles.

Rook exhaled sharply. "You live like this?"

Eli did not turn around. "Not by preference."

The route sloped down, then widened enough that they could walk side by side if they were willing to pretend the Continuum believed in hospitality.

For a few minutes neither spoke.

Rook was the one who broke first. "Avery is going to be furious with you."

"Yes," Eli said.

"And you're still here."

Eli glanced at him. "You say that like I don't know him."

Rook let the corner of his mouth lift once. "Fair."

The flask knocked lightly against Eli's coat when he ducked under a fractured brace. Rook noticed the sound and then, a second later, the movement of Eli's hand checking that it was still there.

Eli noticed him noticing.

"Medicinal," he said.

"Against what?"

"The possibility of hearing one more person tell me to be sensible."

Rook looked ahead again. "Does it work?"

Eli considered that. "Not especially."

It was the driest thing he had said all day. Rook liked him a little more for it.

Static burst through both their comms at once.

"Rook."

Kaden's voice, thin and failing.

Rook hit the receiver on instinct. "Still here."

"Eli too?" Kaden asked.

Eli blinked once, surprised enough to show it. "Am now."

"Good," Kaden said, though he sounded as if goodness had become expensive. "Sarah found local Avery. Not her husband. Repeat, not her husband."

Eli let out a short breath through his nose. "That sentence should not make sense, but somehow it does."

Rook's attention sharpened. "Is she stable?"

"As a concept? Hard to say," Kaden said. "As a person, yes."

Even through the distortion, Rook could hear the strain under the line. It put a hard edge in him almost at once.

"Kaden," he said, quieter, "how much is this costing you now?"

There was a pause.

"We are not doing that in front of company," Kaden said.

Eli's eyes flicked from the corridor to Rook.

Rook did not look back. "That's not an answer."

"It wasn't meant to be." Another break of static. Then Kaden again, flatter. "Elijah's signal is still moving into your sector. The route around you keeps shearing and then correcting. If either of you starts seeing repeats, trust the oldest version first."

Rook filed the warning away and still did not let the earlier thing go. "You sound worse."

"You've said that before," Kaden said.

"You lied before," Rook said.

Silence.

Then, quiet enough that Eli should not have been able to hear it and probably did anyway: "Practice helps."

The channel broke.

For a few steps the only sound was boots on old stone.

Eli kept his eyes ahead. "That sounded private."

"It is," Rook said.

"All right," Eli said.

Rook glanced over. "You're not going to ask?"

"I just did," Eli said.

Rook almost smiled despite himself. "That's a terrible technicality."

"It's an excellent one." Eli stepped over a crack where seam-light pooled too cleanly. "If you want to tell me later, you can. If not, the corridor will invent something ruder than the truth and I'll work with that instead."

Rook looked at him for a moment too long.

There was an ease in Eli that did not come from innocence. It came from surviving a world that punished people for demanding clarity before it existed.

"Something was done to him," Rook said at last.

Eli's face changed, not with surprise but with a more focused kind of attention.

"By your people?"

Rook nodded once.

"Do the others know?"

"Not the whole of it," Rook said.

Eli let that sit without pressing. "And you do."

"Enough to know I should have stopped more than I did," Rook said.

That was apparently the amount of confession the corridor would get from him.

Eli accepted it like a field condition. "Then when we get out of here," he said, "you can decide whether guilt is a useful hobby. For now I'd prefer you keep doing whatever it is that lets dead routes talk to you."

The bluntness of it hit Rook somewhere unexpectedly human. He looked away before Eli could see too much of that.

They walked on.

The next chamber had once been a crossing node. Three corridor mouths. A collapsed marker stand. Shallow grooves in the floor where crates or frames had been dragged long enough to teach the stone repetition.

Rook stopped at the threshold.

Again, the pressure.

Again, a route he should not know arriving as if memory and instinct had signed each other's names.

"Middle," he said.

Eli did not move right away. He was watching the dust.

"No," he said finally. "Middle's what the place wants us to think you'll choose."

Rook frowned. "I can feel it."

"Yes." Eli crouched and brushed two fingers over the floor near the left passage. Fresh disturbance. Not obvious. A heel edge. A drag where someone had corrected balance. "And someone lighter than you took this one recently."

Rook's chest tightened.

Not a boot print.
Too narrow.
Too deliberate.

Elijah.

Or the route pretending to be helpful in precisely the way that got people killed.

Then another fragment surfaced, sharper than the rest.

Warm fur brushing a hand.
A low impatient huff.
Move. This way.

Rook looked sharply toward the left corridor.

"What?" Eli asked.

Rook shook his head once, already moving. "I think he isn't alone."

Eli was beside him in a breath. "Now that is a sentence worth following."

*

Sarah had expected local Avery to move like her husband.

She stopped expecting it after the third turn.

Dr. Avery Chen navigated hard spaces the way engineers negotiated failure: by reducing variables, forcing sequence, and carrying purpose visibly in the body. This Avery moved with attention distributed outward, as if he were not solving the corridor so much as staying inside a conversation it had started before she arrived.

It was not mystical. Sarah distrusted that word on principle.

It was practiced.

He paused at a narrow break where the stone pinched close and touched the wall with the backs of his fingers.

"Active?" Sarah asked.

"Thinking about it," he said.

"That's a horrible answer," Sarah said.

"It's also the accurate one," Avery said.

He waited another two breaths, then continued. Sarah followed through and felt the delayed shiver in the seam a second after they passed, like a trap regretting missed timing.

"You do this often?" she asked.

"Survive routes?" Avery said. "Often enough."

Sarah looked down at the tracker again despite herself. She had narrowed the display to raw presence data, no names attached. It helped less than she had hoped. The signal that should have been her husband still threaded dangerously close to the man walking in front of her, and Rook's presence kept feathering at the edges as if the system couldn't hold him in one outline.

Kaden, by contrast, remained one hard point and nothing else.

Too clean.
Too wrong.

"You have someone here who looks like me," Sarah said.

Avery did not turn around. "Probably."

"That was evasive," Sarah said.

"That was cautious," Avery said.

He stepped over a cracked brace and finally glanced back at her. "You have Sister Orin's habit of asking dangerous questions while pretending you've already decided the answer."

Sarah stared.

"I don't know whether that was an insult," Sarah said.

"Neither do I," Avery said.

She should have resented the comparison. Instead some strange cold recognition moved through her, not emotional, not quite visual either. A composure she knew from the inside reflected badly in another life. Not sameness. Not even comfort. Just a structural echo that made the world feel newly crowded.

Sarah looked back to the tablet. "The machine thinks your people and mine share identity below the surface. Not resemblance. Something deeper."

"And you think that means counterparts," Avery said.

"I think it means the idea is no longer stupid enough to dismiss," Sarah said.

Avery considered that for several steps.

"Rook's signal behaves wrong?" he asked.

Sarah frowned. "How do you know his name?"

"Eli used it enough near the portal to become a problem."

"Fine. Yes. Rook doubles. Not constantly. But when the interference clears, he doesn't register like one body." She hesitated. "Kaden does the opposite. One signal. Singular to the point of absurdity."

Avery's expression tightened, though not with recognition. With work.

"The doubled one," he said slowly, "feels like a route trying to place a man where someone else already was."

Sarah stared at him. "You can tell that from a sentence?"

"No," Avery said. "From several years of bad places and one sentence."

She almost pushed. Didn't. He was still building the thought as he spoke it.

"And Kaden?" she asked.

Avery was quiet too long.

"I don't know yet," he said at last. "Only that singular isn't the same as simple. From what you're describing, the route doesn't fail to find his counterpart. It fails to decide where all of him is."

That was not an answer either.

But it was honest in the same way his earlier admission had been honest. A line drawn where knowledge ended instead of a bluff painted over it.

The comm crackled suddenly.

"Sarah," Kaden said. "Voice check."

"Here."

"Avery with you?"

She looked at the man beside her and felt the old jolt of wrongness all over again.

"One of them," she said.

Kaden made a sound that might have been exhausted approval. "Eli crossed onto Rook's route."

Avery stopped. "He did what?"

Sarah repeated it.

Something like relief passed quickly through Avery's face.

"Good," he said.

"Good?" Sarah said. "You objected to him going alone at all."

"Yes." Avery resumed walking, faster now. "And if he abandoned north on purpose, then he finally noticed what the route was actually doing."

"Which is?"

Avery looked into the dark ahead. "Following Rook first."

*

By the time Eli and Rook found the chalk line, neither of them argued with it.

It sat low on the left wall just past a slant of broken stone, a fresh mark dragged by a tired hand that had still cared enough to be deliberate. Next to it, half lost in the dust, ran a set of narrow animal prints and one faint smear where a tail or flank had brushed the mineral drift.

Eli crouched first.

"Not local vermin," he said. "Too clean-footed."

Rook felt the fragment before he understood it.

Warmth.
Movement ahead.
An impatient living shape pausing long enough to make sure it was being followed.

"Elijah found something," he said.

Eli glanced up. "Or something found him."

There was no fear in the line. Only calculation.

Rook looked deeper into the corridor. The seam-light there felt thinner, stretched over distance that wanted to behave strangely but had not committed yet. A live route. A dangerous one. The kind that punished hesitation more efficiently than bad judgment.

Eli rose and unscrewed his flask.

Rook gave him a look.

"What?"

"We may be close."

"Exactly." Eli took one measured swallow, capped the flask again, and slid it back into place. "I'd like to meet your missing analyst while shaped correctly."

Rook should not have found that reassuring.

He did.

They moved on through the narrowing passage, not bothering anymore with the fiction that one of them was leading and the other following. The route wanted different things from each of them. Eli heard where the seams were becoming impatient. Rook knew which turn would matter before the evidence caught up. Between those failures, something almost efficient emerged.

At the next break, the corridor offered three shadows.

Rook opened his mouth.

Eli cut him off. "Middle if we're being watched. Right if we're being tested. Left if your analyst has found anything even slightly friendly."

Rook blinked. "How did you get all of that that fast?"

Eli's mouth twitched. "Practice helps."

The echo of Kaden's line landed between them for half a second and did not entirely hurt.

Rook looked left.

From somewhere far enough ahead to be uncertain and near enough to matter, something gave a single soft bark.

Not monstrous.
Not human.
Just alive.

Eli went still. Then, with unmistakable satisfaction: "There."

Rook felt it too now. Not just Elijah's direction. Not just the pull of old route-memory. Something simple and immediate threaded through both, turning convergence into fact.

He looked at Eli. "You were right."

Eli started down the left passage. "About which part?"

Rook followed at once. "That I was the real thread."

Eli did not look back, but Rook could hear the grin in his voice.

"Try not to get sentimental about it," he said. "We're only using you as bait for something useful."

Ahead, the corridor bent once and the seam-light shifted.

Somewhere beyond it, Elijah was close.
