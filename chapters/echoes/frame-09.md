# FRAME 09
# COST OF RETURN

Sarah learned quickly that a signal was not the same thing as a route.

A signal could tell you where a person might still be.

A route was the harder question of whether the world intended to let you reach him before that stopped being true.

The tablet did not like what Avery had done to it.

That was fair.

The narrow survey brace clipped to its side kept forcing the display into a shape it had never been built to admit. Every few seconds the screen shivered sideways, tearing into static, then snapped back with one thin pulse of direction before losing nerve again. West-southwest. Lower. Then nowhere. Then back. Honest only in flashes.

Sarah kept moving anyway.

Avery was beside her, one hand on the wall, the other steadying the jury-rigged mess in her grip whenever the corridor pitched hard enough to make the afterimage useless. Behind them came the sounds of the rest of the group reorganizing itself around injury, fatigue, and the fresh particular terror of having run out of ways to talk to Kaden.

"Again," Sarah said.

The screen spat white noise. Then a hard vertical spike.

"There."

Avery nodded once. "Lower shelf."

The route narrowed, bent, and dropped. Not down cleanly. Down with old steps broken out of it, supports split by earlier strain, chalk marks half-erased under later dust. Sarah caught the signs of route use without needing anyone to explain them now. Somebody had lived through this place often enough to argue with it in shorthand.

Below them, something pale moved once in the seam-light and vanished.

Not a person.

Pebble.

He had gone ahead without being asked.

"Good," Eli said somewhere behind her, low enough that the word might have been for himself.

Sarah glanced back.

He looked wrong already.

Not dramatically. Not in a way that would have let an ignorant person point and say breakdown with confidence. Worse than that. His face had gone too still around the strain in it. One hand kept flexing open and shut as if checking whether the fingers still belonged to him. There was dust and dried blood on his sleeve from Elijah's rescue, and an absence at his belt she had not registered until that moment precisely because Eli had carried it so naturally before.

No flask.

The recognition hit Avery at nearly the same instant.

He stopped so sharply that Rook nearly walked into him from behind.

"Where is it?" Avery asked.

Eli blinked. "What?"

"Your flask."

Silence fell badly.

Eli's hand went to his hip by instinct and found nothing there. Sarah watched the answer move through him in stages. Habit. Search. Failure. Memory catching up too late.

"I had it," he said.

Rook looked at him once, quick and narrowed. "Before the Braceback?"

Eli shook his head, then stopped halfway through the motion as if the corridor had tilted on him. "During. Before. I don't-" He shut his eyes for one beat. "I lost it."

Elijah, propped awkwardly between Dr. Avery and the wall where they had paused to let Sarah chase the signal, made a small sound that was not quite speech.

Avery did not soften.

"You need it," he said.

It was not accusation.

That almost made it harder.

Eli looked at him with tired fury and no energy to weaponize it. "Yes," he said. "I noticed the part where it's gone."

"No," Avery said. "You noticed the object is gone. I am saying the rest of us need to notice what that means."

Sarah saw Rook's expression change by fractions.

Not fear.

Recognition.

He already knew.

Avery looked past Eli to the others with the blunt professionalism of a man reporting structural failure.

"Without it he goes wrong," he said. "Not evil. Not useless. Wrong. His head stops agreeing with what is there."

Eli's mouth pulled tight. "Thank you for the public confidence."

"You want me lying?" Avery asked.

"No," Eli said.

"Good," Avery said.

The word struck harder than comfort would have. It was one more fact among too many, and Sarah understood at once why Eli hated it. The man was already frightened. Having someone else narrate the precise shape of that fear in front of witnesses was not mercy.

Sarah looked at Eli directly. "Can you still tell me when you stop trusting what you're seeing?"

He met her eyes. For the first time since they had hauled Elijah out under blood and stone, she saw something in him that looked almost young.

"If I know first," he said.

Rook snorted once. "Comforting standard."

That got the smallest twitch out of Eli's mouth.

"You say that," Eli said, "like you've ever once aimed higher."

There it was.

Dry. Ruined. Human.

"I had a whole speech prepared about your many admirable habits," Rook said. "Losing the flask has made it impossible to deliver honestly."

Eli rubbed one hand over Pebble's rough skull as the Wayhound ghosted back to his side. "Terrible timing on my part."

"Recurring issue with you," Rook said.

The exchange was brief enough to miss if someone had been waiting for sentiment. Sarah did not miss it. Neither, judging by the angle of Avery's face, did the local surveyor beside her.

She touched the tablet again. Another spike. Lower now. Closer.

"Move," she said.

They did.

Kaden had gone down in a chamber that had once, apparently, believed in order.

That belief had not held.

The shelf they reached was cut into a larger worked space supported by split arches and old brace-columns whose metal hearts had begun to show through the stone where the Continuum had worried them for too many years. Marker stakes leaned at angles that suggested prior geometry. One entire side of the chamber had sheared inward, leaving a wedge of broken floor sloping toward a lower cut where pale seam-light breathed up through the cracks.

Pebble reached the edge first and barked once.

Sarah dropped to one knee beside him and forced the tablet flat against the stone.

The signal did not spike this time.

It bloomed.

Not strong. Just close enough to feel obscene.

"He's here."

Rook was already moving before she finished the sentence. Avery caught his sleeve once, redirected him around the unstable part of the shelf, then went down the slope first with the ugly confidence of a man reading support failure faster than anyone else in the room.

Kaden lay half under a collapsed crossbrace, one arm flung above his head as if dropped there by a body that had stopped negotiating with itself on the way down. His comm rig was still clipped to his shoulder, dark now except for one weak pulse buried under dust. There was blood at one ear. More at the corner of his mouth. Not enough to be the worst thing Sarah had ever seen.

Enough.

She reached him at the same time Avery did.

"Don't move the brace yet," she said.

"Wasn't planning to," Avery said.

Dr. Avery slid in opposite, scanner already in hand. Rook knelt at Kaden's side with a speed that had nothing to do with field procedure and everything to do with history nobody else fully had.

"Kaden." His hand stopped just short of the man's face, then redirected to his shoulder instead. "Kaden."

No answer.

Sarah checked airway, pulse, pupils. Alive. Barely responsive. Breathing shallow but regular enough not to terrify her instantly into the worst branch of medicine. That counted as good now. She hated how often the Continuum had taught her to lower the threshold for relief.

"Concussed," Dr. Avery said. "And stressed to hell."

"Useful field classification," Sarah said.

"I contain multitudes."

Rook did not look up. "Can he move?"

Sarah glanced at the pinning brace. Bent metal. Broken stone. Bad angle, but not irrecoverable.

"With care," she said. "Which means not like that."

Rook's hand had already closed around the beam.

He let go.

"Cruel woman."

She and Avery freed Kaden together. Not fast. Fast was for cleaner spaces. Avery took the stone weight in both hands while Sarah braced and turned the twisted metal enough for Rook to slide Kaden clear without wrenching what was still attached inside him.

The moment the pressure came off, Kaden made a sound through his teeth.

Not awake.

Not absent either.

Rook's whole body went still around him.

"Easy," he said, far too quietly for someone unconscious. "Don't embarrass me by dying in a ruin."

Kaden's lashes moved once. Nothing else.

Sarah looked at the blood on the comm rig and understood one piece of it. "He kept forcing the line open."

Rook did not answer.

He did not need to.

Eli had come halfway down the slope and stopped there, one hand on the wall, the other hard against the side of his head. Pebble stayed plastered to his leg as if holding the line physically might achieve something all their field equipment had failed to manage.

"Do not come down the left side," Avery said without looking at him. "It's lying."

Eli swallowed. "I know."

Sarah looked up sharply. "What?"

His eyes tracked to a stretch of stable stone that had not moved once since they entered the chamber.

"It isn't stable," he said. "It only wants us to think it is."

Eli's nose started bleeding.

Not heavily. Just one thin bright line from his left nostril that he wiped away in irritation rather than drama.

Sarah did not like the sight of it at all.

Neither did Avery.

He came back up the slope at once, knelt in front of Eli, and took his wrist with the kind of practical entitlement long familiarity permitted.

"How bad?" Avery asked.

"Annoying," Eli said.

"Meaning bad," Avery said.

"Meaning if I answer honestly you'll tell me to sit down somewhere while all of you carry a man and a portal problem without me."

Avery let go of his wrist and looked him full in the face. "If I thought sitting you down would solve this, I would have done it already."

That shut Eli up for half a second.

Then he laughed once through the pain. "Cruel man."

"Recurring issue with me," Avery said.

Rook looked up then, catching the echo, and for one second the group had something like shape again. Not peace. Just the remembered outline of one.

"Can you walk straight?" Sarah asked Eli.

"No."

The honesty was immediate enough that she trusted it more than reassurance.

"Can you tell me when you're wrong?"

He took too long to answer.

"Sometimes," he said.

Sarah accepted that because the corridor would not wait for better material.

"Fine. Then no one takes a route call from Eli alone. If he sees something, Avery confirms it or Pebble does. Understood?"

No one argued.

Not even Eli.

That frightened her more than the blood had.

They moved out slower than before and therefore more dangerously. Kaden had to be carried. Elijah had to be half-walked, half-supported. The tablet had to be protected from every impact while also being used. Every few minutes the route reminded them that the Continuum did not consider basic anatomy persuasive.

Sarah took lead with Avery through the upper cuts. Rook carried Kaden. Dr. Avery stayed with Elijah. Eli moved near the center because none of them were willing to leave him unwatched and none of them were willing to say that aloud. Pebble ranged the edges, too intent now for comfort, repeatedly cutting ahead and back as if the truth of the route had become impatient with human lag.

"How close?" Dr. Avery asked once.

Avery touched the wall, listening with the backs of two fingers.

"To the portal?" he said. "Too close for confidence. Close enough for consequences."

"Poetry from you is medically concerning," Elijah said.

He sounded gray.

Rook looked back over one shoulder. "You'll forgive us if the atmosphere is not clinic grade."

Kaden stirred just as they cleared a brace-cut arch and entered the long approach west.

It happened in pieces.

First a small involuntary jerk against the support wrap under his arms. Then one hand flexing. Then a rough drag of breath that sounded like he had surfaced into the wrong century and objected on principle.

Rook shifted him down enough to see his face. "Kaden."

Kaden's eyes opened.

Not fully. Enough.

Confusion moved through them, then recognition, then something Sarah hated more than panic because it was older and quieter.

"You all look terrible," he whispered.

"Leadership failure," Rook said.

Kaden's mouth pulled by fractions. "You should have delegated."

The laugh that went through the group was brief and ugly and useful.

Sarah crouched long enough to check him again. "Stay with me. Can you track where you hurt?"

"Can I?" Kaden asked. His voice was dry enough to abrade the air. "Or would you like the highlights."

"Highlights."

"Head. Chest. Teeth. Pride."

"Normal, then," Rook said.

Kaden looked at him more clearly now. Something in that look made Sarah want to drag time backward by the throat and hold it still.

"You found me," Kaden said.

"Still trying not to make a hobby of it."

The corridor ahead narrowed.

Avery's posture changed first.

Portal region.

Sarah knew it before he said anything because the tablet's display had started to misbehave in a new dialect. Not static this time. Doubling. Return ghosts on the screen. The same path trying to become several nearly identical lies.

"We're close," she said.

"Yes," Avery said.

Eli stopped.

Rook turned immediately. "What."

Eli's gaze had fixed on something ahead and to the right. Not the route Avery had just checked. Not Pebble, who had gone rigid on the left side of the corridor with fur high and mouth shut.

"Did you hear that?" Eli asked.

No one answered at first.

Because they had all heard something.

Just not the same thing.

For Sarah it was a crackle in the stone.

For Dr. Avery, judging by his expression, some shift in the corridor support.

For Elijah it seemed to be the sudden tightening in Eli's face.

For Pebble it was apparently enough to make him lunge two steps left and bark once, sharp and furious.

Eli looked right.

That was the error.

Sarah saw it happen and did not know it was one until after.

He flinched toward the wrong side of the corridor with the raw certainty of a man answering danger he believed had already reached them.

"Right side!" he snapped. "Move Kaden right!"

Pebble barked again from the left.

Avery shouted at the same instant, "No-"

But Rook had already committed.

There were people who hesitated under contradictory input and people who chose a line and spent themselves on it without reserve.

Rook had never belonged to the first category.

He drove forward with Kaden's weight, turning his own body between the waking man and what Eli had called as threat. It was exactly the move Sarah would later understand any of them might have made for someone they loved enough or feared losing enough.

Except the danger was not on the right.

The right side only wanted them looking there.

The true failure came out of the left wall where the portal approach had been stitched too many times and trusted too often: a hidden limiter spar buried in the stone gave way under seam-pressure, tore loose with a shriek of old metal, and came across the corridor in a single violent sweep.

Pebble had known.

That was what he had been trying to tell them.

Kaden saw it in the same instant Sarah did.

His eyes went wide.

"Rook-"

Rook turned hard on the sound and made the last choice he had time to make.

He threw Kaden clear.

Not heroically.

Practically. Fast. The exact ugly angle needed to get the other man out of the path of the spar.

The metal missed Kaden by inches.

It caught Rook across the ribs and drove him backward into the broken brace-column behind him with enough force to make the whole corridor answer.

The sound it made was not one Sarah had any professional interest in ever hearing again.

For a second the world became pieces.

Dust.

Stone.

Kaden hitting the ground shoulder-first and rolling badly.

Elijah shouting something from too far away.

Pebble's bark cut short into a raw animal noise.

Eli frozen as if the wrongness of what had happened had exceeded even panic's ability to use him.

Then motion came back all at once.

Sarah reached Rook first with Avery only half a step behind her.

He was alive.

That was the first and worst fact.

Alive enough to know.

Alive enough to hurt.

The spar had not pinned him through. It had struck, broken, driven, and torn. One side of his chest moved wrong. Blood was already spreading beneath him in a dark deliberate sheet. His breathing came shallow and fast, then shallower.

Sarah's hands were on him before thought finished arriving.

"Pressure," she said.

She did not know to whom.

Avery was already there. Dr. Avery dropped beside them. Kaden, half upright now, stared across the shattered feet of corridor with the look of someone watching a fact he had feared for years finally decide to become visible.

Eli had not moved.

Rook's eyes found him anyway.

That was the cruelest part.

Not that Eli had made the call.

That he had to live long enough inside himself to see the result waiting for him in another man's face.

Sarah felt Avery register the same thing.

He did not look at Eli.

He looked only at the wound and said, with pure savage concentration, "Not his fault now. Work."

It was not absolution.

It was command.

Sarah obeyed it because her hands already were.

The wound was too large and moving wrong. Blood kept finding new places to go. Each breath hitched lower than the one before, as if his body had begun giving ground by inches it could not afford.

Sarah saw Dr. Avery understand it.

Avery understood it too, almost before either of them did, because men who lived in bad places learned the arithmetic of endings faster than medicine ever wanted admitted.

Rook understood it last only because shock delayed the courtesy.

"No," Kaden said from the floor.

It came out low and broken enough that Sarah almost would have preferred screaming.

Rook's mouth moved by fractions. "Helpful analysis."

The line should not have landed. It did. Because he was himself even there. Even then.

Kaden made a sound that folded in on itself somewhere between laugh and wreckage.

Eli came forward one step.

Then another.

There was blood on Eli's face now from the earlier nosebleed. More where he had wiped it with the back of one hand and failed to improve the result. He looked like a man approaching a verdict that had already been passed elsewhere.

"Rook," he said.

Just the name.

Rook looked at him. Not accusingly. Almost impatiently, as if Eli had missed something obvious and was wasting the remaining time arriving at it.

"You had one job," Rook said.

Sarah felt everyone in the corridor go rigid.

Then Rook took one painful breath and finished, "Stay where I can see you."

It was so dry, so nearly the shape of ordinary speech, that Eli's face broke around it.

"I told you right," he said.

"Yes," Rook said.

No cruelty.

Only fact.

"Bad habit."

Eli made a wrecked sound and dropped to his knees hard enough to bruise.

"It's my fault this happened," Eli said.

The words tore out of him raw enough to sound involuntary.

"It's my fault this happened."

"I know," Rook said.

Rook, dying in a broken corridor with the portal close enough to smell at the edge of the air, chose not to waste what was left on ceremony.

He looked once toward Kaden instead.

Not long.

Long enough.

Kaden had dragged himself closer despite Sarah's total lack of permission. Blood from his split lip had dried at the corner of his mouth. His face had gone white in the way illness and grief negotiated when neither intended to concede.

Rook saw him and the smallest trace of something gentler crossed what pain had left of his expression.

"Still here?" he asked.

Kaden choked on the answer. "Yes."

"Annoying for you."

That finally got him. Kaden folded over the sound of a laugh he could not complete.

Sarah pressed harder, not because it would save him now, but because surrender felt too much like agreement.

Rook's eyes shifted once more, past them all, toward the dim glow west where the portal was holding because Sister Orin had not yet permitted reality another opinion.

"Move," he said.

Sarah shook her head before she knew she was doing it. "No."

Rook looked back at her with the tired almost-amusement of a man who had found a scientist behaving emotionally in the field and chosen not to embarrass her for it.

"Doctor," he said, and it was impossible to tell whether he meant her or the other Avery.

Maybe both.

"He's right," Avery said quietly.

Sarah wanted to hate him for being correct at a moment like that. The effort failed for lack of time.

The corridor was still coming apart around them. Pebble had stationed himself at the broken left side and would not take his eyes off the dark beyond it. Elijah was trying and failing to stand without help. The portal region hummed through the stone with the sickening high-thin sound of maintained instability.

Rook did not have enough life left in him for argument and everybody knew it now.

That was when Sarah let herself feel it.

Not grief. Not yet.

Only defeat.

Small enough to carry.

"We move him," she said.

Dr. Avery's eyes met hers for one terrible second. He answered the question she had not wanted to say.

"We can't."

Rook exhaled once through his teeth. "Charming discussion."

Eli bowed forward until one hand caught him on the floor. Pebble left the corridor mouth at once and came to him, pressing hard against his side. Eli gripped handfuls of the Wayhound's rough fur like a drowning man selecting one fact he still believed.

Rook watched that too.

"Good dog," he said, and the compliment was somehow devastating in its ordinariness.

Kaden had reached him by then. He did not touch the wound. He knew enough not to do that. He only put one shaking hand against Rook's shoulder and kept it there as if contact could still serve any purpose not already denied.

Rook took one more breath.

Then another, shallower.

His fingers slackened under Kaden's hand.

The focus went out of his face by degrees, not all at once but enough that the man still lying there stopped being reachable by any ordinary route Sarah knew.

His chest rose once more.

Did not quite settle.

Then stopped trying to rise at all.

And because the Continuum was obscene in ways the real world had at least the decency to organize better, the portal gave a violent shudder in the same second, as if reminding the survivors that grief remained secondary to logistics unless they intended all of it to become meaningless at once.

No one said his name immediately.

Because saying it would have made it singular.

Made it finished.

Kaden did first.

Not loudly.

Not with theater.

Just once.

"Rook."

There was nothing in the corridor after that but the sound of people continuing to breathe because their own bodies had failed to receive better instructions.

Then Sister Orin's voice reached them through the comm in one harsh crackle of signal somehow forced back to life by proximity to the gate.

"If you intend to live," she said, "come now."

That ended the moment.

Not because it deserved ending.

Because bad worlds made that choice for you.

Kaden rose too.

Not cleanly.

Not wisely.

But under his own power, with one hand still on Rook's shoulder and the other braced against the broken floor as if refusal alone could keep the shape of the world from finalizing around him.

"No," he said.

Sarah turned at once. "Kaden-"

"No." He looked at none of them. Only at Rook. "We're not leaving him here."

"I can't let him die here."

He bent as if to get both hands under Rook's weight and pull him up by force, by loyalty, by whatever was left in him that still believed effort could overrule fact.

Dr. Avery caught him first.

"Don't."

Kaden tore free hard enough to nearly lose his footing.

"Get off me."

Avery crossed the space in two strides and got hold of his arm.

"Kaden."

"I said no." The words came out raw and half-shredded. "I'm not leaving him. Not him."

The corridor gave another long metal groan somewhere west. Dust fell from the ceiling in a dry slow veil.

Sarah saw the exact second Avery understood that persuasion had already failed.

He looked at Dr. Avery once.

The older man nodded.

Then both of them took Kaden at once.

Not gently.

Not cruelly either.

Just with the kind of practiced force people used when the only alternative was letting grief become another body on the floor.

Kaden fought them.

Badly, because he was injured and half-spent and half out of his mind with it.

But honestly.

He got one hand free and caught at Rook's sleeve. The fabric slipped through blood and dust and did not bring the man with it.

"Rook-"

The name broke on him that time.

Elijah, white-faced and barely upright, turned away because there was no dignity in watching and none in not.

Sarah hated all of them for surviving enough to do the necessary thing.

"Move him," Avery said.

"We are," Dr. Avery snapped back, still holding Kaden as the younger man tried once more to wrench free.

Eli stepped in then.

Not wild now.

Worse, in some ways. Clear.

Whatever in him had been seeing wrong a minute earlier had narrowed into one hard brutal line of usefulness. Grief had not steadied him. It had simply burned away everything not immediately real.

He caught Kaden by the shoulders, forced him to look up, and said, with no softness and no room left for argument, "He's already gone."

Kaden stared at him.

Eli's face was blood-streaked from the nosebleed, gray with pain, and emptied out to essentials.

"If you stay," Eli said, "then I get you killed too."

That landed.

Not because Kaden agreed.

Because some ruined surviving part of him still knew Eli was telling the truth.

His resistance broke by degrees rather than all at once. Not surrender. Structural failure.

Sarah stood first because someone had to. Avery took Elijah. Dr. Avery and Eli got Kaden between them and pulled him back while Rook's blood was still wet on all of them.

He rose, wiped his face once with the back of his hand, and looked straight at the corridor ahead without flinching.

"Left shelf is gone," he said, voice scraped flat. "Take the cut by Orin's marks. Not the wider run. That one folds."

Pebble barked once.

"Good," Eli said at once. "Stay with me."

No one touched Rook again.

That would live in all of them later in different and specialized ways.

The last run to the portal happened half outside memory.

Sarah knew afterward that Sister Orin had still been there exactly where she had promised to remain, standing at the gate with one hand braced against the regulator stone and the other lifted in a hard spare motion that meant hurry now or die here. Seam-light and portal fire cut her silhouette into something older than ceremony and more exhausted than she would ever have admitted aloud. The chamber beyond had roared with energy and old language Sarah almost understood, and somebody had put Kaden through first and Elijah second and Dr. Avery somewhere around the same impossible beat while everything local remained on the wrong side of the threshold.

She knew Eli had looked back exactly once when they reached the threshold.

Not at the corridor.

At the place in it where Rook was no longer visible.

Pebble barked once, furious and grief-struck both, and Eli stopped short at the edge of the gate while the real-world survivors were driven through without him.

Then Sarah was falling.

Then not.

Then the world had corners again.

*

Fluorescent light was almost worse.

Not because it was cruel.

Because it was familiar enough to expect obedience.

Sarah woke on a polished floor under white overhead panels with the smell of antiseptic in the vents and the taste of copper still sitting ugly at the back of her throat. For three full seconds she believed the Continuum had simply invented a new lie to tell her.

Then she saw the ceiling grid.

Real one.

Not imitated.

Dr. Avery was on the floor beside her, breathing, one arm thrown over his face against the light as if offended by the world for surviving it. Elijah lay on a wheeled intake cot somebody had failed to secure properly before disaster rearranged their staffing expectations. Kaden had ended up half against the base of a workstation bank, awake now in the flat stunned way of a man who had returned alive without receiving any corresponding conviction that this counted as luck.

No one local had come through.

Not Eli.

Not Avery.

Not Pebble.

Sarah knew they would not be.

The absence struck anyway.

The room was wrong for other reasons too. Too clean. Too empty. One sealed observation window. One red standby indicator above the only door. An emergency decon suite or intake annex, maybe. Whatever room a company used when it wanted returning field assets contained before deciding whether to classify them as survivors or contamination.

Dr. Avery saw the same thing a beat later.

"We're back," he said.

No one answered.

Because return had proven a grotesquely incomplete category.

Kaden dragged one hand across his face and left a diluted smear of dried blood there. He looked at the room, then at the others, then at the place beside the door where one more person should have been.

It was possible, Sarah thought dimly, that she was watching him understand the shape of a world without Rook in it for the first time.

The workstation nearest him chimed.

Not loudly.

Politely.

A small blue login prompt lifted itself out of sleep and bloomed across the screen.

PROJECT CHIMERA FIELD RETURN NODE

AUTHORIZED ACCESS REQUIRED

MERIDIAN GROUP INTERNAL SYSTEM

The words sat there with the calm confidence of things that had killed people without ever once needing to raise their voice.

Sarah stared at them.

`Meridian Group.`

Not rumor. Not the shape of hierarchy guessed from withheld budgets and careful silences. A name. Formal. Administrative. Real in the ugliest way possible.

Dr. Avery said it first.

Quietly.

"Meridian."

Dr. Avery, still on the floor, turned his head enough to read the screen upside down. His mouth hardened by degrees.

"That's above Chimera?"

"Apparently," Sarah said.

Her own voice sounded remote.

Elijah pushed himself up on the cot despite every medical principle she had ever tried to instill in him.

"No," he said.

She looked over.

He was staring at the screen with a depth of loathing that had not been there before the crossing.

"Not by accident."

Kaden laughed once.

There was nothing humanly cheerful in it.

"No," he said. "Not apparently."

Everyone looked at him.

He did not seem to notice that immediately. His gaze had fixed somewhere lower than the room, somewhere still carrying broken corridor light and the exact sound of metal hitting bone.

"Rook knew," he said.

Sarah said his name carefully. "Kaden."

That seemed to bring him back by fractions.

He swallowed once. Twice. Failed to remove the wreckage from his voice.

"He knew what they were long before the rest of us admitted it. He just..." Kaden shut his eyes. "He kept trying to solve the part in front of him first."

No one interrupted.

The silence in that room had changed species. It was no longer shock alone. The screen had given them the institutional body to match all the damage they had suffered in fragments.

Kaden looked down at his own hands.

"There is something about the company's tests," he said slowly, as if each word had to be checked for structural failure before release, "that happened to me."

Sarah felt Dr. Avery go completely still.

Dr. Avery sat up.

Elijah stopped trying to hide the fact that pain was eating through him from several directions at once.

Kaden went on because grief had apparently broken something in him that fear had not managed to keep closed.

"Not equipment." He smiled without life. "Not upgrades. Not anything they put in a report and called promising." He looked up then, and whatever the Continuum had done to all of them, it had not invented that expression for him. It belonged to this world. To white rooms. To procedural men. To clean language used as a blunt instrument. "They hurt me for data."

No one moved.

The workstation screen hummed softly in the corner like a machine pretending neutrality while its employer bled through every interface.

"Rook tried to stop it," Kaden said. "He couldn't. He was the only one who-" His voice failed, came back worse. "He was the only one left who knew what all of it cost."

Sarah sat down because her knees had made a decision and neglected to ask the rest of her for permission.

Dr. Avery looked at the words `MERIDIAN GROUP INTERNAL SYSTEM` again with the kind of concentration people usually reserved for finding a target through weather.

Elijah's hand tightened on the edge of the cot until the knuckles paled.

Dr. Avery stood up very slowly.

Not because he was weak.

Because too much speed in a room like that might have become damage.

"Then this is it," he said.

Sarah looked over. "What is it?"

"The part where they stop being the structure around the problem," Dr. Avery said. "And become the problem."

Kaden let out one rough breath that might have been agreement if agreement had ever deserved to sound that exhausted.

No one made a speech after that.

No one swore vengeance into the fluorescent air.

Sarah stood, crossed to the workstation, and looked at the login prompt long enough for the hatred beneath her fatigue to settle into something cleaner and more useful.

"We close the breach," she said.

Dr. Avery rose beside her.

"And we do not give this back to them," Dr. Avery said.

Elijah answered from the cot without looking away from the screen.

"No," Elijah said.

Dr. Avery said nothing for one beat.

Then:

"Agreed," Dr. Avery said.

Kaden's voice came last and hardest.

"Burn it out of their hands."

Sarah moved before anyone could make the decision abstract again.

She hit the keyboard awake with the heel of one hand, dragged the nearest data window open, and started killing everything she recognized on sight.

Return logs.

Signal maps.

Transfer-state captures.

Raw breach telemetry.

Anything that looked like route memory translated into machine language.

The workstation threw up warning prompts. She ignored them. Dr. Avery stepped in at once, took the secondary console, and started wiping mirrored buffers with the cold efficient focus of a man who had just been given a target worthy of his concentration.

Elijah got off the cot despite pain and reached the wall cabinet where sealed field drives sat in a neat corporate row. He swept them to the floor one-handed and brought his heel down on the first hard enough to crack the casing.

Kaden pushed himself upright against the workstation bank, swaying once, then reached for the comm dock.

"If they still have live links through this node," he said, voice ragged but sharpening around the work, "I can poison the handshake."

Sarah looked over once. "Can you?"

Kaden's mouth twitched without humor. "Watch me."

He tore the uplink cradle loose hard enough to rip one cable free in a spray of sparks.

No one in the room tried to stop any of it.

That was the answer.

Not a speech.

Not a vote.

Only action arriving faster than caution because caution had finally run out of moral cover.

Monitors went black one by one.

A warning light over the inner door flipped from passive red to a brighter, angrier variant.

Somewhere deeper in the facility, an alarm began considering whether it had enough evidence to become a problem.

Meridian Group.

Concrete.

Institutional.

Real enough to kill for secrecy.

Real enough to break men open and call it research.

Real enough that Rook was dead and the shape of that death now had an address.

Sarah pulled the final drive free and dropped it to the floor.

"This is not stopping here," she said.

No one mistook her.

Not the breach.

Not this room.

Not this one return.

Dr. Avery looked at the dead screen in front of him. "Then we close every opening we can find before they use it again."

Elijah, pale and shaking and absolutely finished with obedience, ground the broken drive casing under his heel.

"And when they come after us for it," he said, "we do not wait politely to die."

Kaden had one hand pressed to the torn uplink and the other braced against the desk to keep himself vertical. His face had gone bloodless with exhaustion. His eyes had not.

"They will," he said.

Sarah met his gaze.

"Good," she said.

Dr. Avery straightened.

"Then they can spend the rest of their lives trying."

That was all.

No anthem.

No grand declaration.

Only four ruined people in a sterile room, closing what they could, erasing what they could, and deciding with perfect clarity that the company above them would get nothing else for free.

The last surviving monitor flickered once, then died under Dr. Avery's hand.

The room dimmed by degrees.

Beyond the sealed door, the building had begun to wake up.

So had they.
