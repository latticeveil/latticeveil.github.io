# FRAME 06
# CONVERGENCE

Elijah heard the small claws on stone before he saw what made them.

He turned in the corridor and found a creature standing a few paces back in the dim spill of pale seam-light, head slightly lowered, ears lifted, body relaxed in the unforced way of an animal that did not yet consider him a problem.

It was about the size of a border collie, though built leaner through the ribs, with long legs meant for broken ground and a tail that curled once at the tip before falling still. Its coat carried the color of wet ash, but under that soft gray ran faint pale lines like buried lattice scars, only visible when it shifted and the light caught them at the wrong angle. Its eyes reflected too much. Not humanly. Not monstrously. Just enough to remind Elijah he was standing in a world that simplified everything except unease.

The creature blinked at him.

Elijah blinked back.

"Well," he said softly into the empty corridor, because nobody could hear him anyway, "you're not what I expected."

It sat.

The gesture was so ordinary that he laughed once before he could stop himself.

The sound surprised him. So did the absence of fear.

He had been afraid of the corridor. Afraid of the silence that never settled right. Afraid of the moments when the Continuum touched his skin and reminded him he had not been made for it. But the animal in front of him did not tighten the air. It did not feel like the room trying to think through him. It felt curious. Cautious, maybe. But harmless in the immediate, obvious way that mattered.

Elijah crouched slowly, one hand braced against his knee. "If you bite me," he said, "that is going to feel extremely unfair."

The creature stepped forward, nose working. It sniffed the air near his fingers, then the back of his hand, then leaned its narrow skull into his palm with complete and unembarrassed trust.

Elijah let out a quiet breath.

"Okay," he murmured. "Hi."

He scratched carefully behind one ear.

The animal closed its eyes.

That was enough to settle the matter.

Whatever else this place held, whatever else the others might be facing, Elijah was safe for this second and the second after it. The creature pressed lightly into his hand again, accepting another slow pet as if this were a perfectly normal meeting between a displaced researcher and a seam-marked cave animal in an impossible world.

Its fur was warmer than he expected.

He stayed there a moment longer than he needed to, one hand resting between its shoulders, grounded by the simple fact of another living thing that did not want anything from him except gentleness.

Then the familiar tingling started in his fingertips.

Elijah pulled his hand back on instinct.

The sensation was brief this time, less pain than wrongness, as though the bones in his fingers had remembered a different geometry and then corrected themselves before he could fully register it. Tiny phantom angles. A pressure to resolve. He flexed his hand once, hard, until it belonged to him again.

The creature watched him with quiet attention.

"Yeah," Elijah said. "I'm working on it."

His watch remained dark except for the weak pulse that marked the others as distant points of presence. Sarah. Dr. Avery Chen. Kaden. Rook. No voices came through. No static. Just silence and the knowledge that they existed somewhere beyond the stone and drift and bad mathematics of this place.

The corridor ahead forked in two almost identical directions. One path sloped down toward a draft of colder air. The other curved upward through a cut in the rock marked with a chalk line so faint he almost missed it.

The animal turned and looked back at him.

Then it took the upward path.

Elijah rose slowly. "You're kidding."

The creature paused and glanced over one shoulder as if waiting.

He could have laughed again.

"Right," he said. "Follow the mysterious cave dog. Excellent field procedure."

Still, he followed.

The creature did not run ahead so much as move in short, confident advances, always staying just far enough forward to remain a guide without feeling like a lure. Twice it stopped and waited for him at narrow turns where the rock pinched close. Once it ducked beneath a fractured brace and looked back until he copied the motion. Each time Elijah obeyed before he had fully decided to.

The route felt easier than the corridor had any right to.

That should have worried him more than it did.

Instead he found himself studying the old marks on the walls: chalk strokes nearly rubbed away, shallow cuts in the stone, one place where someone had pressed a palm against wet mineral dust long enough to leave the shape of a hand before time hardened around it. Survey language. Boundary. Passage. Warning.

Not his language.

Not exactly.

Yet something in him moved as if it had seen these marks a thousand times.

He slowed.

The animal slowed too.

There was a turn ahead. Narrow. Shadowed. The kind of threshold that should have been unremarkable. Elijah stared at it and saw, for half a breath, another hand closing around the same corner with easy familiarity. Not his hand. Smaller knuckles. Different posture. Quick, impatient movement checked at the last second by instinct instead of thought.

The image vanished.

Elijah stood very still.

The corridor around him remained the same. Wet stone. Dust. Pale seam-light. The creature waiting three paces ahead.

But the flicker had left something behind. Not a memory exactly. A pressure. A direction already known before he had chosen it.

"Eli," he said before he could stop himself.

The name settled into the air between the stones like it belonged there.

He pressed his thumb hard into the center of his palm until the edge of pain steadied him. "Small connections," he whispered. "That's all."

He did not know whether he was reassuring himself or warning himself.

The next flash came softer.

Not vision this time. Motion.

A certainty that if the floor ahead gave way, he should lean left, not right. A certainty that the pale seam on the wall was old and sleeping, while the darker one near the ceiling had not finished moving yet. A certainty that the animal in front of him was not dangerous, but hated ringing metal and would bolt if startled by it.

Elijah stared at the creature.

It stared back.

"I don't know how I know that," he said.

The animal sneezed.

He took another step.

Somewhere behind his ribs, fear tried to reorganize itself into usefulness. He let it.

If these brief connections were all he had, then he would use them. Carefully. Quietly. Without pretending they were anything safer than they were.

The corridor widened into a chamber where an old support frame had collapsed in on itself. The creature slipped through first, then waited on the far side while Elijah navigated the broken timber and stone. When he emerged, he found another chalk mark on the wall opposite him. Fresh enough to matter.

One line. Deliberate. Human.

Elijah touched it and felt the faint drag of dust on skin.

Someone had passed through here recently.

The creature bumped its head once against his thigh, impatient but not anxious.

Elijah looked down.

"You really do expect me to keep up."

It wagged its tail once.

That, more than anything, made him smile.

He glanced at the pale seam-lines threaded under its fur and then up at the dim cracks in the ceiling answering them with the same cold geometry.

"All right," he said softly. "If you're staying, I can't keep calling you `the creature` in my head."

The animal watched him with patient attention, as if the problem were his to solve and not especially urgent.

Elijah huffed a quiet laugh. "Scar would be too obvious. Seam's worse."

The animal sneezed again.

He tilted his head, considering it. Not what it looked like. Small, steady, round-faced in the dim light despite the long legs. Not even what it was doing. The name came with the same quiet certainty the route-marks had started carrying, arriving whole before he had a chance to think of something better.

"Pebble," he said at last, testing the word.

The animal's ears twitched once.

Elijah nodded as if that settled it. "Pebble, then."

After a beat he added, quieter, "Even if you are acting like a guide."

Saying it out loud made the corridor feel less empty than it had a moment before.

He followed it deeper into the Continuum.

For the first time since the separation, he did not feel lost. Not found, exactly. But pointed.

That was enough.

Near the portal, Rook could feel the world trying to remember him incorrectly.

The stable anomaly shimmered in the chamber ahead, its edges clear enough to trust and wrong enough not to. Around it, the stone bore the marks of old work: brace slots, chalk residue, anchor cuts, the practical grammar of people who had once built a route here and known they might need to flee through it later. The air carried the faint mineral scent of Veilglass and the colder metallic note of a limiter that had been activated too many times and survived anyway.

Rook stood with Avery, Eli, and Sister Orin while Kaden's voice came and went through the static-ridden comms like a thought that had learned how to speak.

"Sarah is moving," Kaden said, his voice thin, then suddenly near. "Dr. Avery Chen too. Elijah is still cut off, but his signal is moving."

"Moving where?" Rook asked.

"Toward the convergence point," Kaden said. "I think."

Eli dragged a hand through his hair and looked toward the dark route leading away from the portal chamber. "That isn't the same as safe."

"No," Avery said.

Sister Orin stood nearest the portal itself, one weathered hand resting lightly against the stone beside it rather than the anomaly directly. She watched it the way one watched fire in a dry season: not afraid of it exactly, but never trusting it to remember limits on its own.

"If the signals are converging," she said, "they will not converge at the same pace."

Rook barely heard her.

A pressure had started again behind his eyes.

Not pain. Not yet.

A flash of another corridor. A younger set of hands, gloveless, sliding a survey marker into a crack in the wall. A muttered curse at crooked stone. A laugh from farther ahead. Then the whole thing broke apart before he could hold it.

He steadied himself against a brace post.

Eli noticed immediately. "You all right?"

Rook exhaled once through his nose. "No, but I seem to be continuing anyway."

That earned the smallest possible snort from Eli.

Kaden's voice crackled back in. "Elijah's signal just shifted again. Faster."

Rook looked toward the tunnel leading east from the portal chamber before anyone else spoke.

He knew, with the same unreasonable certainty as the flashes, that if he stayed here waiting for the others to arrive neatly, something important would happen without him. Maybe dangerous. Maybe necessary. Maybe both.

"I'm going after him," he said.

That turned all three local faces toward him.

Avery answered first, because of course he did. "No."

"You don't know the route," Eli said over him.

"And you are least stable near thresholds," Sister Orin added, not unkindly.

Rook glanced at the portal, at the place that had already made a liar out of several laws he had once trusted. "Exactly."

Avery folded his arms. "The smart move is to stay put. Let the others come to the stable point."

Rook almost smiled.

"I was never very smart," he said. "I was just good at sounding like I had reasons."

Eli let out a rough laugh despite himself. Sister Orin did not smile, but something in her expression loosened by a fraction. Avery looked unimpressed in a way that suggested he might have liked Rook under different circumstances and resented that fact now.

"This is not wit," Avery said. "This is a bad decision."

"Probably," Rook agreed. "Still mine."

Another flash struck him before anyone could answer.

Stone under moon-cold light.
A voice saying his name, but not in Sarah's accent, not in Dr. Avery Chen's clipped precision, not in Kaden's void-thinned distance.
A sudden certainty that a leftward trail would lead to a ridge marked by three stakes and a broken limiter cap.

Rook shut his eyes against it.

When he opened them, Eli was watching him too closely.

"You're getting more of them," Eli said quietly.

Rook did not bother pretending otherwise. "Pieces."

"Whose?" Avery asked.

That was the question underneath everything, wasn't it.

Rook looked past them, into the tunnel that seemed to pull at some half-laid route in him. "I think," he said, and stopped because honesty sometimes arrived in fragments too, "I think they belong to the man who should have been here before me."

No one spoke for a moment.

Sister Orin broke the silence. "Then you must be careful not to mistake inheritance for instruction."

Rook looked at her. "That sounds like experience."

"It is age," she said. "Experience is the bruise age leaves."

Kaden came through the comm again, voice roughened by interference. "If Rook goes, he should not go alone."

The chamber quieted.

Then Avery made the decision the way practical people always did, by accepting the least-worse arrangement available.

"Fine," he said. "No one wanders blind. We split routes, hold voice contact when we can, and come back here if the signals collapse."

Eli straightened. "I'll take the north corridor. If Elijah's moving under old survey marks, I can read them faster than you can."

"I will stay with the portal," Sister Orin said. "And with Kaden's voice, if he can keep giving it to us. Sarah and Dr. Avery Chen will need a stable point when they arrive."

Avery nodded once. "Then I take west. Rook, east."

Eli looked at Rook again. "You sure you don't want somebody with you?"

Rook considered lying and decided it would waste everyone's time.

"No," he said. "But wanting sensible things has not improved the day so far."

That drew the ghost of a grin from Eli.

"Fair," Eli said.

Kaden's voice returned in a burst of static. "I can keep the channel open in pieces. Not continuously. The gaps are getting worse."

There was strain in him now, more obvious than before. Not physical breathlessness exactly, but the sound of someone holding too many distances together at once. Rook had heard enough lab reports in his life to know the sound of a system being used past its intended tolerance.

"Kaden," Rook said, quieter, "how hard is this hitting you?"

The reply took too long.

"I know where you all are," Kaden said at last. "That is the useful answer."

Not the real one.

Rook filed that away.

He had already gathered enough scattered hints to see the outline of something ugly: the company tests, the void adaptation, the way Kaden spoke about the space between worlds as if it were less a discovery than a scar that had learned to function. And beneath that, another implication still taking shape. A missing counterpart. An absence where there should have been a mirrored life. Not proof. Not yet. Just the shape of a question that nobody in this chamber was ready to say aloud.

Sister Orin seemed to hear the silence around that question all the same.

"What is made by force," she said, eyes still on the portal, "often arrives carrying an absence with it."

Kaden did not answer.

Neither did Rook.

Eli shifted his pack higher on one shoulder. Avery checked the limiter at his belt. The chamber seemed to draw taut around each of them, reality narrowing itself into a set of chosen routes.

Rook looked once more into the eastern tunnel.

He did not know whether the pull he felt there belonged to Elijah, to the man who had been lost before him, or to the Continuum itself arranging pieces into a pattern it wanted finished.

It did not matter enough to wait.

"Voice check when you can," Avery said.

"If you hear screaming," Eli added, "try not to assume whose."

"Comforting," Rook said.

"I'm thoughtful that way."

Then they separated.

Eli vanished north into the older survey paths.
Avery moved west with the patient caution of someone who trusted procedure more than luck.
Sister Orin remained at the portal, one hand near stone, listening to Kaden's thinning voice and to the route itself.

Rook took the east tunnel alone.

By the third turn he found the first thing that made his blood go cold: a survey mark cut into the wall at shoulder height, three clean strokes and a diagonal correction, exactly where his latest flash had told him it would be.

He touched the mark.

For one sharp heartbeat, memory folded over him.

The smell of rain on stone.
Laughter just ahead.
His own hand, or another man's, striking the wall twice before moving on.
The absolute certainty that someone he trusted was close.

Then it was gone.

Rook opened his eyes to the dim seam-light and the empty tunnel.

"Good," he murmured to nobody. "That's probably fine."

His voice sounded steadier than he felt.

He kept moving.

The fourth turn tried to close behind him.

Rook heard it before he understood it: a dry stone whisper, not from ahead or behind but from the shape of the corridor itself. He stopped hard. The seam-light along the right wall narrowed by a fraction. Then another. The tunnel was simplifying, shaving away options with the same patient violence he had already started to associate with unfinished things trying to become singular.

"No," Rook said quietly.

He did not mean it as protest. He meant it as procedure. Frame. Conduit. Limiter. Not prayer. Structure.

He backed up one step and the floor under his right heel gave a short, precise shudder. The memory-flashes came with it, sharper than before.

A hand not quite his own striking chalk against stone.
Three lines.
Pause.
Listen.
Never step where the wall wants you.

Rook moved left without thinking.

The place where he would have planted his next step sheared downward by six clean inches, as if the world had cut a piece off itself with a ruler.

He stared at the new edge. "All right," he breathed. "That's new."

The corridor ahead wavered. For one impossible second he saw two routes occupying the same space: one dry and narrow with old survey marks at shoulder height, the other broader, wet, and half-collapsed. They laid over each other like badly matched transparencies. His head throbbed with the effort of holding both.

Rook braced one hand against the wall.

The stone was cold, then briefly warm, then cold again. Not temperature. History.

Another fragment hit him.

Running.
Not fear exactly, but haste sharpened by familiarity.
Someone ahead saying, "Left wall. Always left here."

Rook looked up and found the old chalk scar exactly where the fragment said it would be, almost invisible beneath mineral bloom.

He stared at it for a beat too long.

It was not just a mark. It was a decision rendered in shorthand. Hug the stable side. Do not trust the open route. The kind of compact field logic Chimera drilled into people who were expected to walk into bad spaces and come back useful.

His throat tightened.

No one from Chimera should have been here before them. No survey team. No lost advance crew. No classified predecessor everyone had somehow forgotten to mention. They were the first people the company had ever pushed fully into a place like this.

So why was the wall speaking in procedures that belonged to them?

"You really did know this place," he muttered, not sure whether he meant the missing man, himself, or the shape of the problem opening under both.

He slid along the left wall, body turned sideways as the corridor pinched. The simplifying seam tracked him for three breaths, then lost interest and settled back into the rock with a faint ticking sound, like something disappointed by restraint.

Rook stayed still until the tunnel behaved again.

His comm spat a burst of static against his ear. Then Kaden's voice arrived in broken pieces.

"...Rook... answer... still there?"

Rook exhaled once. "Unfortunately."

Silence. Then a breath from the other side of the channel that sounded almost like relief.

"You dropped," Kaden said. "The route around you folded."

"I noticed," Rook said.

"Elijah's signal is still moving," Kaden added.

Rook straightened slowly. "Toward me?"

"Toward the same region. I can't promise more than that."

There was strain under every syllable now, something stretched thin enough that even Kaden's economy could not hide it.

Rook started forward again, slower this time. "You're sounding worse."

"I'm sounding useful," Kaden said.

"That was not the question," Rook said.

Kaden was quiet for a beat too long. When he answered, his voice had gone flatter, the way people sounded when they were stepping around the edge of a truth instead of over it.

"The old tests changed what counts as distance for me," he said. "That helps. It doesn't come free."

Rook let that sit.

He had heard enough versions of the word `tests` in sealed rooms and controlled voices to know when someone meant procedures no decent report would name plainly.

"Kaden."

"Don't." The word came out tired, not sharp. "Not while I'm holding this together."

Rook accepted that for what it was: not refusal, exactly. Delay.

"Fine," he said. "Then just tell me whether the channel is going to hold."

"In pieces," Kaden said.

"Useful pieces?" Rook asked.

"If you're disciplined," Kaden said.

Rook smiled despite himself. "You really do know how to flatter a man."

That got him something like a laugh through the static, thin and brief.

"Keep moving," Kaden said. "And if the corridor starts repeating itself, stop trusting the third version."

"That's a very specific warning."

"I've become a very specific person."

The line broke.

Rook walked on through seam-light and old scratches, following a route he knew he should not know. Twice he found marks before he understood why he was looking for them. Once he ducked under a fractured brace a heartbeat before the rotten beam above it cracked loose and dropped where his head had been. Each small survival felt less like skill than theft.

By the time the tunnel widened again, he was breathing harder than the pace deserved.

The chamber beyond had once been a waystation or work node. Three stone shelves cut into one wall. Rusted brackets. A scatter of old chalk stubs turned soft by time. On the far side stood a broken support frame that had been repaired long ago with a different hand and a different logic. The old work was careful. The later work was urgent.

Rook stepped inside and stopped.

There, in the dust near the collapsed frame, lay a fresh disturbance: a print too narrow for a boot, too deliberate for random fall. Beside it, another lighter mark he couldn't read cleanly.

Not proof.
Close enough.

He crouched and touched two fingers to the nearest print.

The chamber lurched.

For a heartbeat he was somewhere else entirely: same stone, same shelves, but lamplight instead of seam-light; another body crossing the room ahead of him; the certainty of companionship so ordinary it hurt.

Then a voice, male and familiar in the way of memory before naming:
"If I go missing, assume I took the stupid route on purpose."

Rook recoiled so hard he nearly lost his footing.

When the world settled back into the present, the room was empty again.

He swallowed against the rawness rising unexpectedly into his throat. The line had landed too close to his own thoughts, as if the dead or missing version of him had always been building toward the same bad joke.

"I hate that," he said to the room.

No answer.

Only the low mineral hush of the Continuum considering him.

He got back to his feet and kept moving.

Sarah had stopped trusting straight corridors an hour ago.

The one ahead still looked honest by the standards of this place: narrow stone throat, pale seams webbing the ceiling, old support work set at practical intervals by people who understood strain. But her tablet had begun doing something interference alone did not explain. The signal for Avery would drift, sharpen, vanish for half a second, then return not weaker but more certain, as if it were correcting toward a body rather than a direction.

That should have comforted her.

Instead it had left a cold, analytic pressure in the back of her mind that refused to resolve.

She crouched at a break in the wall where mineral water had gathered shallowly in a crack and keyed the tablet to low-scan mode again. The display fluttered once, stabilized, then showed what it had shown three times already:

Avery.
Close.
Exact.

Not approximate. Not degraded. Exact.

Sarah stared at the readout.

Project Chimera had designed the field tracker to anchor on the individual body, not clothing, not voiceprint, not whatever nonsense environmental static might imitate in a ruined corridor. Biometric specificity had been the whole point. Blood chemistry. tissue signatures. genomic indexing collapsed into something compact enough to take into the field.

Too exact.

She felt the thought arrive before she wanted it.

If the system was reading the body at that depth, then it had never been built to tell one version of a person from another.

Her fingers tightened around the tablet casing.

"No," she said softly, because the implication was stupid and impossible and increasingly difficult to dismiss.

The display did not change.

Exact match.

Not my Avery, she thought, and hated herself immediately for thinking it before proof. But the idea had already rooted. Not because the signal was false. Because it might be true in the wrong way.

Sarah rose and kept moving.

The corridor bent around a fractured pillar and opened into a long chamber crossed by fallen beams and old hanging cables turned mineral-stiff with age. Her light swept over them in slow arcs. Work site. Not recent, but not ancient enough to feel mythic either. A place between uses. A place people had once passed through often enough to leave pattern behind them.

Her comm crackled.

"Kaden?"

For a second there was only hiss. Then his voice reached her, blurred at the edges.

"Still here," Kaden said.

"I need you to listen carefully," Sarah said.

"Dangerous phrasing, coming from you," Kaden replied.

If she had been less tired, she might have smiled. Instead she stepped over a fallen brace and kept her eyes on the corridor ahead.

"The tracker is wrong," she said.

"That narrows nothing down," Kaden said.

"Not wrong like drift. Wrong like match logic."

Silence. Then Kaden said, "Say that again."

Sarah licked suddenly dry lips. Speaking it aloud made it real faster. "Chimera built these systems to identify exact biological signatures. If this place has... equivalents..." She hated the word. It felt cleaner than the truth. "Then the device may not be distinguishing between them. It may be reading them as one person."

Kaden did not answer at once.

When he did, his voice had gone very still.

"That would explain more than one thing," Kaden said.

"Yes," Sarah said.

"Don't follow certainty blindly, then," Kaden said.

Sarah looked at the tablet again. Avery. Near. Exact. The line might as well have read husband if it wanted to be cruel.

"Bit late for that," she muttered.

"Sarah," Kaden said.

Something in Kaden's tone made her stop.

"What?"

"If the system is collapsing counterpart signals, then mine..." Kaden said.

He didn't finish.

He didn't need to.

Sarah closed her eyes for half a second. One clean signal. Only Kaden. She had noticed that too and filed it away under later because later had seemed like a luxury they'd probably earn.

"We'll talk when we're breathing easier," she said.

"Bold assumption," Kaden said.

The line thinned but held.

Sarah stepped through a narrow break in the hanging cables and felt the signal spike so hard the tablet vibrated once against her palm.

Close.
Exact.
Ahead.

Her heart kicked hard enough to hurt.

The chamber beyond was wider and cleaner than the route behind it, a stone crossing-place with one fallen arch and a second still standing. Pale seam-light threaded the surviving curve of it, outlining a figure on the far side in silver-gray edges before the rest of him resolved.

Tall.
Dark hair.
The set of the shoulders wrong only if you had time to think about it.

"Avery," Sarah said, and heard the break in her own voice too late.

The figure turned.

For one full, violent heartbeat, relief obliterated every better instinct she had.

Then he moved.

Not the face first. The stillness.

Dr. Avery Chen carried tension like a held tool, purpose always gathering in the body before action. This man turned with the effortless economy of someone who belonged to the stone beneath him, as if the chamber had room for him already made. His coat was wrong. His gear was wrong. The attention in his eyes was wrong, not empty and not cold, but measuring in another language entirely.

And when he spoke, Sarah understood the words immediately and knew at once she should not have.

"You're not supposed to be here," he said.

Not her husband.

The certainty hit so cleanly it was almost merciful.

Sarah stopped three paces into the chamber, tablet still half-raised in one hand like a weapon too embarrassed to admit it wasn't one.

The display continued to insist:

Avery.
Exact.

Her stomach dropped.

Not interference.
Not drift.
The tracker was doing exactly what it had been built to do. It was matching the body too closely, diving beneath surface difference to the structure that made both men legible as the same impossible answer.

Two versions.
One signal.

Sarah looked from the readout to the man in front of her and felt grief, shock, relief, and scientific horror collide too fast to separate.

He had Avery's face only where nature was lazy. Everything else belonged to a different life.

The man on the far side of the chamber took one careful step forward, not threatening, not welcoming either. Watching.

"Who are you?" he asked.

Sarah opened her mouth.

No useful answer came out.

Because the worst part, the part that made the world feel briefly unstable beneath her boots, was that for one heartbeat she had not been mistaken alone.

Her equipment had agreed with her.
