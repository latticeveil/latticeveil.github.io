# FRAME 08
# BURST LOCK

The corridor bent once, then again, tightening toward a darker throat of stone where the seam-light thinned enough to feel withheld.

Rook heard it first as distance.

Not footsteps. Not another bad echo pretending to be helpful. A single bark from far enough ahead to be uncertain and near enough to matter.

Eli stopped so fast the motion looked like recognition before thought.

"That's him," he said.

Rook glanced over. "Elijah?"

Eli shook his head once, already listening harder. "No. The bark."

Another sound came down the corridor. Shorter this time. Impatient in a way no wild thing had any right to sound.

Eli's whole face changed.

Not much. Too little for anyone who did not know him to call it softness. But enough.

"Pebble," he said, and there was almost relief in it now. "I know that bark."

The Wayhound came around the bend in a low pale rush a second later, seam-marked fur catching the weak corridor light. He did not hesitate on seeing them. He ran straight to Eli and stopped hard in front of him as if arriving at the obvious answer to a problem everybody else had been slow enough to complicate.

"There you are," Eli said, dropping one hand at once to the rough fur of his neck. "You found him. Good."

Pebble huffed once, impatient and satisfied both, shoved his narrow head briefly into Eli's hand, then turned back down the corridor. He did not go far. He moved three paces, stopped, looked back.

Come on.

Rook saw Elijah before Eli did.

Not because the corridor gave him a cleaner line of sight. Because there was something wrong now about the way familiarity arrived. A shape in the dim. One shoulder lower than the other from fatigue. One hand on the wall. Pebble pausing just ahead of him like a guide making sure the person being rescued had not mistaken the arrangement for dignity.

"There," Rook said quietly.

Elijah came around the bend with the look of someone who had not been alone long enough to enjoy it and had still managed to be injured by the attempt. Dust streaked one sleeve. Blood had dried brown along the cuff where he had scraped his hand somewhere earlier and failed to care about it. He opened his mouth, likely to say Rook's name first.

Then Eli stepped half a pace forward, and the moment changed.

Elijah stopped.

Eli did too.

The resemblance was not exact. It was worse than exact. Exact could have been filed under anomaly and argued with later. This was the same answer written by two different hands. Face, build, the set of the mouth when neither was speaking, the same tired intelligence looking out through damage that had not happened in the same order.

Pebble gave an annoyed little bark, as if human beings were once again failing basic route discipline.

Elijah looked down at him, then back up at Eli. "How do you know I named him that?"

Eli frowned. Not defensive. Simply thrown by the premise. "I named him that."

Elijah stared.

Rook watched the sentence land and keep falling.

"What do you mean," Elijah said, more fascinated than alarmed now, "you named him?"

Eli's hand stayed on Pebble's neck, fingers sunk lightly into the rough fur there. "I mean I named him when I first met him." He glanced at Pebble as if expecting support and getting only old patience in return. "He kept it."

That should have gone somewhere loud.

Instead Elijah took one slow breath, then another, and Rook saw confusion begin to sharpen into effort rather than panic. Not disbelief. Measurement. He looked at Eli as if the right angle might make the explanation present itself.

It did not.

So Rook said the thing the corridor had already decided all of them were going to have to admit.

"You two look enough alike to ruin everyone's day."

Elijah let out a short disbelieving breath that almost counted as a laugh.

Eli looked him over with the bluntness of a man checking a wall for structural failure. "You look worse."

"Good," Elijah said. "I worked at it."

That was close enough to normal human speech that Rook felt his own shoulders loosen by a degree.

Not safety.

Only contact.

Pebble circled back and settled against Eli's leg with proprietary confidence, as if there had never been any question where he belonged once the route had finished delivering what he had gone to fetch.

Elijah noticed.

His eyes narrowed just slightly, not with resentment but with recalculation. "He knows you."

"Yes," Eli said.

Elijah absorbed that in silence. Then: "Right."

He took one step nearer.

Pain hit him immediately.

It crossed his face fast enough that another person might have missed it. Rook did not. Elijah's hand came up hard to his temple. At the same instant Eli flinched, two fingers pressing briefly above one brow as if some narrow bright spike had passed through him and gone on without asking permission.

The corridor was still there.

Pebble was still leaning against Eli's leg.

And over that, for less than a heartbeat, Elijah was somewhere else.

Stone corridor.
A seam in the air.
A hand clamped hard to another sleeve.
Eli's voice, low and clear in a way Elijah had not yet heard from the man standing in front of him now:

Avery. That's a thin place.

Then it was gone.

Elijah blinked once, sharply, and the corridor came back around him in full. Eli was still standing there, jaw tight now, eyes narrowed with the private irritation of pain he had not invited.

Rook moved before either of them decided to pretend nothing had happened.

"Give each other a little room," he said, casual enough to pass for field sense if no one looked at him too closely. "You both look worse when you're standing on top of it."

Elijah stared at him.

"On top of what?" he asked.

"My patience, mainly."

That bought him just enough compliance to be useful. Elijah stepped back half a pace. Eli did the same without needing the instruction repeated. The pressure in the corridor eased by fractions at once.

Rook filed that away.

Elijah lowered his hand from his temple. "I'm fine."

The lie was not especially convincing.

Eli's answering look suggested he knew that as well as anyone, but he let it pass. Maybe because his own head was still hurting. Maybe because people with certain kinds of damage recognized the hierarchy of available dignities and tried not to interfere with the smaller ones.

Rook touched his comm. "Kaden."

Static answered first.

Then Kaden, thinner than before: "Still here."

Elijah's expression shifted at the sound of a known human voice not attached to the people currently damaging his understanding of reality. "Where are the others?"

"Moving," Kaden said. "Sarah's with local Avery. Sister Orin's still holding at the portal. The rest are moving west."

"Four," Eli said.

There was a brief crackle of silence. "Right," Kaden said. "Pebble counts."

That got another almost-laugh out of Elijah. Less from humor than relief. Enough.

Rook looked down the corridor beyond them. "How far?"

"To everyone being a single problem?" Kaden asked. "Not far enough."

Elijah frowned. "Sister Orin stayed with the portal?"

"That was the plan," Kaden said. The static around his voice roughened, then thinned again. "If the gate fails while we're all congratulating ourselves elsewhere, it becomes a much shorter book."

Elijah pushed one shoulder off the wall and forced himself straighter. "Pebble led me through two bad splits and one chamber that kept trying to put the same turn in front of me twice. Portal's west of here. Or where west has been pretending to be useful."

Eli's attention sharpened. "You can read route drift now?"

"Only when it makes my head feel like someone is filing the inside of it," Elijah said. He looked at Eli once more, quick and involuntary. "That got worse when you got close."

Rook chose not to dignify that with analysis.

"Fine," he said. "You stay with Pebble. Eli, take seam-side. We move before the corridor decides reunion scenes are a luxury item."

They did.

*

Sarah had stopped trying to make the tablet behave and had settled for making it confess.

There was a difference.

Something in the local stone kept teaching the screen new forms of dishonesty every twelve steps. Stable lines drifted. Clean markers doubled briefly, then corrected. Raw signal was the only part that still had the decency to lie badly.

Avery walked ahead of her through a narrow route that looked worked, abandoned, and recently offended all at once. He had one hand on the wall again, not superstitious and not careless, reading the place with the backs of his fingers as if the seams were a language too rude to speak aloud.

He stopped so abruptly Sarah nearly walked into him.

"What?" she said.

Instead of answering, he crouched and touched two fingers to a deep score in the stone beside the route. Not a seam-mark. Not tool work. Four parallel gouges, sunk hard enough to peel grit loose from the wall.

"Big," he said.

Sarah's tablet gave a short corrective pulse. One signal ahead. One close. Familiar.

Then Dr. Avery Chen came around the bend with his scanner in one hand and fatigue written cleanly through the set of his shoulders. For one second Sarah forgot the route, the gouges, the local Avery at her side, everything except the fact that her husband was upright and moving under his own power.

Sarah said his name before she could help it. "Avery."

He looked up at once. Relief crossed his face too fast to be anything but real.

Dr. Avery answered at once. "Sarah."

She closed the distance in four quick steps and caught his wrist rather than throwing herself at him, because the Continuum had taught all of them to mistrust balance and because relief was easier to manage when given a job. Warm skin. Solid pulse. Real.

"You took your time," she said.

"The geometry objected." His eyes flicked past her shoulder and stopped on the man behind her. Not confusion. Recognition of impossibility. "I assume that's not me."

"No," Sarah said.

Avery rose.

The two men looked at each other with the kind of attention usually reserved for live ordnance and unfamiliar mathematics.

"Dr. Avery," Avery said.

Dr. Avery's mouth tightened by a fraction. "Of course."

"Could be worse," Avery said. "At least you're handsome."

There was no room for more than that. A low scrape sounded somewhere below the shelf, followed by the small tumble of loosened stone moving under too much weight.

Sarah turned toward it at once. For a moment she saw nothing but broken braces, pale seam-light, and the lower cut of rock.

Then something broad moved behind a snapped line of stakes. Not clearly enough to name. A rise of mass. A dark-backed shape sliding between worked stone and shadow, low and deliberate before it vanished again.

Avery had already drawn back from the edge. "Move."

Dr. Avery took one look at the gouges in the wall and did not argue.

Sister Orin had remained at the portal exactly as promised, holding the site with the kind of certainty Sarah was beginning to understand was less temperament than profession. They left her there and kept west.

"You do that," Sarah said to Avery, more to keep herself thinking than because the question mattered, "like it's giving you minutes of warning."

"Seconds," Avery said.

"Comforting," Sarah said.

"No," Avery said. "Useful."

She checked the raw tracker. One signal still feathered at the edges where Rook refused to behave like a single body. One singular hard point still marked Kaden's presence with the kind of certainty she distrusted on principle. And farther west, Elijah's signal had stopped drifting.

"They found him," Sarah said.

Avery glanced back once. "Or he found them."

Before she could answer, static cracked in her ear and Kaden's voice came through in pieces. She caught Rook's name. Elijah's. Something about route pressure. Then the channel thinned again, losing whole syllables to the Continuum's indifference.

Sarah touched the comm harder than necessary. "Kaden. Repeat."

No answer.

Then, faintly: "Still working."

Working.

Not fine. Not stable. Not all right.

Sarah looked at the tracker again and realized she had started reading his signal the way people checked the pulse of an injured friend they were pretending not to worry about.

"He sounds worse," she said.

Avery did not answer immediately. They passed through a brace-cut arch where the stone seemed to hold its shape out of habit rather than confidence.

"He sounded wrong earlier too," Avery said at last. "This is more."

Dr. Avery glanced at the raw signal display in Sarah's hand. "If Orin loses the portal before we get back, none of this matters."

"She won't," Avery said.

Sarah looked up. "Confidence or data?"

"Sister Orin," Avery said, as if that answered both.

Sarah exhaled through her nose. "That's a deeply unscientific assessment."

"You keep saying that like it will make bad places respect your field."

She would have objected if he had not already been right too many times.

The route widened suddenly into a worked shelf overlooking a lower cut of stone. Sarah stopped at once.

Below them, the Continuum opened in a way that made the tablet's rendering lag behind what her eyes were seeing. Broken survey braces. Old marker stakes snapped off at the base. Pale seam-light in the cracks like frost trapped beneath rock. And everywhere, signs of use. Deliberate cuts. Repaired joins. Old field logic.

She looked from the ruins to the man beside her.

"You have locator stakes," she said.

"Yes," Avery said.

"Signal anchors," Sarah said.

"In a manner of speaking," Avery said.

Sarah held up the tablet. "These use identity lock and return-state triangulation."

Avery looked at the screen, then at the old stakes below. "Those use place memory and route agreement."

For the first time since the counterpart revelation, the shock that moved through her was not personal.

It was structural.

Different language.
Same shape.

"You've been solving the same problem," Sarah said quietly.

Avery's mouth moved by a fraction. "I was beginning to suspect your people had terrible habits for a reason."

The comm hissed again.

This time it was Rook.

"Sarah."

She answered at once. "Here."

"How fast can you get west?" Rook asked.

There was something under the question. Not panic. Worse. Effort being used to prevent panic from becoming contagious.

Sarah's grip tightened on the tablet. "Why?"

Rook's pause was brief and therefore terrible.

"Because speed has become medical."

Sarah was already moving.

*

The westward route got meaner by design.

Not randomly. Not in the decorative way some dangerous places used cruelty as a substitute for character. This stretch of the Continuum had been worked, abandoned, and then partially reclaimed by failure. Old supports jutted from walls at angles that implied previous geometry. Chalk marks ended where stone had later learned contempt. One side passage opened into a clean black slit that seemed less excavated than omitted.

Pebble kept them off the worst options.

That alone was reason enough not to waste time pretending the animal was incidental.

He would run ahead, vanish in a dim cut where seam-light blurred, then reappear and stand in whichever path had not yet decided to kill them efficiently. Eli trusted him with the ease of repetition. Elijah, who had started the last hour believing he had named the creature himself into importance, kept giving him looks that bordered on anthropological.

Rook stayed between Eli and Elijah whenever the corridor allowed it.

He never announced why.

Once, when the passage narrowed enough that they had to bunch, Elijah's breath caught and he winced at once. Eli's hand went to his temple in the same beat. Rook shifted left as if by accident, took more of Elijah's weight than the man required, and said, "Walk staggered. Whatever this place is doing, it likes proximity too much."

Neither argued.

Kaden's voice came and went in thinning threads.

At first he still sounded like himself, merely filed down by interference.

Then the delay got worse.

He would answer half a beat too late, as if first needing to gather himself from a distance he had not been asked to travel. Sometimes the response arrived clipped short, not because the signal lost it but because he had chosen economy over whatever pain longer speech required.

"Portal line still holds," he said once.

Later: "Sarah's close. Avery too."

Later still, after a bad shear in the corridor had forced them through a sloping cut of broken stone where Pebble's paws found purchase faster than any human boot: "Try not to make me track you one by one."

Rook recognized the strain for what it was because he had heard it before.

Not here.

Elsewhere.

White rooms. Clean language. Men who talked about tolerance thresholds like the body was a material that ought to be grateful for being tested to failure by specialists.

He touched the comm during one of the longer lulls. "Kaden."

Static.

Then, low enough to suggest the others were no longer meant to hear: "You pick your timing badly."

"You sound terrible."

"I've sounded terrible for years. You're only promoting it because we're in a ruin."

Rook stepped around a fractured brace and let the others gain several paces ahead. Eli noticed and did not slow. Another kindness disguised as practicality.

"How much is it costing you?" Rook asked.

Silence stretched. Not absence. Decision.

When Kaden answered, the line had gone flatter and more intimate all at once.

"Holding the connection open feels," he said, then stopped, not from indecision but because the truth had evidently caught in places he preferred not to expose. "It feels too much like the tests."

Rook's hand tightened around the receiver.

Ahead, Pebble barked once. Eli answered something he did not catch.

Kaden went on before he could stop him.

"Every time I force the line stable it feels like they're doing it again. Like they found a way to turn my nervous system into equipment and never finished putting it back." He laughed once, sharply, with no humor in it. "I can feel where they taught me to stay open."

He took one rough breath and kept going anyway.

"You were there for the worst of it," Kaden said. "You watched them keep going after it should have stopped. And when it got bad enough that even you moved, they put you on the floor and held you there like you were the problem."

Rook shut his eyes once.

That part had never stopped existing.

Hands on his shoulders. One arm twisted back hard enough to numb the hand. Kaden half-upright in the chair, not screaming by then, which had been worse. Somebody saying procedure through clenched teeth as if the word could make cruelty clinical.

Kaden's voice came back through the static, thinner now. "You still think if you'd fought harder, you'd have gotten me out before any of it stayed. I know."

He drew one shallow breath.

"I don't."

Rook said nothing.

Because that was the ugliest part. Not that he had tried and lost. That some violent, unfinished corner of him still believed that if he had broken one more wrist, shattered one more instrument, bitten through one more orderly answer, he could have stopped the part of Kaden that never came back.

"Kaden," he said quietly.

"No, let me say one thing while I still-"

"You don't owe me a better version of what I already know."

The line went still.

Rook looked down at the dust around his boots because some reflex in him still thought guilt should have posture to match.

"I should have ended it before they got that far," he said.

Kaden made a tired sound on the other end that might, in gentler circumstances, have been affection.

"You tried to slow it," he said.

"I failed."

"Yes," Kaden said.

There was no cruelty in it. Only fact.

Rook closed his eyes for one beat.

When he opened them again, the corridor was still there, waiting to be crossed rather than admired.

"Stay alive long enough for me to be useful about this later," he said.

"Sentimental for you," Kaden said.

"Ruinous, honestly."

When the line broke a few seconds later, it did not feel terminal.

Only expensive.

Rook put the comm away and caught up to the others just as Pebble stopped so abruptly that Eli nearly stepped into him.

The corridor ahead widened around a ruined post that had once been a holding node. Stone shelf on the left. Broken anchor irons on the right. Deep scratch marks across the wall at hip height, long enough to be old and deep enough to have remained visible despite several attempts by time to deny them.

Eli went still.

"That's not route damage," he said.

Elijah followed the line of his gaze and felt, rather than heard, the answer arrive.

Not through feedback.

Through animal history.

Something that had claimed the place hard enough for the stone to remember.

Pebble lowered himself at once, fur lifting along the spine, mouth closed.

Rook's eyes moved to the broken anchor irons. Bent outward. Not corroded through. Torn.

"Name?" he asked.

Eli's expression had gone thin in a way that meant the world currently deserved very little from him except competence. "Braceback."

"That's a terrible name."

"It's a terrible animal."

Elijah looked from one to the other. "How terrible?"

Then the answer stood up.

It had been half-hidden against the far wall, broad enough and still enough to pass briefly for collapsed ruin. When it moved, the chamber seemed to recoil from its certainty.

Bear was the closest ordinary word and not close enough.

It came up heavy on forelimbs built like impact tools, shoulders humped under cracked stone plating that ran from skull to spine in overlapping slabs. Between those plates coarse dark hair rose in ridges, thickest around the neck and flanks, mineral dust caught in it like old ash. Pale seam-lines glimmered faintly in the cracks when it breathed. Its head was massive, jaw a little too long, eyes small and set wrong in a face that looked assembled for force rather than grace.

The Braceback lowered its head.

Pebble gave a low sound that was not fear and not challenge.

Warning.

Eli did not take his eyes off the creature. "Territorial. Fast in bursts. Don't let it pin the corridor."

Rook looked once to the side routes, once to the broken post, and understood the shape of the problem almost at the same instant Eli did.

"It wants us into the right cut."

"Yes," Eli said.

"Why?"

Eli's mouth twitched without humor. "Because it lives here and isn't eager for discussion."

The Braceback charged.

Not immediately.

Worse.

First it covered three paces in a pace and reminded every human present that mass did not preclude speed if the world had been built badly enough. Then it hit the broken shelf where Rook had expected it to hesitate and instead used the rubble like a staircase designed by violence.

"Left!" Eli snapped.

They moved as one body and still barely made it.

The Braceback slammed through the space Elijah had occupied a second earlier and tore a support remnant free from the wall in passing. Stone exploded. Pebble darted low across the creature's front with infuriating accuracy, not to injure, only to turn its attention badly.

It worked.

Briefly.

The beast wheeled after him, shoulders grinding against stone, seam-lines brightening in the plates along its back.

Rook grabbed Elijah by the sleeve and shoved him toward the left passage.

"Move."

Elijah moved.

The route, offended by urgency, gave them a narrowing run of broken floor and one brace slot missing half its brace. Eli saw the geometry before the others did.

"Rook," he said, already angling for the wall. "If it commits here, the shelf goes under its left side. Keep it high. Pebble, off."

Pebble vanished from under the monster's line as if the command had always belonged to him.

Rook got one hand on the fractured support and put the other on Elijah's shoulder just hard enough to redirect him through the narrowest safe part of the cut. The Braceback hit the threshold behind them with enough force to shake dust from the ceiling.

Eli drove his boot into the loosened wedge-stone at the base of the support.

The shelf dropped.

Not enough to bury the creature.
Enough to ruin its first answer.

One forelimb punched through.
Stone gave.
The Braceback snarled, a sound like rockfall taught to hate.

"Go!" Eli snapped.

They went.

For three breaths the plan held.

Then the damaged brace tore farther than Eli had asked it to.

The dropped shelf collapsed entirely under the Braceback's weight, and the beast came through in a spray of stone and rage with one side bloodied and its speed somehow made meaner by the injury instead of less.

Elijah turned at the wrong time.

Not from stupidity.

From the human error of checking whether everyone else had made the gap.

The Braceback hit the last stretch in a single violent burst and caught him across the leg.

Rook saw claw first.
Then cloth.
Then blood.

Elijah's shout cut off almost immediately as his leg went out from under him. He hit the stone hard, both hands out, face empty with shock for a fraction of a second before pain got there and finished the job.

Blood sheeted down his trouser leg at once.

Too fast.

Rook was moving before the thought completed itself.

Eli got to Elijah's side first, stopped dead a pace short of contact as if the world had physically reminded him of the feedback problem, then rerouted into usefulness with brutal speed.

"Left wall!" he shouted. "Rook, up. Pebble, keep it off the mouth."

The Braceback hit the edge of the cut again, slowed this time by broken footing and its own fury. Pebble streaked across the opening and vanished into a side shadow, dragging its attention half an inch off center. Half an inch was enough for bad places and good decisions alike.

"Can you stand?" Rook asked.

Elijah laughed once, wild and thin. "That seems optimistic."

Blood was running through Rook's fingers now, hot and immediate.

This was no longer a tactical problem.

It was arithmetic.

Too much blood. Too little time.

Rook got one arm under Elijah's back and another beneath his knees, then changed strategy halfway through because the leg made that impossible without cruelty. He hauled him up across his shoulders instead, Elijah making a raw sound that Rook was going to remember later in worse hours than this one.

"Sorry," he said.

"You are deeply forgiven if we live."

Eli backed into the lead position at once. "This way. Don't crowd me."

The Braceback came again.

Rook ran.

There was no heroism in it. Only a man carrying too much weight through a corridor that kept offering choices designed by something with contempt for blood.

Elijah bled down his back. Not steadily. In pulses. That was the part Rook hated most.

Eli did not look behind him. He listened to the seams, to Pebble's quick corrections, to the places where the route still believed in passage if treated with enough disrespect.

"Right," he said.
"Low."
"Step over the cut."
"Not there."

Once, Elijah's hand spasmed against Rook's chest where it had hooked instinctively for balance.

"Rook."

Too quiet.

"Still here," Rook said.

"Good."

Eli heard the blood before he let himself look at it.

Not with his eyes. With the old field math in him that counted time in breaths and distance in who would die first if he chose badly.

He cut them through a narrow side break where the wall pinched enough to force the Braceback to reconsider its mass as an argument rather than a privilege. Pebble met them there, spun once, and shot back the way they had come just to make sure the creature followed the larger motion and not the correct one.

The corridor turned.

Light changed.

Voices ahead.

Sarah came around the bend fast enough to count as collision if Avery had not caught her by the arm a half second beforehand.

What she saw was not a reunion.

It was Rook carrying Elijah under enough blood to make every other fact about the scene secondary.

"Down," she said at once. "Now."

Rook obeyed without question.

The moment Elijah's weight shifted off him, the man folded badly toward the wall, consciousness hanging on from what looked like principle more than physiology.

Sarah was already on her knees.

"Pressure kit," she snapped.

Dr. Avery Chen came around the opposite side of the split route a heartbeat later, scanner still in hand. For one split second everything hit at once: the same face twice, the same startled pause, the same impossible resemblance trying to matter in the middle of blood loss.

Then Sarah looked up once and cut through both of them with, "Later."

That ended it.

Dr. Avery handed over gauze and seal-wrap from his field kit. Avery knelt opposite Sarah without waiting to be asked and got both hands under Elijah's ruined leg in the exact angle required to keep the wound open enough to work and closed enough not to worsen it. Rook saw Sarah notice that, catalog it, and move on.

Eli stayed a little back, breathing hard, one hand on the wall, Pebble pressed against his side.

Elijah's eyes opened once and found him.

For a beat the resemblance between them landed again, now with blood and exhaustion distorting all the clean lines. Dr. Avery saw it this time. So did Sarah.

No one said anything.

Sarah cut the torn fabric, saw the full damage, and went still only long enough for the terror of it to become process.

"He's lost too much," she said. "Not all of it from one source. Good. Very good. That means we're still having a conversation."

Elijah made a weak protesting sound. "I was under the impression-"

"Quiet."

He obeyed.

She packed the wound fast and hard. Elijah's hands clenched on empty air. Rook had the appalling instinct to move closer and then remembered he was wearing half the man's blood already, which felt like enough participation in suffering for one minute.

Avery did not flinch under the pressure required to hold the leg steady. His face had gone flat with the kind of concentration Sarah trusted more than comfort.

Dr. Avery crouched near Elijah's head, checking pupils, breathing, coherence, because no decent emergency ever arrived alone.

"Stay with me," he said.

Elijah looked at him, then blinked as if the face in front of him had chosen a particularly unhelpful time to become complicated.

"There's... an unfair amount of you now," he murmured.

Dr. Avery might have laughed in another life.

"You can complain about it later."

Sarah sealed the dressing, bound it tight, then pressed again to test what reality had left them. Less blood now. Not none. But less.

She let out one careful breath.

"He's not dying," she said.

The sentence hit the corridor harder than any shouted order had.

Not dying.

Not safe.
Not well.
Not remotely stable.

But alive enough for hope to risk being admitted into the room.

Rook sat down against the wall before his knees made that decision publicly for him. Eli slid down opposite without ever taking his eyes fully off Elijah. Pebble lay between them like an old answer they had not yet earned.

For a minute the group had something close enough to relief that nobody was willing to insult it by naming it.

Dr. Avery cleaned blood off his hands with the kind of aggression that suggested he was doing it for reasons deeper than hygiene. Sarah checked the wrap again. Avery looked down the route behind them as if expecting the Braceback to return with paperwork.

Elijah breathed.

That was the whole miracle.

Rook became aware, slowly and with resentment, that he was still listening for Kaden under every other sound.

The comm broke the quiet before he could decide whether to call.

"Rook."

Kaden sounded as though the distance between worlds had started charging interest.

Rook had the receiver up in an instant. "Here."

"Everyone together?"

Sarah answered before he could. "Enough of us. Elijah's alive."

On the other end of the line, something like relief went quiet instead of loud.

"Good," Kaden said.

That one word cost him enough that everyone heard it.

Sarah looked up sharply. Dr. Avery stilled. Even Eli lifted his head.

Rook knew before the next sentence arrived that it would matter.

"I don't think," Kaden said, and had to stop long enough to find breath again, "I don't think I feel well enough to keep-"

The line broke.

Not cleanly.

It collapsed mid-word in a burst of static so abrupt it took the room a second to understand what had happened and another second to choose fear over denial.

"Kaden?" Sarah snapped.

Nothing.

She hit the comm again. "Kaden."

Nothing.

Rook was already on his feet.

"He told me earlier he was hurting," he said.

Every eye in the corridor turned toward him.

He gave them only what they were allowed to have.

"Holding the connection together was costing him more than he let on."

Sarah's expression changed. Not softer. Sharper. She looked down at the tablet in her hand, then at the old survey hardware hanging from Avery's belt and the marker stakes built into the worked stone around them.

The realization arrived all at once.

"We don't need comms," she said.

Dr. Avery frowned. "What?"

"We're already together," Sarah said. She looked at Avery. "Your people use local anchors."

Avery understood her before she finished the sentence. "To keep a route agreeing with itself."

"And ours uses identity lock to force a return-state through noise." Sarah turned the tablet in one hand, already thinking past the failure. "Kaden still reads as one signal. Singular. If we stop trying to talk to him and just find the signal-"

Avery was unclipping a narrow survey brace from his belt before she finished. "Your device follows a body. This follows where a place is willing to admit a body passed through."

Dr. Avery looked from one to the other. "Those are not the same thing."

"No," Sarah said. "They're the same problem."

Rook watched that land too.

Not the personal uncanny this time.

The larger one.

Different worlds. Different language. The same architecture beneath both.

Sarah dropped to one knee, set the tablet flat, and pulled a signal lead free with less ceremony than the hardware probably deserved. Avery knelt across from her and laid the brace beside it. The thing was old metal and memory-work, forked at one end, etched shallow with lines Sarah would have dismissed as decorative an hour ago and now recognized as instructions in another dialect.

"If this shorts my system," Dr. Avery said, "I would like it recorded that I objected."

"Object now," Sarah said. "Useful later."

He shut up and handed her a spare cell.

Avery pressed two fingers to the brace and held it there until the seam-lines in the old metal brightened faintly, not with power exactly but with agreement.

Sarah spliced the lead.

For one second nothing happened.

Then the tablet screen tore itself sideways into static and came back with a single narrow spike punching through the noise.

Gone.

Then back again, weaker.

Not a line.
Not a map.

A burst.

Sarah stared.

"Again," Sarah said.

Avery adjusted the brace by fractions, turning it not toward geography but toward some local argument the route was making about where presence could still be found honestly.

The spike returned.

West-southwest.

Gone.

Returned.

Farther down.
Lower than they were.
Close enough to matter.

Unstable enough to be lost at any time.

Dr. Avery leaned in. "Can we hold it?"

"No," Sarah said.

Avery's mouth tightened. "But we can chase it."

The burst faded again, leaving only the afterimage of certainty badly earned.

"When we can send anything again," Sarah said, already on her feet, "Orin gets one line only: hold the portal. We find Kaden before we make it back to her."

Elijah pushed himself upright with obvious effort. "Please tell me this is the part where nobody recommends rest."

Sarah looked at him as if considering whether medicine extended to murder.

"You are not walking."

Rook stepped forward immediately. "Fine."

Eli got one hand under Pebble's jaw and looked at the corridor where the signal had briefly existed. His face had gone back to that hard useful stillness the Continuum seemed to reward by not killing outright.

"If the burst drops," he said, "the route still has to remember where a man fell."

Avery rose with the brace in one hand and the tablet in the other as if neither properly belonged to him and both were currently his problem anyway.

"Then we move before it forgets."

The tracker spat one last faint pulse.

There.

Then nowhere.

Sarah took the tablet back, stared once at the dead screen, and did not say what everyone had already thought:

If that was the last clean lock, they might already be too late.

No one needed it voiced.

The corridor ahead was enough.
