# FRAME 13

The front bandit moved first.

That was his second bad choice.

His first had been looking at Orin's cord and deciding he understood what it meant.

He came in low with the hook tool angled toward Avery's bundle, not toward Avery. That told Avery enough. The man did not want a fight. He wanted food, medicine, and the kind of victory that could be carried away quickly before the village remembered how many people had watched.

Avery put his boot against the medicine bundle and slid it back against the storage wall.

The hook caught cloth.

Not the bundle.

Avery had already let his torn outer sleeve hang loose from his wrist. The hook snagged it, pulled, and gave the front bandit exactly half a breath to believe he had Avery by the arm.

Avery stepped in with the pull.

He drove his shoulder into the man's chest, caught the hook haft with both hands, and turned it down hard. The tool struck the stone between them. The bandit's fingers opened because fingers were not built to argue with leverage.

The woman with the field knife moved then.

Eli saw her before Avery did.

"Left," Eli said.

Not shouted.

Certain.

Too certain.

Avery saw the knife woman's wrist begin to turn, blade coming backward along her forearm, exactly where Eli had already placed it with that single word. Eli's eyes had gone too still. His body was not moving to dodge. It was arranging around the attack as if the attack had already happened and he had only to stand where the result required him.

For one narrow moment, the whole path seemed to wait for Eli's answer.

Eli's hand opened.

Empty.

Then his jaw tightened, and he took one ordinary step back.

The knife passed where he had been.

Only where he had been.

Orin's cord snapped across the woman's ankles before she could recover. The pale line caught, the square weight kissed stone, and the woman's feet went out from under her with humiliating efficiency. She hit the path hard enough to lose the knife and most of her certainty.

"That was graceful," Rook said, "if one ignores nearly all of it."

The rope-weight bandit swung at his head.

Rook leaned aside without looking impressed. "I was speaking to her."

The bandit swung again, angrier and wider.

Rook stepped inside the arc, tapped two fingers against the man's elbow, and said, "This is where your arm regrets you."

The man's arm did.

Rook was already gone by the time the rope weights struck the storage wall. One weight cracked stone paste from a seam. The other looped around its own cord and trapped the man's wrist in a knot that had not existed a moment earlier.

Rook looked at the knot.

"Useful," he said, mildly surprised.

The club carrier rushed Orin.

Orin did not hurry.

She lifted her cord with one hand, not like a weapon and not like a prayer. Like a person lifting a known tool from a known place. The club came down. Orin turned her wrist. One square weight struck the club just above the grip. Another wrapped the man's forearm. The third hit his shoulder with a soft, final sound.

The man sat down.

It looked less like falling than being assigned to the ground.

"Stay," Orin said.

He stayed.

The youngest bandit had a small blade after all.

He had hidden it poorly because fear made hands honest. It came out of his belt bright and shaking. His eyes were not on Orin or Avery. They were on Eli.

Avery started toward him.

Eli moved first.

Not far.

Not strangely.

He stepped into the boy's reach with his hands visible and empty. The boy flinched backward before Eli touched him. His heel struck a loose wedge of stone at the path edge. For one ugly heartbeat, his weight tipped toward the drop.

Eli caught the front of his coat.

The boy made a small animal sound.

Eli pulled him forward and put him hard against the storage wall.

"Do not make me save you twice," Eli said.

The boy dropped the knife.

The last bandit had enough sense to stop moving.

It was a late sense, but Avery respected timing when it arrived before more blood.

Wind slipped through the narrowed path. The village behind them had gone so still that even the cistern ticks seemed indecently loud.

The front bandit pushed himself up on one elbow.

Avery put the hook tool against the stone with his boot and looked down at him.

"No," Avery said.

The man looked at the hook. Then at Avery.

"That was not a question," Avery added.

Rook crouched beside the rope-weight bandit and untangled the man's wrist only far enough to bind both hands properly with the same cord.

"I would like it noted," Rook said, "that I have improved your equipment."

The bandit spat at him.

Rook leaned back just enough.

"And your aim remains a local tragedy," Rook replied.

"Quiet," Orin said.

Rook tied the knot and became quiet with the injured dignity of a man punished for excellence.

They bound the bandits with their own wraps and cords. Not cruelly. Not comfortably. The difference mattered, but not enough to take more time than necessary.

Avery checked the medicine bundle first.

Cloth intact. Bandage herb still tied. Water skins uncut. Rootvine meal and travel loaves dusty but usable.

Good.

He checked Eli second.

Eli saw it coming and gave him a look. "No."

"I have not asked," Avery said.

"You were arranging to," Eli said.

Rook made a pleased sound from behind the bound rope-weight man.

Avery did not look at him. "That has become less charming every time."

"Impossible," Rook said. "It began very low."

Orin stood over the front bandit.

The man had stopped smiling. His face looked smaller without it. Not younger. Only less armored.

"You know the east road is needed," Orin said.

The man wiped blood from a split lip with the back of his bound hand. "Everyone needs roads."

"You chose this one," said Orin.

"Road chose us first," the man said.

Orin's expression did not change. "No."

The word landed harder than anger would have.

The front bandit looked past her toward the village, where people had found work again with trembling hands. A woman at the drying rack tied the same bundle of leaves three times. Tavin was gone from behind the cistern pier. Avery hoped that meant sense had finally reached his feet.

Orin crouched so the front bandit had to meet her eyes.

"Listen carefully," Orin said. "You will stay bound until the village decides what to do with you. If you cut loose and follow, I will leave you somewhere the road can make an honest judgment."

The man gave a short laugh. It had no humor in it.

"Listen to her," Rook said from behind Avery. "She has a gift for finding unfriendly places."

The front bandit's eyes shifted to Rook, then back to Orin.

Something in his face changed.

Recognition had been there before. Avery had seen it in the first pause, the way the man's eyes had touched the pale cord and chosen wrong. This was older. Less certain. More personal.

"Still giving orders," the front bandit said.

Orin did not move.

The man smiled then, but it was not the smile he had worn while standing. This one cost more.

"Vey," he said.

The name did not fit the air.

Eli looked up.

Rook's mouth closed.

Avery watched Orin's hand.

Her fingers did not twitch. The cord did not move. That was how Avery knew the hit had landed.

"Dain," Orin said.

The front bandit's smile thinned at the sound of his own name.

The front bandit leaned his head back against the storage wall. "Or is it Orin now, traitor?"

No one spoke.

Behind them, a pot clanged somewhere in the village. The sound came too late and too ordinary.

Orin stood.

"Do not follow," she said.

That was all.

She turned east.

For a moment, Avery thought Eli would ask immediately. He did not. He bent, picked up the basket he had carried from Mara's second shed, and settled the strap over his shoulder. His face had gone careful in a way Avery disliked more than open fear.

Rook retrieved the medicine bundle and offered it to Avery without comment.

Avery took it.

They left the bandits bound at the bend, visible from the village and far enough from the drop that even foolishness would need help killing them.

No one from the village called after them.

That felt like judgment.

Or mercy.

In Anchorfall, those were beginning to share tools.

*

The east road began as an argument between generations of repair.

Old stone steps lay under newer bracewood. Newer bracewood had been pinned through older anchor plates. Ropes ran along the inner wall in doubled lines, one sun-faded, one recently tarred, each pretending the other was unnecessary. Chalk marks had been refreshed in some places and left to ghost away in others. Every twenty paces, someone had tried to make the road safer.

Every twenty paces, the road had answered with a different opinion.

Orin walked first.

Avery let her.

That, too, was becoming a habit.

Rook walked last now, because he had decided the road behind them deserved suspicion. Eli stayed between Avery and Orin with one hand on the inside rope and the other close to nothing. Not the flask. Not a tool. Nothing.

Avery noticed.

Eli noticed him and looked irritated by the care it took to say nothing.

Good.

The silence lasted until the village had turned into smoke and angled roofs behind them.

Then Eli asked, "Vey?"

Orin did not stop.

The wind moved hard across the road and pressed her robe against the old cord at her wrist.

"A dead name," she said.

Rook looked at the cliff wall. "Names are usually more cooperative when buried."

The joke was soft.

It still failed.

Orin stopped.

Rook stopped too.

He looked at her face, then down at the road. "I withdraw that."

"No," Orin said. "You spoke it. Let it stand."

Rook's mouth tightened. He did not answer.

Avery shifted the medicine bundle higher. The path here was wide enough to stand, but not wide enough for comfort. A repaired rope rail ran along the outer side, threaded through anchor rings that had seen better judgment.

Eli kept his hand on the inner rope. "He knew you."

"Yes," Orin said.

"From before Sister Orin?" Eli asked.

Orin looked at the road ahead.

For a moment, Avery saw something that was not memory exactly. Or perhaps it was only his mind filling the shape her silence left behind.

A younger woman on a broken road with blood on her sleeve.

Hands tying knots around stolen supplies.

People laughing too loudly near a fire because fear had been trained into swagger.

A name spoken like a thrown stone.

Vey.

"Before," Orin said.

Eli's fingers tightened around the rope. "Were you like them?"

That question should have been too blunt.

It was also the only honest one.

Orin turned her head enough to look at him. "Worse."

Eli did not step back.

Avery gave him credit for that.

Orin continued walking, and after a moment they followed.

"I led people who thought hunger made law optional," Orin said. "I thought strength could become order if it was placed in the right hands. Mine, mostly."

Rook said nothing.

That was how Avery knew he was listening hard.

The road bent around a fallen anchor post. Someone had marked the break with three chalk strokes and a cord loop. The cord had frayed halfway through.

Orin touched the post as she passed, not gently.

"We took from travelers," said Orin. "From storehouses. From crews that had more than we did. Sometimes they did. Sometimes they only looked as if they did from far away."

Eli's face had gone still.

Avery watched him more than Orin now.

"Then?" Eli asked.

Orin's eyes remained on the road. "Then I learned rules."

"That made you better?" Eli asked.

"No," Orin said. "It made me more dangerous first."

Another flash that might have been Avery's imagination: a chalk line drawn by firelight; rough hands learning clean knots; a man with a hook tool listening while a younger Orin showed him how to read an anchor's weakness; someone crying in the dark beyond the reach of the fire.

"I taught them road law," Orin said. "Marks. Anchors. Where to step. Where not to bleed. How to bind a shelf so it held long enough to cross. I thought knowledge would make them useful."

"Did it?" Rook asked quietly.

Orin looked at him. "Yes."

Rook's face changed by almost nothing.

"Useful for what?" Eli asked.

Orin's answer came after three steps.

"For hurting people with better discipline," Orin answered.

The words sat on the path like a dropped tool no one wanted to pick up.

Avery understood then why the front bandit had looked at her cord and chosen badly. He had not misunderstood Orin's authority. He had remembered a different kind of it.

"Some came back," Orin said. "Some took work. Anchor hands. Rope crews. Shelf repair. Hard work for bad backs and worse weather. Some learned the difference between taking from a road and keeping one alive."

She glanced back once, toward the village they had left.

"Some learned the knots and kept the old reasons," she said.

"The front man," Eli said.

"Yes," Orin said.

"And you blame yourself," Eli said.

Orin stopped so suddenly that Eli nearly walked into her.

"Do not soften it for me," she said.

Eli's chin lifted.

"I am not," Eli said.

"Then hear it cleanly," Orin said. "People died because of what I did. People died because of what I taught after I thought I had changed. A hand does not become clean because it later learns to bind wounds."

Avery expected Eli to answer quickly.

He did not.

That was new.

Orin studied him. Her expression shifted, not into softness, but toward something older and less guarded.

"There was a child," said Orin.

The road seemed to lose sound around the words.

Eli's eyes narrowed slightly.

"Yours?" Eli asked.

"No," Orin said.

That was all Orin gave him at first.

Wind moved grit along the stone. It gathered briefly in a crack, then scattered.

"A child I knew," Orin said. "From a village that should have been left alone."

Avery looked toward the west, though the route-hold was too far ahead to see.

Orin's eyes followed his for half a breath.

"It was not a hold then," she said.

The words made the road under Avery's boots feel older.

Orin looked back to Eli. "Her name was Neris. She heard things before the rest of us learned they were speaking. Roads. Seams. Old breaks under good stone. People feared what they could not measure with their hands."

Avery looked at Eli.

Eli was looking at Orin.

"What happened?" Eli asked.

Orin's mouth tightened.

Another fragment: a small hand pressed to stone; adults speaking too loudly; a chalk mark cut through before it finished drying; a child's voice saying something no one wanted to be true.

Then nothing.

"Enough," Orin said.

Eli looked away first.

Rook's voice came carefully from behind them. "That is a word people use when the rest is heavier."

Orin did not rebuke him.

"Yes," Orin said.

They walked again.

For several paces, only the road spoke: bracewood under boots, rope against rings, wind passing through broken bridge bones below.

Then Orin said, "What you did at the bend."

Eli's hand moved toward his flask and stopped halfway.

Avery saw it.

So did Orin.

"I did not do anything," Eli said.

"That is not true," Orin said.

Eli's face closed. "I did not use anything."

"Better," Orin said.

Eli frowned.

Orin kept her eyes forward. "The danger is not that you hear more than others. The danger is not that you are strange. The danger is letting the road decide your hands are available."

Eli did not answer.

"You saw the knife before the knife finished choosing itself," Orin said.

Avery filed that phrase away with immediate dislike.

Eli's voice went quieter. "It was just movement."

"No," Orin said. "It was invitation."

The road turned under an overhang where old water stains striped the stone black. Avery ducked beneath a low brace. Eli passed under it without seeming to notice.

"And I stopped," Eli said.

Orin looked back at him then.

"Yes," Orin said.

It was not praise.

It was weight.

Eli held her gaze for a moment, then nodded once.

Not happy.

Not comforted.

But steadier.

Avery accepted that as the best answer the road had allowed them today.

*

The east storehouse appeared behind a run of old anchor posts where the cliff road widened into a working yard.

It was not impressive.

That made Avery trust it more.

A low stone building had been set into the cliff face, half under overhang, half standing against weather. Its roof was weighted with flat slabs and crossed with bracewood bands. Two broad doors faced the yard, each reinforced with iron strips and painted with old route marks: frame line, store mark, salt warning, dry-hand sign. A narrow window near the roof had been sealed from inside with a board and three pins.

The left door was shut.

The right door was open by the width of a hand.

No one spoke for a moment.

Rook sighed. "I dislike when useful buildings develop opinions."

"It is not open enough for theft," Avery said.

Orin looked at the door gap. "No."

"Open enough for invitation," Eli said.

Avery looked at him.

Eli's hand was on the wall beside the yard entrance. His eyes were focused on the door, but not only the door.

"Not teeth," Eli said.

Rook looked relieved for half a breath.

Eli added, "Not yet."

Rook's relief left again.

Orin crossed the yard first. Avery would have objected, but there was no version of the objection she would have respected. He followed close enough to be useful. Eli came after him, then Rook, who watched the road behind them as if the bound bandits might grow better ideas in their absence.

The yard held practical things. Empty handcarts. Two broken brace frames. A coil rack with only three good coils left. A trough full of rainwater gone mineral-white around the edges. Nothing moved.

The storehouse door had not been forced with tools.

That was the problem.

The latch was still set. The seal cord still threaded through the ring. The chalk across the threshold was cracked cleanly down the center as if the building had inhaled and the line had failed to stretch with it.

Orin crouched.

"Route pressure," she said.

"From below?" Avery asked.

Orin touched the broken chalk without smearing it. "From wanting to be elsewhere."

"Buildings want that often?" Rook asked.

"Only when something teaches them," Orin answered.

Rook looked at the open door. "I withdraw the question."

Orin cut the seal cord rather than untie it. Avery noticed that.

"Bad cord?" he asked.

"Bad promise," Orin said.

She pushed the right door open.

It did not creak.

That was worse.

Inside, the storehouse smelled of salt, dry chalk, oil, old rope, and stone that had spent too long holding its breath.

Shelves lined the walls in ordered rows. Most were intact. Some leaned very slightly toward the center aisle. Crates sat in marked stacks: CHALK, HARD. SALT, ANCHORING. CORD, SPARE. PINS, IRON. WEDGES. CLAMPS, BRACE. A table near the back held small tools wrapped in cloth and labeled with neat black strokes.

At first glance, it was exactly what they needed.

At second glance, Avery saw the wrongness.

Dust had slid from several shelves in clean diagonal lines. A box of chalk had opened without spilling. A salt jar had cracked but not fallen apart, gray-white mineral caught in the fracture like the jar was deciding whether gravity still applied. Three brace clamps lay on the floor in a row too straight to be accidental.

The place had not been ransacked.

It had been considered.

Eli stopped at the threshold.

"No easy shelves," Avery said.

Eli gave him a thin look. "I was going to say that."

"I enjoy variety," Avery said.

"You enjoy being first," Eli replied.

"Field efficiency," Avery said.

Rook leaned around them. "Do we have a plan, or are we insulting the architecture until it yields?"

Orin stepped inside. "Both, if time permits."

They worked quickly.

Not hurried.

Hurried made hands stupid.

Avery took inventory from the nearest stable shelf while Orin checked threshold marks and Eli watched the aisles with his hands empty. Rook gathered cord, pins, and wedges, speaking softly to each crate as if politeness might keep them from collapsing.

"You are doing that deliberately," Avery said.

"Yes," Rook said.

"Why?" Avery asked.

"If a crate ruins my day after I have been courteous, that is on the crate," Rook said.

Eli almost smiled.

Almost was enough.

The hard chalk came in short wrapped sticks, denser than field chalk and less willing to crumble under damp fingers. Avery opened one packet and checked the grain. Good. Dry. Old, but not dead. He packed three bundles, then a fourth because the route-hold had already eaten more luck than it deserved.

The anchoring salt was stored in thick jars padded with straw and sealed in waxed cloth. Gray-white, speckled, heavier than the volume promised. Not kitchen salt. Not ritual salt only. Mineral with bite in it. The kind that made the air feel briefly clean when the jar opened.

Orin watched him weigh two jars in his hands.

"More," she said.

"Weight," Avery said.

"More," Orin repeated.

He packed more.

The salt went into cloth wraps, then into a crate, then into another because one crate should not be allowed to decide the whole day. Avery marked both with fresh chalk. SALT. KEEP DRY. DO NOT BLEED NEAR.

Rook read the last mark.

"That feels personal," Rook said.

"It is practical," Avery said.

"Those are often personal," Rook replied.

From outside came a distant scrape of stone.

Everyone stopped.

The scrape did not repeat.

Eli closed his eyes for one breath.

"Road settling," he said.

"Or?" Avery asked.

"Or road practicing," Eli said.

"That is less helpful," Avery said.

"Yes," Eli said.

They kept working.

Spare cord came down next. Good cord, route cord, waxed and marked at measured intervals. Rook approved of the knots on the storage loops with a seriousness Avery had not expected from him.

"You respect cord," Avery said.

Rook did not look over. "Cord does not pretend to be more than it is."

"You like that?" Avery asked.

"I envy it," Rook said.

Avery let that sit without comment.

Pins and wedges were easier. Iron pins in cloth rolls. Brace wedges in oiled boxes. Clamp screws wrapped separately so they would not bite through the bags. Orin chose the brace clamps herself, rejecting two that looked sound to Avery until she showed him the hairline split near the hinge.

"That would fail under a singing anchor," she said.

Avery put it aside.

"How many singing anchors are you expecting?" Rook asked.

Orin gave him a look.

Rook nodded. "Enough. Of course. Always enough."

At the back of the storehouse, Eli had gone still.

Avery set down the wedge roll.

"Eli," Avery said.

"I know," Eli said.

"That was not yet a question," Avery said.

"You were arranging to," Eli said.

Rook made a small sound. "It really does spread."

Eli did not smile this time.

He was looking at the cracked salt jar on the shelf.

The fracture in the clay had widened. Not much. Enough that a thin line of salt had spilled down the side and gathered on the shelf below. The grains formed a pale crescent around an old stain in the wood.

Blood, Avery thought.

Old enough to be brown.

Not enough to be safe.

Orin saw it too.

"Leave that jar," she said.

Rook's hand, already halfway toward it, stopped. "Gladly."

Eli stared at the crescent.

Avery moved between him and the shelf.

Eli blinked.

"I was not going to touch it," he said.

"I know," Avery said.

"Then why move?" Eli asked.

Avery looked at the salt, the stain, the way the shelf seemed to lean toward Eli by the width of a bad thought.

"Because I could," Avery said.

Eli's expression shifted.

He did not thank him.

Good.

They finished the load.

Two salt crates. One chalk crate. A bundle of cord. Pins, wedges, clamps, paste jars, spare weights for Orin's cord, oilcloth, three repair slates, and a small tin of black marking thread Orin took without explaining.

Avery did not ask.

He was learning.

They carried the first load into the yard.

The road outside looked the same.

That was not a comfort.

By the second load, Avery's shoulders had begun making their own arguments. By the third, Eli's face had gone pale around the mouth, though he moved without complaint. Rook took the heaviest cord bundle and pretended not to notice Eli noticing. Orin took the spare weights and one salt crate as if age had never negotiated with her spine.

They had everything stacked in the yard when the sound came from the west road.

Not stone.

Not wind.

Breathing.

Hard, uneven breathing, and the scrape of something heavy being urged too quickly along a road that objected.

Rook reached for his knife.

Orin lifted one hand.

"Wait," Orin said.

Around the bend came Tavin.

He was holding the lead strap of a Stoneback Hauler twice his width and many times his patience.

The creature moved low and broad, four thick legs set under a body plated in dull gray stone hide. Not armor exactly. More like the cliff had once considered becoming kind and stopped halfway. Its head was blunt, its dark eyes set wide and calm despite the hurry. A cart harness crossed its shoulders, empty traces dragging behind it. Chalk marks had been brushed along the harness straps in Mara's practical hand.

Tavin saw them and tried to stand straighter.

He ruined the effect by nearly falling over.

Eli was already moving.

Avery was closer.

He caught the boy by one shoulder before Tavin's knees finished deciding.

"Breathe," Avery said.

Tavin tried.

It came out broken.

"Mara sent you?" Orin asked.

Tavin nodded too fast. "She said you would need the hauler."

Rook looked at the stacked salt and chalk. "Mara has a gift for insulting our carrying capacity from a distance."

Tavin swallowed and tried to pull himself upright under Avery's hand.

"She said..." Tavin looked at the equipment, then toward the west road, then at Orin. "She said not to waste time arguing."

"Wise woman," Rook said.

Tavin did not smile.

Avery's hand tightened on his shoulder. "What else?"

The boy's throat worked.

For one second, he looked as young as he was.

Then he forced the words out like carrying them correctly mattered more than fear.

"Something found your hold," Tavin said.

The yard went very quiet.

Eli's hand moved.

Not to the flask.

To the nearest salt crate.

His fingers rested on the chalk mark Avery had written there: DO NOT BLEED NEAR.

Orin's face lost all expression.

"What did you see?" she asked.

"I did not see it," Tavin said quickly. Too quickly. "Not all of it. People came down from the upper road. One of the anchor crews. They said the hold started... answering wrong. The board moved. The walls made noise. Something big below the road." His breath caught. "Mara said take the hauler. She said if I stopped to be scared, she would be angry later."

Rook looked toward the west.

"Effective woman," Rook said, but the words had no lift.

Avery released Tavin's shoulder only when the boy could stand without him.

"Harness," Avery said.

Everyone moved.

No argument. No wasted questions. The route-hold was not a place they loved. It was a place with a board, walls, tools, wounded memory, and enough temporary safety that losing it would change the shape of every choice after.

That was close enough to love for the day.

They loaded the hauler fast.

The Stoneback accepted the weight with a low breath, not quite a groan. It shifted once under the salt crates, settled, and turned its head toward the west road before anyone touched the lead.

Tavin stroked the side of its neck with shaking fingers.

"It knows the way," Tavin said.

"Does it know why?" Rook asked.

Tavin looked at him. "They know enough."

Rook nodded once, accepting the correction.

Orin checked the harness marks. Avery checked the load straps. Eli checked the road ahead by not looking at it directly. Rook tied off the back trace and tested it with both hands.

"Good?" Avery asked.

"Good enough to become our problem later," Rook said.

"Then good," Avery said.

They started west.

The hauler moved faster than Avery expected for something built like a patient wall. Its feet found the road with heavy certainty. Not graceful. Better than graceful. Reliable. Where the path dipped, it took the inside line before Tavin guided it. Where a brace plank flexed, it paused long enough for everyone else to step past the weak edge. Twice, Avery felt the road seem to smooth under them, not changing exactly, only choosing not to be difficult for the length of one breath. Once, the air around the hauler's hooves thickened strangely, and three bad paces passed in the time Avery expected one.

Stoneback Hauler, he thought.

Not a cart animal.

A route animal.

The east road folded behind them. The storehouse disappeared around the bend. The equipment load creaked, salt jars knocking softly inside their crates.

No one spoke for a long time.

Then Eli called, "Tavin."

The boy looked over from the hauler's head.

"You did well," Eli said.

Tavin's face tried several expressions and failed at all of them.

"I was told not to stop," Tavin said.

"You did not," Eli said.

That seemed to matter.

Avery watched Eli after that. His hand stayed away from the flask. His eyes stayed on the road. Whatever Orin had said had not made him easier.

It had made him more deliberate.

Avery would take deliberate.

For now.

They reached the last long bend before the route-hold as the light began to thin behind cloud.

The road should have climbed there.

The hauler stopped before it did.

Not slowed.

Stopped.

Its feet spread against the stone. Its head lowered until the lead strap went slack in Tavin's hands. A shiver passed through the stone plates along its shoulders, one after another, like a hand moving under water.

Tavin whispered, "No."

Orin lifted her cord.

Eli went still beside the salt crates.

Avery looked ahead.

The road to the route-hold waited in its proper place.

For once, it did not pretend to be safe.

Then the ground answered from beyond the bend.

One deep impact.

Then another.

Not collapse.

Steps.

Rook's voice came very quietly from behind Avery.

"What in the world is that?"
