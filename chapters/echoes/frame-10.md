# FRAME 10
# OPEN DEBTS

"Sister Or—"

The sound cut.

Reality corrected.

No one spoke.

Avery didn't mark it.

Didn't name it.

But he felt it.

The pattern hadn't ended.

It had only—

paused.

Avery did not count the pause.

Counting made a shape. Counting made a record. Counting took a thing the corridor had not yet finished doing and gave it edges firm enough to argue with.

So he let the breath pass unmarked.

Sister Orin stood two paces ahead of him, one shoulder turned away from the line Eli had made by accident. The three of them did not face each other. That mattered. The corridor wanted them placed cleanly: one point, another point, another point, answer waiting in the middle.

Orin had broken it by the smallest possible movement.

Avery respected that despite himself.

He did not trust her.

Those were separate matters.

Eli had gone still in a way Avery disliked. Stillness did not suit him. Eli at rest was usually not resting at all, only moving his attention faster than his hands. This was different. This was the kind of stillness that followed a wound before pain decided what name to use.

"You're early," Orin had said.

The words stayed in the air after the sound was gone.

The corridor liked them.

Avery could feel that too.

Eli's hand found the flask at his hip.

He did not seem to decide to reach for it. The motion simply happened, quick and familiar, fingers closing around worn metal as if checking that one part of the world still had an honest edge.

For a moment he only held it.

Then he uncapped it and took a long swallow.

Too long for thirst.

When he lowered it, his breathing had steadied by a fraction.

Not the way Eli knew a route. Eli heard them in his bones and then pretended the rest of them were slow for needing tools. Avery had tools. He had training. He had enough old caution to know when a place was listening for the next foolish contribution.

He kept his hands away from his chalk.

Orin's eyes flicked to him.

"Good," she said.

Avery said nothing.

"Do not mark what has not yet chosen to be true," Orin said.

"That is not survey doctrine," Avery said.

"It is older than survey doctrine," Orin said.

Eli drew a breath that did not quite settle. "What did you mean?"

Orin did not look at him.

That, too, was deliberate.

"By which part?" Orin asked.

Eli's mouth tightened. "You know which part."

The corridor gave a small, pleased sound.

Not aloud.

Worse.

In the walls.

Stone settled where no stone had shifted. Seam-light thinned, then brightened, then arranged itself into three faint bars across the floor: one under Orin, one under Avery, one reaching for Eli's boots.

"Move," Orin said.

She did not raise her voice.

Eli moved anyway.

Not back. Not forward. Sideways, because Orin's hand cut once through the air and Eli had enough field sense left to obey the shape before his pride arrived to object.

The third bar of light slid past his boot and struck the opposite wall. For one thin second the mark there looked like a doorway seen edge-on.

Then it went flat.

Avery felt his jaw set.

"It is still active," Avery said.

"It is still hungry," Orin said.

"Those are not the same thing," Avery said.

"No," she said. "They are not. That is why you are alive."

Eli looked at the place where the light had missed him. His hands had started moving again, but badly. Two fingers tapped against his thumb in an irregular sequence, stopping each time the corridor seemed to notice.

"You said open doors are debts," Avery said.

Orin reached into the leather roll at her hip and drew out a length of pale cord threaded with small square weights. Each weight was marked on four faces. The fifth face of each had been scraped smooth.

"They are," Orin said.

"We did not open it," Avery said.

"No," Orin said.

That should have helped.

It did not.

Orin looped the cord around two fingers, measured the air between herself and the nearest wall, and frowned as if the corridor had been rude enough to answer incorrectly.

"You came close enough for it to remember your hands," Orin said.

Eli looked up. "That is not better."

"I was not improving it," Orin said.

Avery almost appreciated that.

Almost.

The corridor repeated itself in a subtler way this time. The left wall showed the faintest ghost of Orin's inclined head. Not fully. Not enough to claim. A movement without the body that had made it.

Avery looked away before his attention completed the image.

Orin saw.

"Follow where I step," she said. "Not where the route offers."

"To where?" Avery asked.

"A route-hold," Orin said.

The word was familiar in pieces. Route, of course. Hold, of course. Together it belonged to older work, sealwright work, the kind surveyors inherited after the people who built it were gone and the people who maintained it had stopped explaining why the original shape mattered.

Not a refuge first. A place where a bad route could be made to spend itself more slowly.

Avery had seen three in his career.

Two were dead.

The third had lied.

"How far?" he asked.

"Near enough if you do not argue with the wrong wall," Orin said.

Eli made a small sound that might have become a laugh in a better corridor.

Orin turned her head just enough to stop him from trying.

"No unnecessary answers," she said. "It will take them."

The corridor dimmed by one breath.

Avery did not count that either.

*

Orin walked as if the floor had already admitted its sins to her.

She did not move quickly. That was the first thing Avery had to force himself to accept. Every trained part of him wanted speed. Leave the active corridor. Break line of sight. Put distance between the failed Second Door and the bodies it had almost convinced into usefulness.

Orin chose pace instead.

Step.

Wait.

Angle.

Step.

At the first junction, the corridor offered three exits.

Avery knew there had been one.

The original route continued right, through a low arch where older survey marks cut shoulder-high along the stone. A second passage opened ahead, narrow and clean, with seam-light placed conveniently down the center like an invitation pretending not to know what invitations were. The third appeared to the left as they approached. It did not open so much as become remembered: a dark slice in the wall, damp air moving out of it, the mineral smell of a place that had seen water recently.

Eli's attention snapped left.

Avery caught it.

Orin caught it first.

"No," Orin said.

Eli stopped with one foot half-lifted.

"I didn't move," Eli said.

"You wanted to," Orin said.

"That is not usually a punishable offense," Eli said.

"It is not punishment." Orin took the pale cord and dropped one weighted end across the floor between Eli and the left-hand passage. The weight hit stone and stuck there as if the floor had briefly turned soft. "It is bait."

The damp passage sighed.

Avery looked at the shadow inside it and understood what had drawn Eli's attention. Not danger. Not safety. Recognition. The passage had offered the shape of a route Eli would choose on instinct: irregular footing, live air, a problem with enough teeth to feel honest.

"It knows him," Avery said.

Eli's face went carefully blank.

Orin did look at him then.

Not long.

Long enough.

"It knows the part of him that answers too quickly," Orin said.

"That is most of him," Avery said.

Eli shot him a look.

It was quick. Irritated. Normal enough that Avery felt some of the cold in his chest loosen.

Then the left passage spoke in Orin's voice.

"Sister Or—"

The sound cut itself off at the same broken place.

The cord trembled.

Orin's hand closed around the free end so hard the marks on her fingers whitened.

"Eyes down," she said.

Avery obeyed.

So did Eli.

Pride had uses. Survival had more.

Orin spoke one word Avery did not know.

"Keth," Orin said.

It had old edges. Not a command. Not a prayer. A naming of condition, perhaps. The kind of word built before trades separated themselves into clean professions and everyone understood that a bad threshold could be a weather event, a legal matter, and a predator all at once.

The weighted cord went still.

The left passage lost depth.

Not vanished. Not closed. Flattened, like a drawing badly remembered by stone.

Orin stepped over the cord and took the right-hand arch.

"Now," she said.

Avery followed.

Eli came after him, too close.

Avery did not comment on it.

For the next several turns the route behaved almost correctly. That was its worst attempt yet. The floor held. The walls kept their distance. Old survey cuts appeared where Avery expected them: paired notches for stable passage, a half-circle for low overhead compression, three short strokes warning against voice-carry through the next bend.

He had left some of those marks himself.

Not here.

That was the problem.

His hand drifted toward the wall.

Orin clicked her tongue once.

Avery stopped.

"That mark is mine," he said.

"No," Orin said.

"It is my cut," Avery said.

"Yes," Orin said. "That is not the same answer."

The words irritated him because they were useful.

Avery studied the mark without touching it. The angle was his. The depth was his. The slight correction in the second stroke, where his wrist always compensated for a nick in his field knife, was his as well.

The dust inside the cut was older than his boots.

Eli leaned forward despite himself. "Avery."

"I see it," Avery said.

"Do you?" Eli asked.

Avery almost answered sharply.

Then he heard what Eli had heard.

The mark was waiting.

Not visibly. Not in any way a report would tolerate. But there was pressure around it, a readiness, as if the wall had set a hook and now needed only the dignity of being believed.

Avery lowered his hand.

"Copied from memory," he said.

"Borrowed from debt," Orin said.

"How many words do you have for things trying to kill us?" Avery asked.

"Fewer than the things deserve," Orin said.

Eli breathed out through his nose.

This time it did become a laugh, barely.

The corridor tightened around the sound.

Orin stopped.

The laugh died.

For one moment all three of them stood with different amounts of regret.

Then the corridor ahead repeated the laugh back.

It did not sound like Eli.

That was worse than if it had.

Avery felt the route trying to place them again. Orin in front. Avery behind. Eli at the rear. Three points along one line now, not a triangle, but the pressure tested them, considering whether a line might be bent into what it wanted.

Orin lifted two fingers.

Avery knew field command when he saw it.

Hold.

She took one step back.

Not forward. Back.

The route hesitated.

Orin took another step, passing Avery without looking at him. Her sleeve brushed his coat. The seal-lines embroidered at her cuff were not decorative up close. They were repairs in thread: small geometric decisions anchored into cloth, worn pale where her hands had rubbed over them for years.

She stopped beside Eli.

The three of them no longer made a useful line.

The corridor gave a low, stone-deep creak.

"It wants completion," Avery said.

"All unfinished things do," Orin said.

"Not all unfinished things follow people," Avery said.

Orin looked down the corridor toward the arch they had not yet reached. "Enough of them."

Eli swallowed. "Am I early to it?"

Orin's face did not soften.

That would have been easier to dislike.

"You are early," she said, "to more than one thing."

Eli's eyes sharpened.

"That is an answer designed to be hated," Eli said.

"Then hate it while walking," Orin said.

Avery stepped between them before Eli could spend fear as defiance.

"What does he need to do?" Avery asked.

Orin's gaze moved to Avery.

Something passed there that was not quite approval. Recognition of a useful question, perhaps.

"Stop answering first," Orin said.

Eli opened his mouth.

Avery said, "Eli."

Eli shut it.

That was how Avery knew the words had landed hard.

Orin turned again. "The hold is close. The debt has learned enough of your shapes to follow. It has not learned enough to choose well."

"Then it can choose badly," Avery said.

"Yes," Orin said.

"And if it chooses badly around us?" Avery asked.

"Then I will try to make its mistake smaller than yours," Orin said.

It was not comforting.

It was, unfortunately, the most credible thing she had said.

*

The first sign of outside air arrived as pressure.

The corridor did not widen. It thinned.

The stone on the right lost weight by degrees, dark blocks giving way to narrow gaps where wind moved through and brought with it the smell of cold mineral rain, dry grass, old dust, and distance.

Eli noticed first.

Of course he did.

His head lifted.

Not toward a sound. Toward space.

Avery felt the pull a breath later. The route ahead sloped upward, steps cut too shallow for comfort, each one repaired at least twice. Brace slots lined the left wall. Some held fresh-cut supports. Others were empty, their edges polished by hands and weather. Here and there, Orin's marks crossed older survey shorthand, not erasing it, not correcting it, binding it to a wider geometry Avery did not yet understand.

He wanted to stop and read all of it.

He did not.

That was new trust, and he disliked how quickly necessity could counterfeit wisdom.

The corridor ended without ceremony.

One moment there was stone above them.

The next there was sky.

Avery stopped despite himself.

The route opened onto a cliffside shelf cut into the face of a vast broken escarpment. The world dropped away to their right in terraces of blocky stone and pale scrub, descending through layers of old construction and older collapse until distance turned detail into haze. Far below, thin lines of water moved through square-cut ravines, catching light and losing it. Across the gap, the opposite cliff rose in stacked planes, marked by abandoned arches, sealed windows, and route-bridges that stopped halfway across open air as if the builders had changed their minds or the world had.

Wind pressed Avery's coat against his side.

It felt indecently large after the corridor.

Eli made a small sound.

Not fear.

Recognition, maybe. Want. The kind of sound a surveyor made when the world revealed a problem big enough to deserve him.

Orin did not let either of them keep it for long.

"Left edge," she said.

Avery looked.

The shelf curved along the cliff toward a squat structure grown half from stone and half from repair. The route-hold did not look built so much as argued into remaining. Its outer wall leaned into the cliff face. Anchor irons ran from its roof into the stone above, some rusted dark, some newly replaced. Windbreak screens of woven metal and treated fiber covered gaps where windows might once have been. Seal-lines marked the threshold in chalk, charcoal, scraped stone, and thread tied through drilled holes.

No two marks belonged to the same century.

All of them were still working.

Avery could feel it.

The cliffside shelf between them and the hold was not long. Thirty paces, perhaps. Forty if the route lied, and it had shown talent for that.

Halfway along, the path split around a standing stone.

The left branch hugged the cliff wall.

The right branch ran nearer the drop but looked cleaner, wider, newer.

Orin took neither.

She stopped in front of the standing stone and removed a small chisel from her sleeve.

Eli stared at the right branch.

Avery saw the appeal. Wider footing. Better sightline. Fewer old repairs to trust.

Wrong, then.

"Do not," he said.

Eli glanced at him. "I know."

"You looked," Avery said.

"Looking remains legal," Eli said.

"Less legal than it was this morning," Avery said.

That got him another flicker of almost-normal irritation.

Orin set the chisel against the standing stone.

"Hands flat to the wall," she said.

Avery looked at the cliff wall. It was three paces away.

"Both of us?" Avery asked.

"Unless one of you has become less interesting," Orin said.

Eli muttered, "Working on it."

They moved to the wall.

Avery set both palms against cold stone. Eli did the same beside him, shoulders tight, fingers spread. The cliff shivered faintly under Avery's skin. Not collapse. Not movement. Memory under pressure.

Behind them, Orin struck the chisel once.

The sound rang wrong.

It went out across the open air and came back from too many places.

Once from the cliff opposite.

Once from below.

Once from the corridor behind them.

And once from the right-hand path, where no wall stood to echo it.

Eli's fingers twitched against the stone.

Avery did not look.

"Again," Orin said.

"To us?" Avery asked.

"To the route," Orin said.

She struck the chisel a second time.

This time Avery felt the stone under his hands answer. Not sound. Agreement. A low confirmation moving through the cliff face into the route-hold and back again.

Orin spoke softly.

"Closed to hunger. Open to passage. Debt held, debt named, debt delayed," Orin said.

Plain words.

But not casual.

She struck the chisel a third time and said the old word again.

"Keth," Orin said.

The right-hand path folded.

It did not collapse. There was no broken stone, no dust, no dramatic surrender to the drop. It simply lost its claim to being a path. One breath it was a clean shelf. The next it was an angle of cliff no reasonable foot would trust.

Eli looked anyway this time.

So did Avery.

For a moment, on the place where the path had been, three shadows stood without bodies.

One taller.

One restless.

One robed.

Then wind took them.

Orin lowered the chisel.

"Now the left," Orin said.

Avery pulled his hands from the wall slowly. His palms tingled. Fine dust clung to the lines in his skin.

"You did not close it," he said.

"No," Orin said.

"You made it less convenient," Avery said.

"Most survival is discouraging convenience," Orin said.

Eli looked at Avery. "I hate that I am going to remember that."

"Remember it quietly," Orin said.

They took the left path.

The route-hold watched them approach with every mark on its threshold.

*

Inside, the hold smelled of stone dust, old oil, dried herbs, and weather trapped in cloth.

It was larger than it had looked from outside. That was common in old route structures and never reassuring. The first chamber sat half within the cliff, its outer wall broken by narrow wind slits screened in dark fiber. Beyond it, three short rooms opened around a central work floor. One held stacked braces sorted by length and grain. One held jars of chalk, ash, powdered mineral, bone-white grit Avery did not touch, and small folded packets tied with red thread. The third was closed by a hanging of patched canvas marked with a warning Avery did not know.

Beside the canvas stood an emergency rack: two axes kept bright at the edge, a row of field knives in oiled sheaths, three sealed bottles packed in wool, and one long curved frame of dark material with its string slackened beside it, its grip wrapped in old limiter marks. Avery looked at that last piece longer than he meant to. Orin noticed and stepped between him and the rack without comment.

The central floor was all lines.

Not decorative.

Functional.

Circles nested inside squares. Paths interrupted before they became loops. Threshold marks crossed out with patient violence. Survey shorthand sat beside older sealwright notation, the two systems overlapping in ways that would have made any academy instructor either furious or quiet.

On the far wall hung a route board.

Avery went toward it before he decided to.

Orin let him take three steps.

"No names aloud," Orin said.

He stopped.

The board was made of dark wood worn smooth by many hands. Thin strips of marked stone had been set into it, each one tied by thread to another, then another, forming a map that was not geography exactly. Route relationship. Risk relationship. What could reach what. What should not be allowed to remember what.

Some strips were turned face-down.

Some were cracked.

One near the lower edge had been wrapped in black cord until the name beneath could not be read.

Avery wanted to ask.

He did not.

Trust could also be a discipline.

Eli moved to the wind slit instead. He stood with one shoulder near the wall, looking out across the cliff terraces without leaning too close.

That restraint cost him something.

Avery saw it and filed it where he kept facts that mattered more than reports.

Orin crossed to a low table and began emptying her pockets.

Pale cord. Chisel. A narrow knife with no cutting edge. Three square weights. A roll of thread. A small block of dark wax. Chalk wrapped in cloth. A shard of translucent gray material that caught the light and held it badly.

Avery looked at the shard.

Orin covered it with her palm.

"Later," Orin said.

"I did not ask," Avery said.

"You did," Orin said.

Eli spoke from the slit. "He does that with his face."

"You are not helping," Avery said.

"I was not trying to," Eli said.

The hold creaked in the wind. The sound moved through anchor irons overhead, then down the walls, then into the floor lines. Each line took a little of it and gave it somewhere harmless to go.

Avery understood one piece of the place then.

"The hold bleeds pressure," Avery said.

Orin looked up from the table.

This time the thing in her expression was approval.

Small.

Real.

"It gives a debt other work to do," Orin said.

"A false route?" Avery asked.

"No. False things fail under attention. Useful things are true enough," Orin said.

Avery crouched near the nearest floor line. He kept his hands to himself.

The line was not a line, not truly. It was a sequence of cuts interrupted by packed chalk and thread so fine it almost vanished in the groove. At each break, a different material had been pressed into the stone. Ash. Salt. Wax. Something metallic. The route was not being blocked. It was being made to negotiate.

The hold had not hidden them from the debt. It had given the debt terms.

"This is why it followed us here," Avery said.

"This is why I let it," Orin said.

Eli turned.

Avery rose slowly.

"You let it follow us," Avery said.

"Yes," Orin said.

Eli's expression changed first. Fear into anger, quick and clean. "You used us as bait."

"No," Orin said.

"That was very nearly a yes," Eli said.

Orin's hands stilled on the table.

The room seemed to listen.

Avery said, "Careful."

Eli looked at him as if betrayed.

Avery did not enjoy that.

He also did not retract it.

Orin waited until the room stopped leaning toward Eli's anger.

"Bait is offered without purpose except capture," she said. "You were already remembered. I brought the memory to a place built to hold it."

"That is a beautiful distinction if you are not the remembered part," Eli said.

"It is an important distinction if you intend to remain alive," Orin said.

The words landed hard because they did not rise.

Eli looked away first.

Not surrender.

Field discipline.

Avery knew the difference.

"What happens now?" Avery asked.

Orin took up the dark wax and warmed it between her fingers. "Now it tries to spend the debt."

The route-hold answered her as if it had been waiting for the cue.

The floor lines brightened.

Only slightly.

Enough.

At the threshold behind them, the open door to the cliff shelf showed the corridor they had left.

Not the exterior path.

The corridor.

Dark. Narrow. Familiar in the wrong order.

Eli swore under his breath.

Avery's hand went to his chalk, then stopped.

Orin saw.

"Good," Orin said.

"Stop saying that when things are terrible," Avery said.

"Good choices often occur there," Orin said.

The false corridor beyond the threshold showed Avery's mark on the wall. The copied one. His cut, older than his hand, waiting patiently for him to accept authorship.

Then it showed Eli's left passage, damp and alive.

Then it showed Orin inclining her head.

"Sister Or—"

The sound cut.

The hold took the cut sound and sent it into the floor.

One of the wax plugs split.

Orin moved at once.

"Avery, third line. Press, do not mark. Eli, wind slit, count the gusts but not aloud," Orin said.

Eli stared. "You just told him not to count."

"I told him not to count the pause. Count weather. Weather is less vain," Orin said.

Avery was already moving.

The third line was not obvious until he stopped searching like a surveyor and started reading like someone under orders. Three interruptions. Three materials. Three places the route wanted to become continuous.

He put two fingers down on the middle break and pressed.

The floor was cold.

Then hot.

Then neither.

The false corridor at the threshold sharpened.

Eli stood by the wind slit, jaw tight, eyes half-closed.

His lips did not move.

Good.

Avery felt the line under his fingers trying to decide whether his hand belonged to the copied mark outside. The pressure was intimate and repulsive. It knew the angle of his wrist. It knew the old nick in his field knife. It knew the professional satisfaction of a clean warning cut placed where a younger surveyor would see it before stepping wrong.

It offered him authorship.

It offered him correction.

It offered him the dignity of having been right all along.

Avery pressed harder.

"Not mine," he said.

Orin's head snapped toward him.

For one terrible second he thought he had answered aloud and doomed them.

Then the floor line steadied under his hand.

Orin returned to the table. "Again if it offers."

The false corridor changed.

Now it showed Eli.

Not the Eli at the wind slit.

An Eli half a step younger, perhaps. Or only less tired. Standing in the damp side passage with his head turned as if someone had called from farther in.

Eli's breath caught.

Avery looked away from the threshold and toward the real Eli.

"Gusts," Avery said.

"I know," Eli said.

"Then count them," Avery said.

"I am," Eli said.

His voice had gone thin.

The threshold-Eli lifted a hand.

Not waving.

Inviting.

Orin said one of the plain formulae again, low and precise. "Debt held. Passage refused. Name unspent."

The false Eli smiled.

It did not look like him.

The wind hit the slit hard enough to make the whole hold shudder.

Eli flinched, then steadied.

"Seven," he said.

Orin went still.

Avery looked at him.

Eli closed his eyes as if he could pull the word back by hating it.

The threshold listened.

"Seven," the false Eli said.

This time it sounded exactly like him.

The floor line under Avery's fingers surged hot.

Orin threw the dark wax into the central square.

"Down," Orin said.

Avery did not ask who.

He dropped.

Eli dropped.

The threshold flashed white, then black, then open sky. The shape of the corridor struck the floor lines like water hitting a grating. For one moment every mark in the hold burned visible: old cuts, new chalk, thread repairs, anchor points, warning strokes, the long slow argument of generations refusing to let routes have the last word.

Orin stood in the middle of it.

Both hands open.

Robes snapping in wind that was not in the room.

"Keth," she said.

Then, in plain speech:

"You are owed nothing here," Orin said.

The hold answered.

Not kindly.

Effectively.

The false corridor tore into strips of light and went down into the floor. The wax in the central square blackened. The chisel on the table cracked from handle to tip. One of the hanging brace straps snapped and fell hard enough to make Eli jerk toward it before he caught himself.

Then the threshold showed the cliff shelf again.

Wind.

Sky.

Distance.

No corridor.

Avery stayed on one knee with his fingers still pressed to the line.

No one spoke for several breaths.

He counted those.

They were ordinary enough to deserve it.

At five, Orin said, "You may stand."

Eli pushed himself up first. "I said it aloud."

"Yes," Orin said.

"I know not to do that," Eli said.

"Yes," Orin said.

He looked at her, anger gone hollow now. "That is all?"

"No," Orin said.

Orin crossed to him and took his chin in one hand before he could decide whether to object. She turned his face toward the light from the slit, studying his eyes, then the skin beside them, then something Avery could not see.

Eli endured it badly but did endure it.

"It kept only the number," she said.

"Only," Eli said.

"Only matters," Orin said.

She released him.

Avery stood. His fingers ached. The third line had left pale dust on his skin in a pattern almost like a ring.

"And me?" Avery asked.

Orin looked at his hand. "You refused a mark that wore your habit."

"I said it aloud," Avery said.

"You said no," Orin said.

"That distinction feels convenient," Avery said.

"Most survival is discouraging convenience," Eli said, flatly.

Avery looked at him.

Eli looked back.

Neither of them smiled.

That helped more than smiling would have.

Orin began resetting the table with quick, economical motions. The cracked chisel went into a separate pouch. She scraped the blackened wax from the central square with the edge of the bladeless knife. The square weights were retied. The cord was inspected, knot by knot.

Avery watched her work.

This was not mysticism, whatever language clung to it. Or not only that. It was maintenance. Emergency engineering with older materials and fewer illusions about control.

"You have done this before," Avery said.

"Yes," Orin said.

"This exact debt?" Avery asked.

Her hands paused.

The pause was brief.

It was enough.

"Close enough," Orin said.

Eli looked from her to Avery. He had recovered some color, but not enough. "Memory, not prediction."

Orin tied the cord's final knot. "Yes."

"Whose memory?" Eli asked.

The room tightened.

Not the route this time.

Orin.

Avery said, "Eli."

Eli did not back down. "She keeps saying things like they are simple. They are not simple."

"No," Orin said. "They are old."

"That is also not an answer," Eli said.

"It is the answer you can carry without dropping the rest," Orin said.

Eli stared at her for another breath, then looked away toward the slit.

Avery knew that look. Eli would return to the question later, from an angle less likely to get him ordered into silence. That was fine. Later had uses.

"What did the hold cost?" Avery asked.

Orin glanced at the cracked chisel pouch. "A tool. Three wax stops. One old line weakened. Some of your patience."

"You value those equally?" Avery asked.

"No. I can replace the tool," Orin said.

Eli made that almost-laugh again.

It did not disturb the room this time.

Avery took that as a measurable improvement.

They spent the next quarter hour making the route-hold less injured.

Orin did not ask permission. She assigned tasks as if Avery and Eli had arrived as junior hands late to work and underdressed for it.

Avery should have objected.

He checked the north wall braces instead.

The structure used three different systems of support. One was standard route crew work: compression braces seated into cut slots, good for ordinary shift and minor route pressure. One was older, relying on anchor irons that bit upward into the cliff and transferred strain through the roof. The third belonged to Orin's trade: flexible restraint, thread and chalk and material breaks designed to interrupt meaning before meaning became force.

He understood perhaps half of it.

That was more than he had expected.

Less than he liked.

Eli was given the task of sorting chalk by texture, not color. He objected for nearly six seconds before realizing that the difference mattered. Soft chalk for temporary refusal. Hard chalk for route warning. Grit-bound chalk for places where the stone wanted to drink the mark too quickly.

"This one is wrong," he said at one point, holding up a pale stick.

Orin did not look over. "Why?"

"It feels like it wants to be on a door," Eli said.

"Put it in the red tray," Orin said.

Eli looked at the tray. It was empty.

"That seems bad," Eli said.

"It is," Orin said.

He put it down carefully.

Avery watched the exchange while tightening a brace pin.

There it was again. Orin did not praise Eli's instinct. She used it. That was either wisdom or cruelty, and Avery was not yet certain the difference mattered in a place that punished delay.

When the north brace stopped humming under his hand, he stepped back.

"Done," he said.

Orin crossed the room, tested the pin once, then looked at his work.

"Acceptable," Orin said.

"Careful. I may grow arrogant," Avery said.

"I have met surveyors," Orin said.

Eli looked up from the chalk trays. "That means she already thinks you are."

"Thank you," Avery said.

"I was helping," Eli said.

"You were sorting chalk," Avery said.

"With emotional range," Eli said.

For a moment the hold felt almost ordinary.

Then wind pressed against the outer wall, and the route board gave a soft click.

All three of them turned.

One of the face-down stone strips had shifted.

Not much.

Enough to show the lower edge of a name.

Orin crossed to it faster than Avery had seen her move.

She pressed two fingers against the strip and held it down.

The board clicked again beneath her hand.

Eli stood. "Is that us?"

"No," Orin said.

Avery left the brace wall. "Then who?"

Orin did not answer at once.

The board clicked a third time.

This time a thread connected to the hidden strip snapped tight and pointed toward a marked route near the board's upper left. Avery read the visible name there.

Anchorfall Cliffs.

He did not say it aloud.

Orin noticed.

Of course she did.

"Good," she said.

Avery was starting to hate the word less. That seemed dangerous.

"The debt is reaching beyond the hold," Avery said.

"Trying to," Orin said.

"Toward Anchorfall," Avery said.

Eli came closer, but not too close. He had learned something today, then. "What's at Anchorfall?"

Orin kept her fingers on the face-down strip.

For the first time since she had stepped into their corridor, her age seemed less like authority and more like weight.

"Rook," she said.

Avery knew the name. Not well. Enough.

A route-runner with a reputation for arriving before permission and leaving before blame could find a stable footing. Clever. Useful. Too fond of shortcuts. The sort of man senior surveyors either learned to use carefully or spent years citing in safety lectures.

Eli's attention sharpened with immediate interest.

"Rook is waiting for you?" Eli asked.

"He was told to wait," Orin said.

Avery looked at Orin's hand on the board. "Those are not the same answer."

"No," Orin said.

The route board clicked again.

The strip under Orin's fingers stayed down, but the thread to Anchorfall trembled.

Eli's face lost the last of its humor. "The open-door debt can reach him?"

"If it learns the route faster than we move," Orin said.

Avery looked toward the threshold, where the cliff shelf waited under clean wind and too much sky.

The hold had done its work.

Not finished the danger.

Held it.

Delayed it.

Made the mistake smaller than theirs.

She had not saved them by getting them away from it. She had saved them by choosing where it was allowed to arrive.

He understood now why Orin had brought the debt here instead of running from it through ordinary stone. He understood why she had spoken in fragments and orders. He understood why she had not given Eli the answer he wanted. Information was not harmless simply because it was true.

Trust did not arrive as warmth.

It arrived as a decision.

Avery turned back to Orin. "What do you need?"

Orin studied him for a moment.

This time, when she answered, she did not sound like she was testing him.

"Your marks," she said. "His ears. My hold."

Eli glanced at Avery.

Still shaken.

Still angry.

Ready anyway.

Avery nodded once.

"Then we go to Anchorfall," Avery said.

The route board clicked under Orin's hand, soft and insistent.

Outside, far below the cliff, wind moved through the broken terraces like a thing searching for a door.

Orin lifted her fingers from the hidden name.

The strip stayed down.

For now.
