# FRAME 14

"Anchor Maw," Orin said.

She did not raise her voice.

That made it worse.

The road beyond the bend answered again. One step. Then another. Each impact moved through the stone under Avery's boots and up through his teeth. The Stoneback Hauler backed until the cart traces creaked and Tavin had to stumble aside to keep from being pressed against the inner wall.

Rook looked from Orin to the road. "I dislike how quickly that became a name."

"You saw its child," Orin said.

Eli's head turned.

The hauler gave a low, miserable sound and lowered itself until the salt crates tilted in their lashings.

"The thing at Anchorfall," Avery said.

"A Mawling," Orin said. "Young. Smaller. Hungry enough to be foolish." Her eyes stayed on the bend. "It took a scent from you."

"It smelled us?" Rook asked.

"No," Orin said. "It remembered us wrong."

The next step struck closer.

Stone dust sifted from the inside wall.

Orin moved then. She caught Tavin by the back of his coat and pulled him away from the hauler's shoulder before the animal lurched again.

"Fear," she said. "Blood. Footprints. Broken marks. The way damaged stone answers when you run across it. A Mawling can carry all of that back through route pressure if it survives long enough to cry for what made it."

Eli's hand had gone to his flask.

This time he did not stop himself.

"The child found us," Orin said. "The mother followed."

The Anchor Maw came around the bend.

For a moment Avery's mind tried to make it smaller.

Distance should have helped. It did not. The creature filled the road as if the road had been built around the possibility of it and then regretted the design. One leg rose, thick as a standing man from foot to shoulder, and came down with the blunt care of a thing that did not need to hurry. Its body stretched above and behind that leg in a broad, broken mass of dark stone hide, wide enough that Avery saw it first as moving wall and only after as animal. Pale seams ran between the plates, opening and closing with each breath. Some of the plates looked grown. Some looked torn from old route braces and fitted badly into meat that had never wanted them.

Its head was worse.

The mouth split down the front into two hard halves, anchor-biters where jaws should have been. They opened apart, clicked, and met again around nothing. The sound made the hauler try to crawl backward.

Behind the Maw, the route-hold came into view in pieces.

Not as a place.

As damage.

The warning board lay cracked across the workfloor. One low roof had folded inward. Anchor irons had been pulled from the stone and left standing at bad angles like broken teeth. Chalk dust blew in pale sheets. The central lines Orin had repaired before were cut through, not erased but bitten open. People were moving below the outer wall, too many and too slow, carrying bundles, children, and one old man between two shoulders toward the lower entrance Avery had only half noticed before.

The Underhold.

The Maw lowered its head toward them.

Its pale seams brightened.

"Move," Avery said.

No one argued.

They drove the hauler into the lee of the inner wall before the creature finished deciding whether the cart was prey or stone. Tavin was shaking so hard he could barely unfasten the lead strap. Rook cut it free with one quick motion.

"Take him below," Orin said.

"I can help," Tavin said.

"You can help by breathing where the ceiling is thicker," Orin said.

Tavin looked like he might fight her.

Then the Maw struck the route-hold wall.

It did not leap. It lunged with its head, split mouth clamping onto an anchor post thicker than Avery's thigh. The post screamed. Not wood. Not metal. Route pressure. The sound went straight through the marks in the ground and made every chalk line shiver.

Tavin ran.

A woman near the Underhold entrance shouted orders with a field medic's raw authority. Avery recognized her from Anchorfall by the set of her shoulders before he recognized her face. She had one arm in a sling and was using the other to shove a frightened man toward the stairs.

"Below!" she shouted. "Carry those who cannot stand. Leave the tools. Leave them."

"No," Orin said.

Avery looked at her.

Orin was looking at the cart.

"Some tools," Orin corrected.

They unloaded like people stealing from their own future.

Orin opened the long wrapped bundle from the equipment cache first. Inside lay a bow made from layered pale wood and blackened metal strips, not decorative, not graceful in any harmless way. Its limbs were marked with limiter cuts so fine Avery did not see them until the bow shifted in Orin's hands and caught the weak light.

Rook's expression changed.

"You had that," Rook said.

"No," Orin said. "I avoided having it."

She strung it as if regret had muscle memory.

Then she pulled the route-worked longstaff from the cart.

Rook's face changed before she threw it.

"That one," he said.

"Still yours," Orin said.

She threw it to him. Rook caught it one-handed, nearly dropped it from the weight, and recovered with offended dignity. The staff was dark bracewood with pale inlay at the grip, thin lines of anchoring salt worked into grooves along its length. When he shifted his hand, the staff corrected a fraction too quickly, as if it remembered balance before he did.

"It has been a while," Rook said.

His fingers found the old grip by touch anyway.

"Vault with it," Orin said. "Do not block with it unless you enjoy becoming a lesson."

"I am fond of several parts of myself," Rook said. "I will keep the lesson outside them."

She handed Avery the axe next.

It was shorter than a woodsman's axe and heavier behind the head. The edge had been newly honed, the haft wrapped for climbing grip.

"Leg joints," Orin said. "Plates if you have to. Seams if the road is kind."

"And if it is not?" Avery asked.

"Be rude," Orin said.

He took the axe.

The dagger at his hip suddenly felt very small.

For Eli, Orin chose last.

It was not a bow and not a spear. A light one-handed staff, no longer than Eli's forearm from elbow to fingertip, wrapped in gray cord and set with a narrow strip of pale glass down one side. The glass did not glow until Orin touched it. Then it answered with a thin blue-white line that made Eli step back before he chose to.

"No," Eli said.

"Yes," Orin said.

"I do not know how to use that," Eli said.

"That is why I am giving it to you," Orin said.

Rook glanced at the staff and then at Eli. "A rare vote of confidence delivered as an accusation."

Orin held the staff out until Eli took it.

The blue-white line moved under his fingers.

Not away from him.

Toward him.

"It will not make the Echo obey," Orin said. "Nothing honest will. It gives your hand a place to put what your body is already trying to spend."

She tapped the flask with two fingers.

"Keep that hand free," Orin said.

Eli swallowed.

"You know that because of her," Eli said.

Orin's eyes did not leave his. "I know what I failed to give her."

That was all she said.

Eli stood beside the cart with the light staff in one hand and the flask open in the other, staring into the flask as if anger might fill it.

"I am low," he said.

Avery heard the roughness in it. Not complaint. Alarm.

Rook's humor left his face.

Orin looked at the flask, then at Eli.

"How low?" Orin asked.

Eli's jaw worked.

"Too low for that," Eli said.

The Maw stamped one foot on the edge of the workfloor. The stone buckled. A route shock went through the hold, not a wave of air but a refusal moving under the surface. A line of chalk lifted from the ground in a pale ribbon and blew apart.

Eli flinched before it happened.

Orin saw.

Her face went very still.

"Give it to me," Orin said.

Eli held the flask closer.

"No," Eli said.

"Eli," Orin said.

"No," Eli said.

The word was too sharp. Too young.

The Maw hit the anchor post again. This time it tore the post free and threw it sideways. It crashed through a tool shed and sent jars of mineral salt bursting across the floor.

Orin stepped in close enough that Eli had to look at her.

"Remember that girl," Orin said.

Eli went rigid.

Avery remembered the road east. Her name was Neris. She heard things before the rest of us learned they were speaking.

"She ran dry in a bad place once," Orin said. "Not of drink. Of anything that told her where her edges were. I did not know enough then."

Eli's fingers tightened around the flask.

"You do now?" Eli asked.

"More than I deserve," Orin said.

The answer did something to him. Not trust. Nothing that clean. But his grip loosened.

Orin took the flask.

She crouched beside the nearest crate of anchoring salt, dragged one finger through spilled chalk, then cut a five-point mark across the flask's metal side. Avery expected the mark to smear.

It sank in.

Not into the metal.

Into the idea of the metal.

Orin poured the last mouthful from the flask onto the chalk. The liquid should have spread. Instead it drew itself into the mark in a thin amber line and began turning through the points like a small, stubborn wheel.

"An infinite well?" Rook asked quietly.

"A poor one," Orin said. "Made fast."

"Naturally," Rook said. "We would hate to be spoiled before being crushed."

Orin ignored him. She pressed the flask into Eli's hands.

"It will answer if you do not ask it to be more than it is," Orin said. "Drink enough to stay inside yourself. No more. The staff will help you spend less badly, but it will not make you empty-proof."

Eli stared at the flask.

"That is real?" Eli asked.

"Most useful things are," Orin said.

He drank.

Avery did not know what he expected. Light. Music. A neat change in the air. There was none of that. Eli swallowed once, shut his eyes, and breathed through his nose as if he had found the edge of a table in a dark room.

Then his eyes opened.

The Maw turned toward the Underhold entrance.

"Now," Orin said.

The first arrow struck the creature's left foreleg.

It did not pierce the stone plate. It struck the pale seam above the joint and stayed there, humming. The Maw's step hit wrong. Its foot came down half a pace short, cracking the workfloor instead of the stair mouth where three villagers were still dragging a cot below.

The field medic looked up.

"Move them!" Avery shouted.

She did.

Rook ran.

Not away from the Maw.

Toward its attention.

He scooped a handful of loose stones from the broken workfloor as he moved. The first pebble struck the creature's split mouth. It was a ridiculous sound. Small stone against enormous stone. A tick. Nothing.

The Maw's head turned.

Rook threw another. This one struck a pale seam below one eye.

"Yes," Rook said. "That is the face of a creature ready for conversation."

The Maw lunged.

Rook planted the longstaff and vaulted sideways with a speed that made Avery's knees hurt in sympathy. The split mouth closed on empty air and broke the edge of the route board instead.

Avery ran for the left side.

He did not think of it as climbing until his boot found the first broken plate. The Maw's hide was not smooth. It was worse. Too many handholds, none of them trustworthy. Some plates shifted under his weight like loose slate. Some held as if grown from mountain. Pale seams pulsed under his fingers, warm and cold at once.

The axe was a weight in one hand. His other hand found a ridge, then a crack, then a brace-like spur of dark stone. He climbed because below him the Underhold entrance was full of people and above him the Maw had joints.

Orin's second arrow hit the rear leg. The Maw stumbled again, more irritated than hurt.

Eli stood in the ruined workfloor with the light staff in one hand and the flask in the other.

For one second he looked lost.

Then the air around the Maw slowed.

Not all of it. Not enough to be seen cleanly. Dust moved at the usual speed until it reached the creature's head, then dragged as if caught in honey. The Maw's split jaws opened. The motion stuttered.

Eli's face twisted with confusion.

"I know this," Eli said.

No one had time to answer.

Blue-white fire crawled across his fingers and into the pale glass set along the staff. Not flame exactly. Not lightning exactly. Something in between, shaped by a person who had never been taught which name to give it. It leapt from the staff to the Maw's head and burst along the pale seams.

The creature screamed.

The sound hit the route-hold and made every broken anchor answer.

Avery almost lost his grip.

He drove the axe into the seam above a shoulder plate because there was no room for fear. The first strike glanced. The second bit. The Maw bucked, and the world became hide, air, and Avery's left hand refusing to open.

"Avery!" Rook shouted.

"Busy," Avery shouted back.

"I recommend less of that," Rook said.

Rook threw another stone into the Maw's eye seam.

The creature turned hard toward him.

Good, Avery thought.

Then: Too hard.

The motion swung the shoulder under him. His boots came loose. He dropped six feet, slammed against a lower plate, caught a ridge with his elbow, and felt something in his forearm complain with bright sincerity.

He kept the axe.

That counted.

Orin fired again. Again. Again. Each arrow struck a seam or joint, never wasted, never dramatic. The Maw's legs did not fail, but they began to remember they had limits. Its left foreleg dragged. Its right rear leg hit wide. Every correction bought the villagers below another breath.

Eli drank again before he lifted the staff. His flask hand shook harder after the swallow than before it.

"Left," Eli said.

The Maw went right.

Not because Eli commanded it. Because the route under its right foot suddenly looked easier. Avery felt the lie pass through the stone as a wrong invitation. The Maw accepted and stepped into broken flooring. Its weight punched through old boards and into the storage hollow beneath.

It roared and tore free, dragging half the floor with it.

The route-hold came apart around them.

One low wall collapsed. The water jars burst. A shelf of chalks tipped, spilling white sticks across the ground like bones. The old warning board split in two under the Maw's tail. A roof beam slid loose and crashed over the entrance path, leaving only a narrow gap into the Underhold.

The field medic shoved the last child through the gap.

"Clear!" the field medic shouted.

Then the Maw's head turned toward the sound.

Rook was already moving.

He had run out of loose stones. He kicked one up with his boot, caught it, and threw it into the creature's mouth as the jaws opened.

"There," Rook said. "A gift. Courtship requires persistence."

The Maw bit down.

The pebble shattered.

Rook bowed.

The creature lunged.

Rook planted the staff, vaulted over the first bite, landed on a broken wall, and sprang again. For three impossible breaths he stayed ahead of it by being exactly where no one sensible would stand. The Maw followed because Rook made himself more irritating than the Underhold, more immediate than Avery, brighter in its anger than the injured road.

Avery climbed again.

He reached the shoulder ridge, hooked one knee around a brace plate, and struck at the leg joint below. The axe bit deeper this time. Dark grit spilled from the seam. The Maw's leg dipped.

Eli drank again.

Too fast this time.

Some of it ran down his wrist. He did not seem to notice. The staff's pale glass had gone bright enough to show the bones of the hand wrapped around it.

He hit the head again.

The blue-white burst cracked across the split mouth. The Maw's path stuttered. Its front half slowed while the rear kept moving, and for one ugly second the creature was fighting its own body.

Eli laughed.

It was not a good sound.

It was wonder with the bottom missing.

"Eli!" Avery shouted.

Eli did not hear him.

He had the flask at his mouth again.

The Maw's foreleg came down.

The ground did not shake like ground.

It kicked.

The blow ran through the broken workfloor just as Eli lowered the flask and tried to thumb the cap shut. The metal snapped out of his wet fingers before he could holster it. It struck stone, bounced once, and skidded under a leaning brace, close enough for him to see and just far enough that the ruined floor between them kept moving.

Eli's hand went to his side.

Found nothing.

His face emptied, then broke.

"No," Eli said.

He lunged for it. Another tremor shoved him to one knee. The staff clattered against the ground beside him, still lit.

The Maw screamed again.

The route answered.

Eli turned in place, searching. His eyes passed over the flask once and did not find it. They passed over Rook.

And stopped.

Rook had landed near the broken route board. Dust covered his coat. The longstaff was braced across both hands. He was alive, human, breathing too hard.

Eli saw something else.

Avery knew it by the way Eli's shoulders changed.

Not fear.

Recognition.

The worst kind.

"No," Avery said.

Eli raised one hand.

Rook looked at him.

"Eli?" Rook asked.

"Move," Eli said.

Rook's eyes flicked behind him, looking for the threat Eli saw.

There was none.

Only Rook.

Blue-white fire gathered in Eli's palm.

The Maw's tail swept across the workfloor.

Rook turned at the sound. Too late. The tail caught the wall beside him and exploded stone into his side. He flew sideways, struck the ground, and rolled under the shadow of the Maw's front leg.

The creature lowered its head.

Eli fired.

Not at the Maw.

At Rook.

Or at whatever stood in Rook's place inside Eli's broken sight.

Orin hit Eli from the side with her whole body.

The burst went wide and tore a glowing scar across the stone behind Rook's head. Eli hit the ground hard. Orin landed on top of him, one forearm across his chest, her face inches from his.

"It is not real," Orin said.

Eli fought her.

"It is there," Eli said.

"No," Orin said.

"It is wearing him," Eli said.

"No," Orin said.

The Maw's mouth opened above Rook.

Orin looked once.

Only once.

Then she shoved Eli down by the shoulder and stood.

"Stay inside your skin," Orin said.

She ran at the Anchor Maw.

For the first time since Avery had known her, Sister Orin made herself obvious.

She shouted. Not words at first. Sound. Command. Insult. Old road-language with teeth in it. She fired one arrow into the Maw's mouth seam, another into the eye ridge, then dropped the bow and snapped her weighted cord loose with both hands.

The Maw turned from Rook.

Its jaws closed where his chest had been half a breath before.

Orin ran under the line of its head.

Avery dropped from the shoulder.

He hit the ground badly, rolled worse, and came up running because Rook had not moved.

"Rook!" Avery shouted.

Rook moved then.

Barely.

One hand dragged the longstaff against his body. Blood marked his temple and one side of his mouth. His eyes were open but unfocused.

"I dislike," Rook said, each word thin, "being courted back."

"Stay down," Avery said.

"I have become fond of it," Rook said.

The Maw lifted one leg to crush Orin.

She threw the cord.

It wrapped the leg above the joint. One square weight spun around, struck stone, and locked. Another followed. The third snapped back into Orin's palm hard enough that Avery heard skin split.

The Maw stepped.

The cord went tight.

Orin slid two feet across broken chalk.

"Avery!" Orin shouted.

He was already moving.

The joint was exposed for one breath, the same left foreleg he had been cutting from above, now bound and turned wrong. Pale seams flared around the braced plates. The axe felt too heavy. Then it felt like the only honest object in the world.

Avery swung.

The first blow cracked the outer plate.

The Maw wrenched against Orin's cord. Orin set her feet and leaned back with both hands. Her face went bloodless.

"Again," Orin said.

Avery swung again.

The axe bit through the seam.

The leg folded.

Not all at once. Nothing that large fell simply. The Maw tried to correct with the rear leg Orin had already pinned twice. Eli, still on the ground, made a sound like he was being torn awake and slapped one hand against the stone.

The route under the Maw slowed.

Only for a breath.

Long enough.

The creature's weight shifted past recovery.

It came down on its side and took half the route-hold with it.

Stone burst. Dust swallowed the workfloor. The impact threw Avery from his feet and drove the air from his lungs. For a moment the world was gray and soundless except for the ringing inside his head.

Then the Maw moved.

Its underside faced them.

There was a core there.

Not a heart. Avery knew that without knowing why. It was a knot of dark stone and pale light set under the ribs, shielded by plates thinner than the rest and pulsing with the same wrong pressure that had made the road answer. Every seam in the creature seemed to lead there.

"Core!" Orin shouted.

Avery was already standing.

Eli pushed himself up on one elbow. His eyes were still wrong, but they found the Maw this time. He raised both hands and the air around the core thickened. The pulsing slowed.

"I can hold it," Eli said.

His voice did not sound certain.

It sounded remembered.

Orin snatched up the Limiter Bow and fired into the exposed underside. One arrow. Two. Three. Each struck around the core and pinned plates open. Rook, from the ground, lifted one shaking hand and threw the smallest stone Avery had ever seen.

It hit the exposed core with a pathetic tick.

"Symbolic," Rook whispered.

Avery climbed.

The fallen body was a hill of broken plates and breathing stone. His boots slipped in grit. His left hand burned. He dropped the axe because the angle was wrong and because the dagger at his hip was suddenly the correct size for the work ahead.

The Maw thrashed.

Its split mouth snapped at nothing. Its rear legs tore trenches through the floor. One route shock rolled out from its body and cracked the Underhold lintel. Voices cried out below.

Avery climbed faster.

The core pulsed once.

Eli cried out and drove both hands down as if pinning something only he could see.

The pulse slowed.

"Avery," Orin said.

Not warning.

Permission.

He reached the underside plates, hooked one arm around a ridge, and drew the dagger.

The core opened.

Inside the pale light was darker than the stone around it. Avery thought of a mouth. Of a wound. Of a road that had learned hunger and mistaken it for law.

He drove the dagger in with both hands.

The Anchor Maw stopped.

Not died. Not at first.

Stopped.

Every plate along its body locked. Every seam flared white. The dagger shook in Avery's grip hard enough to numb his fingers. Then the light snapped inward.

The Maw's body exhaled.

The breath smelled of wet stone, old blood, and chalk after rain.

Avery fell with it.

The body began to come apart before the dust settled.

Not collapse.

Unmake.

The dark plates lost their edges first, crumbling into powder along the pale seams. The legs that had shaken the hold softened into gray grit. Brace-like ribs folded inward without sound. Wherever the Maw had been stone, it became dust. Wherever it had been hunger, it became nothing Avery could name.

The underbelly core remained.

It dropped from the dissolving body with a heavy, wrong sound and rolled once through the ash-gray powder. Dark stone. Pale light trapped inside. Too solid for what the rest of the creature had become.

Avery stared at it until Orin said his name again.

*

Afterward, the route-hold was quieter than any living place should be.

People came out of the Underhold slowly. Some crawled through the narrowed entrance. Some carried others. The field medic emerged last, face streaked with dust, sling gone, wounded arm hanging useless at her side.

The field medic's eyes moved from the ash-gray hollow where the Anchor Maw had been to Orin.

No one asked if they had won.

The answer was lying everywhere, except for the creature itself.

The route board was gone. The tool sheds were split open. The water storage had emptied into cracks. Anchor irons lay twisted out of line. Chalk and salt mixed underfoot until every step left a pale print. The hold still existed as stone, but not as shelter. Not as a place that could promise a second night.

Avery found Rook sitting against a broken wall with the longstaff across his knees.

"You are bleeding," Avery said.

"I am diversifying," Rook said.

"Stop," Avery said.

"I would love to," Rook said.

The field medic crouched beside him and began checking ribs with efficient cruelty. Rook made a sound that was almost language.

"He will live," the field medic said.

"I had reached the same conclusion," Rook said weakly. "Mine involved less poking."

Avery looked for Eli.

He found him near the broken brace where the flask had fallen.

Eli was on his knees, one hand under the beam, reaching. His fingers brushed metal and missed. Brushed again. Closed.

He pulled the flask out and held it against his chest.

For one moment his whole body folded around it.

Relief should have made Avery feel better.

It did not.

Eli unscrewed the cap with shaking hands. He drank once, then stopped himself with visible effort. The infinite well mark Orin had cut into the metal moved faintly under the dust, a small amber line turning through its points.

Eli looked up.

He saw Rook.

Human.

Hurt.

Alive.

The color left his face.

"I did not know," Eli said.

Rook's eyes were closed. "I recommend knowing in advance next time."

Eli flinched.

Rook opened one eye.

"That was a joke," he said. "A poor one, due to head injury. You may blame the masonry."

Eli looked down at the flask.

Avery crossed the broken floor and crouched beside him.

"Later," Avery said.

Eli's grip tightened.

"I saw something," Eli said.

"I know," Avery said.

"No," Eli said. "You do not."

Avery thought of the blue-white scar in the stone behind Rook's head.

"I know enough for right now," Avery said.

Eli did not answer.

A stone clicked somewhere beyond the ruined wall.

Small.

Wrongly timed.

Avery stood.

Orin already had the bow in her hand.

Dain stepped through the breach the Maw had made.

He looked worse than he had at the village bend. One eye was swollen nearly shut. His hands were free, though rope burns circled both wrists. Blood had dried along his jaw. Behind him, half hidden by the broken wall, stood the youngest bandit and the knife woman from the road, both pale with exhaustion and fear.

"I told you not to follow," Orin said.

Dain smiled.

It was a terrible thing to see on a ruined hold.

"Still giving orders, Vey," Dain said.

Eli tried to stand.

Avery put a hand on his shoulder.

Dain saw the movement.

Saw the flask.

Saw Eli.

Something in his face became clean and hateful.

"Another one," Dain said.

Orin's bow did not move.

"Leave," Orin said.

"You kept the last one too," Dain said. "Called it mercy. Called it protection. Roads whispering to a child and you all pretending that was not rot waiting to learn teeth."

Eli went still.

Avery understood too late.

Dain's hand came out from behind his coat with a field knife.

He did not throw it at Orin.

He threw it at Eli.

Avery moved.

Not fast enough.

The knife struck under Eli's ribs and drove him backward against Avery's hands. Eli made one small sound, surprised more than pained, and the flask slipped from his grip.

Orin fired.

The arrow took Dain in the throat.

He fell without drama.

That made it worse too.

The knife woman screamed once and clapped both hands over her mouth. The youngest bandit dropped to his knees. No one else moved.

Orin lowered the bow.

"I did not want that," Orin said.

No one mistook her meaning.

Her hand stayed on the bow, but it was shaking now.

"He made it the only road left."

Avery had Eli on the ground by then. His hands were over the wound. Blood pushed warm between his fingers, too much at first, then less when the field medic slid in beside him and took control.

"Hold here," the field medic said.

"Is he dying?" Avery asked.

"Not if you stop asking and press," the field medic said.

Avery pressed.

Eli's eyes found his.

"That was not real either?" Eli asked.

"That was real," Avery said.

"Unfortunate," Eli said.

"Quiet," Avery said.

"Yes," Eli said, and passed out.

*

Dain burned at dusk.

Not where the Maw had come apart. Orin would not let the two smokes mix.

She chose a flat shelf below the broken hold, salted the body herself, and used both hands though one was split from the cord. The remaining bandits watched from ten paces away under Rook's tired eye and the field medic's sharper one. They looked younger without Dain standing in front of them. Younger and emptier.

Eli lay inside the Underhold entrance with his wound bound under borrowed cloth. The flask rested in his hand even in sleep. Avery had tried once to move it aside so the medic could work more easily.

Eli had woken enough to say no.

Avery had left it.

Now he stood beside Orin while she set the last line of salt.

"Neris," Avery said.

Orin did not look up.

For a while Avery thought she would not answer.

Then Orin said, "Dain killed her."

The fire had not yet been lit. The body was only a shape under salted cloth. Somehow that made the words colder.

"Because she was like Eli," Avery said.

"Because he feared what she might become." Orin's voice was steady in the way stone was steady before it cracked. "She heard roads. Wrong seams. Bad pressure under good ground. People called it blessing when it saved them and sickness when it frightened them."

Rook stood a few steps behind them, one arm wrapped against his ribs.

"And you knew," Avery said.

"I knew she was rare. I knew she was useful." Orin's mouth tightened. "That was the first sin. I learned to love her after."

The fire wick caught.

A thin flame moved along the salted line.

"I was Vey when I found her," Orin said. "I had men who listened because I frightened them, and roads that listened because I had learned where to hurt them. I thought I could turn violence into order if I gave it rules."

The salted cloth began to smoke.

"Dain learned the rules," Orin said. "Then he decided the child was a future danger. He said killing her was mercy. Prevention. Clean work before rot spread."

The smoke rose straight for a few feet, then bent with the evening wind.

"She was close enough that I should have known," Orin said.

"You did not kill him then," Avery said.

"No," Orin said.

"Why?" Avery asked.

Orin watched the fire take.

"Because I wanted to," she said. "More than I had wanted anything. I thought if I killed him for her, Vey would be all that remained of me."

The body burned.

No one spoke until the first flame settled.

"Today?" Avery asked.

Orin's eyes were on the smoke.

"Today he tried to murder the same child twice," Orin said.

Avery thought of Eli's face when the knife struck. Surprise. Then an exhausted attempt at humor because terror had no proper handle.

"Eli is not Neris," Avery said.

"No," Orin said. "He is not."

She took a handful of salt and cast it into the edge of the fire.

It flared white.

"But Dain did not know the difference," Orin said.

Behind them, Eli stirred in his sleep and tightened his hand around the flask.

The little amber mark turned once against his palm.

Orin looked back to the fire.

For the first time that day, her face had no command left in it.

"I am sorry, Neris," she said.

The name entered the smoke and did not come back.

Orin's hand closed around the last pinch of salt.

"I should have been more than a shelter when it was easy. I should have been the hand between you and the knife."

The salt fell from her fingers into the flame.

It flared white, then softened.

Above the ruined route-hold, burial smoke climbed into the darkening road.

"You deserved a better mother than the one I learned to be too late."
