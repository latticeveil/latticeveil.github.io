# Knot 05: The Surveyor's Name

The Embercoal in the yard pit popped, throwing a spray of orange sparks toward the dark canopy of the terrace trees.

Tavin sat on the stone bench opposite me, his posture stiff, his young fingers clamped around his mending pin so tightly his knuckles showed pale beneath the road-grime. He was listening with that fierce, desperate attention young men only give when they are trying to fit their own anger into someone else’s history.

"You were fifteen," Tavin said quietly. His voice cut through the hum of the night wind. "Your mother shut the door. And you just... walked?"

I rested both hands on the smooth head of my bracewood cane and let out a long, quiet breath.

"I walked," I told him. "When a door like that slams behind you, you do not stand on the threshold waiting for the wood to soften. You turn your face to the road, because staying still makes the cold sink straight into your bones."

***

The southern switchbacks were caked in grey lime-dust that rose in thin, choking puffs under my boots.

Four days. Four days since the bracewood door had slammed shut behind me. My small canvas pouch—the one that had held a handful of dry Anchor Biscuit crumbs—was completely empty. My water skin rattled with nothing but air. The cold had crept deep into my shoulders, a dull, heavy ache that made every breath feel like chewing glass.

I carried my father Thomas’s survey slate jammed tight beneath my left arm, wrapped in the wool blanket he had left behind.

Whenever I passed a wayfarer, a haul-driver resting his stoneback, or a ragged trader huddled by a route-shack, I stopped. My voice was raspy, dry as lime, but I asked the same questions every time.

*"Have you seen a high-terrace surveyor? Has anyone vanished into thinned stone near the southern shelves? Where are the places where the rock speaks back?"*

I pulled out the slate and scratched down coordinates, route numbers, and rumor-types whenever someone gave me a name or a location. *My Hero* was out here somewhere. He had walked these thinned paths, and I convinced myself that if I mapped every place where people disappeared into the Veil, I would eventually find the seam he had stepped through.

On the fourth night, the wind blew hard off the high cuts, bringing a bitter, freezing fog that settled over the limestone ledges.

I huddled beneath a shallow rock overhang, my knees pulled tight to my chest, shivering so violently my teeth clicked together like loose pebbles. Hypothermia was setting in—a numb, heavy creeping that started in my toes and drifted up toward my heart.

In the pitch darkness of the ravine below, a sudden, heavy scraping sounded against the dry rock.

It wasn't wind. It was the slow, dragging weight of an outland beast—a wild braceback or a route-prowler hunting in the cold shadows.

Terror shot through me, sharp and child-like. I froze, holding my breath until my lungs burned. I didn't have a blade. I didn't have a light. I was just a fifteen-year-old girl sitting alone in the dark on a ledge that didn't care if I lived or died. I pressed my father's survey slate against my chest like a sheet of iron, tears hot and bitter against my freezing cheeks.

*"Papa..."* I whispered into the dark, my voice barely a thread. *"Papa... where are you?"*

The heavy scraping dragged along the rocks below and then slowly faded into the distance. I lay back against the damp limestone, wiping my face with a trembling hand. I was terrified. I was small. But when the first grey light of dawn broke over the peaks, I pulled myself up on stiff, freezing legs and kept marching down the switchbacks.

***

By mid-afternoon, my body gave out.

The combination of starvation, freezing cold, and sheer exhaustion caught up to me on a sharp turn near a ruined route-shack. My vision swam with dark, geometric spots. My knees buckled, and I hit the lime-dust hard, rolling onto my side. I couldn't move my fingers. I couldn't lift my head. The hypothermia was turning everything warm and hazy, sinking me into a heavy, twilight stupor.

Through the fog in my ears, I heard boots crunching in the loose gravel.

I couldn't open my eyes fully, but I felt the cold air move as someone stooped down over me. Hands rough with calluses rolled me onto my back.

"Great," a voice muttered—young, sharp, and bitter. "Another high-terrace mouth to feed... She's freezing to death, Dad. Look at her clothes. Mender's wool."

It was a boy's voice. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, but hardened with a nasty, cynical edge.

"Hold your tongue, Dain," a second voice replied. This one was older, deeper, with the quiet, disciplined cadence of a man who used to command respect before the world broke him.

I felt a hand tug at the survey slate pinned beneath my arm. I tried to tighten my grip, but my numb fingers had no strength left.

"Hey—watch it, Dad," Dain's voice came again, closer now. I heard the sharp slide of my father's slate being pulled free. A long silence followed, punctuated only by the whistle of the outland wind.

"Look at the slate, Dad," Dain said, his tone shifting from annoyance to a reluctant, resentful scowl. "Look at the edge-work. Those aren't child's scribbles. Her coordinate geometry is clean... She knows how to read weeping stone better than anyone in our crew."

I lay there in the dust, drifting in and out of consciousness, hearing their voices like they were shouting from the bottom of a deep well.

"She's caked in lime-dust and her pulse is threadbare," the older man said softly. I felt large, warm hands lift the slate, brushing the powder from its slate-frame. "She has a surveyor's eye, Dain. And she's a child dying in a ditch."

"She's a liability," Dain snapped, his voice tight with resentment. "She'll slow us down on the south routes. We barely have enough Anchor Biscuits for ourselves!"

"We are not leaving a surveyor's child to freeze on the high cuts," the older man said with quiet, unbreakable authority. "Gather her blanket. She comes with us."

Strong arms scooped me up off the frozen ground. I couldn't keep my eyes open. The warmth of a heavy wool coat pressed against my face, and the dark finally pulled me under completely.

***

When I woke, the smell of woodsmoke and hot salt-broth filled my senses.

I was sitting by a crackling pit-fire inside a dry overhang, wrapped in two thick blankets. A wooden bowl was pressed into my hands. I drank greedily, the hot broth scalding my throat and sending a blessed, aching warmth flooding back into my frozen limbs.

Across the fire sat the older man. He had sharp, intelligent eyes framed by deep lines of grief and fatigue, wearing the battered leather harness of an outland raider over the faded tunic of a high-terrace mender. Beside him stood Dain—fifteen years old, tall, lean, with watchful, hostile eyes and his arms crossed tight over his chest.

The older man handed me a damp rag and a small wooden bowl of clean water.

"Clean the lime-dust off your face, kid," he said gently. "It eats into the skin if you let it sit."

I took the rag with trembling fingers and wiped the heavy grey dust, dried tears, and road-grime from my cheeks, forehead, and jaw.

As the dirt came off, the firelight caught my features clearly.

The older man was looking at the survey slate resting on his knees. His thumb brushed over the custom brass notch filed into the handle—a notch my father had made himself. He looked up from the handle to my clean face, his eyes widening in sudden, stunned recognition.

"Orindessa...?" he whispered, his voice trembling.

Dain took a sharp step forward, staring at me in disbelief. "Orindessa? From the terrace village? What... what are you doing out here?"

The older man—Malrec—looked at me with a mixture of shock and deep, sorrowful concern. "Word reached the trade routes two weeks ago that Thomas had vanished into a thinned cut. Then we heard his daughter had vanished from the village. The surveyors on the high road were looking for you, child... We thought you were dead in a ditch."

Hearing my birth name spoken out loud hit me like a blow to the chest. It brought back the agonizing image of my mother's face—her eyes red with fury, her hand bruising my wrist as she screamed at me and slammed the heavy bracewood door.

A cold, hard shield snapped shut inside my heart. I flinched, pulling my shoulders straight.

"Orindessa stayed in the village," I said, my voice flat and icy. "That name belongs to a house that doesn't exist anymore. Don't call me that."

Dain scowled, turning to his father. "See? I told you. Another arrogant high-terrace child with a temper."

A rough, weathered raider sitting near the woodpile chuckled, spitting into the coals. "Fine by us, girl. You don't like your name, that's your business. But if you're holding Thomas's slate, how about you just be our *Surveyor*?"

I bristled instantly, clutching my blankets tighter. "I am a mender's daughter. I'm not joining a gang of road thieves."

The rough raider snorted. "Nobody walks the high cuts alone on an empty stomach, little surveyor. Out here, a lone girl with a slate is just meat for the road beasts. You read the thinned lines for us... we keep you fed and alive. Fair trade."

Dain stepped closer, his voice cutting and realistic. "You think you're going to survive out here on your own? You won't make it past the next ridge on bare boots. You need food and shelter as much as we need someone who can read weeping stone."

Malrec raised a hand, silencing the crew with quiet authority. He looked at me with steady, compassionate eyes.

"We are not murderers, Orindessa—or whatever you choose to call yourself," Malrec said softly. "The south routes are weeping, and our crew has been guessing where the stone is soft. You give us clean geometry, and you get a warm fire, a full pouch, and men who will keep the outlands from swallowing you whole while you search for your father."

***

I paused my story there, staring into the flickering Embercoal pit in my town yard.

Tavin was leaning forward, his eyes wide. "Did you agree right then?"

I let out a soft, dry laugh, resting my chin on my hands over the head of my cane.

"My pride wanted to stand on its own two feet, Tavin," I told him gently. "I bristled, I scowled, and I pretended I was weighing their offer like a grand master surveyor... But looking back now, my gut already knew the truth. When you have spent four nights shivering on bare limestone with nothing in your stomach but dust, the smell of hot salt-broth and the sight of a dry shelter tend to settle any argument your dignity tries to make. I pretended I was making a noble choice. In truth, I was a starving child who had nowhere else to go."

***

I accepted their deal.

The next morning, I went out on my first scouting run with Malrec's crew. I walked near the front of the line, my father's slate balanced on my forearm. When we reached the narrow shelf above Veilcut Ravine, the stone began to hum—a high, sickly vibration that meant the route was weeping and thin.

The raiders stopped, looking at me.

I held up the slate, aligned the sighting notch, and calculated the stress geometry just as my father had taught me. 

"The center shelf is hollowed out by Echo pressure," I told them, pointing with my chalk. "Step three paces to the left along the dark vein. The anchor-iron will hold there."

Malrec watched me work, a look of profound, tragic respect in his eyes. The crew followed my line safely around the drop, and whenever the stone got soft down the trail, they hollered back: *"Hey! Surveyor! Check the cut!"*

That evening, after we returned to our main camp, Malrec walked over and sat down beside me on a stone bench. He watched me carefully wiping the chalk dust off my father's slate.

"Calling you 'Surveyor' all day feels rude, kid," Malrec said quietly. His voice was gentle, fatherly. "It feels demeaning—like you're just a tool we brought along from the high cuts instead of a person. You deserve something that feels like a real name."

I didn't look up from my slate. "I told you. I don't have a name anymore."

Malrec smiled softly. "Sur-*VEY*-or... How about **Vey**?"

I stopped wiping the slate. I rolled the word over in my head. *Vey.* It was short. Sharp. Clean. It had no ties to the mother who had locked me out, no ties to the village that had turned its back, and no weight of broken promises. It was the name of someone who walked the roads and survived.

"Vey," I murmured. I looked up at Malrec and nodded. "Vey is fine."

***

The next day, Malrec led Dain and me deep into the cliffside trail, turning off into a hidden fissure in the rock.

"Come with me, Vey," Malrec said. "I want to show you where we keep our real work."

He led us down a long, sloping stone corridor into a massive underground cavern.

As soon as my boots hit the stone floor, a strange, terrifying chill ran up my spine. The air in the cavern was cool and dry, smelling of turned earth, wet salt, and old copper. Embercoal lanterns burned in sturdy iron brackets along the walls, casting a warm, golden glow across the chamber.

Everything was clean, sturdy, and active. Heavy stone platforms were carved neatly into the floor. Tool benches lined the perimeter, stacked with neat rows of survey slates, jars of Anchoring Salt, and iron mending pins. Fresh anchor-irons were forged straight into the solid limestone walls.

My heart began to hammer against my ribs. In my later years, I would see this exact cavern again—battered by time, its shelves collapsed, its stone scarred by the footsteps of Avery, Eli, and strangers from another world. But here, in my youth, it was pristine, active, and whole.

I stepped toward the curved western wall, drawn by an impossible, pulling memory.

The damp limestone was smooth under my palm. I ran my fingers along the rock, searching until my tips caught on a jagged, vertical groove.

I stopped dead in my tracks.

It was a **shoulder-height scratch**, clawed deep into the limestone.

Five years ago, when I was ten years old, I had snuck into this exact cavern system. I had been terrified by the dark, and my fingers had clawed into the wet wall right before my mother had grabbed my wrist and dragged me out into the light.

The realization hit me like a bucket of ice water poured over my head. My breath caught in my throat.

*This was the raider camp.*

Five years ago, my mother had bruised my wrist to drag me away from these dark tunnels, warning me that the outland raiders were monsters who would destroy everything I was. And now, at fifteen years old, I had walked right back into their cavern, taken their food, and joined their ranks. I had become the very thing she had tried to save me from.

"Vey?" Malrec's voice called out from the back alcove. "Are you alright?"

I pulled my hand away from the scratch, wiping my cold palm on my tunic. "I'm fine," I lied, my voice shaking slightly as I walked deeper into the chamber.

Malrec was standing before a massive alcove at the rear of the cavern. He reached up and pulled back a heavy canvas tarp, revealing his secret project.

Standing in the shadows was an unfinished **Waybound Frame**—a massive portal arch built from dark, polished stone, fitted with heavy anchor-iron brackets and intricate rune-slots along its inner rim. It was incomplete, missing its upper crown stone and Conduit core, but its sheer scale was breathtaking.

"What is this?" Dain asked, his eyes wide as he looked up at the stone arch.

"It is a way out," Malrec said softly. He looked at Dain, then turned his gaze to me. "I am building this in secret from the rest of the crew. If the south routes collapse completely—if the hunger out here turns men into beasts—this frame will give us a path to a world that isn't broken."

Malrec stepped closer to me, placing a heavy, warm hand on my shoulder.

"You are our surveyor now, Vey," Malrec said, his eyes filled with a heavy, foundational truth that would stay with me for the rest of my life. "You will see hard things on these roads. You will see men steal to keep their children fed. But listen to me now, and remember it when the road gets dark: *We do what we must to keep the hearth lit, Vey. But do not make the mistake of calling our hunger a law.*"

***

In the town yard, the Embercoal pit settled into red embers.

Tavin sat in silence for a long time, staring at the stone flags beneath his boots.

"Did you believe him?" Tavin asked softly. "About the frame?"

"I believed he loved his son," I told Tavin, my voice gentle in the quiet night. "And I believed he wanted to protect me. In the outlands, Tavin, that was the closest thing to a law any of us had."
