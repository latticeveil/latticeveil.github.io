# Knot 04: The Unapproved Mark

The high terraces do not forgive an amateur’s hand. 

I watch you, Tavin, turning that brass mending pin over in your palm. Your knuckles are still pink, your skin too soft to know the bite of raw lime-dust. You think mending is just a matter of matching lines on a slate. But the stone is not a drawing, and it does not wait for you to correct your spelling.

By the time I was fifteen, my mother’s house had become a tomb of dry timber and cold ash. The kitchen, once thick with the yeast-heavy hum of rising root-bread, had gone completely silent. Elena did not scream at me in those years. She did not throw things. She simply retreated into a robotic, flinching exile of her own making, setting my plate down without ever letting her eyes brush mine, as if my very skin carried the same erasing static as the Knot that took my father.

To the village menders, Thomas had become a tragedy—a cautionary tale of a surveyor who pushed too close to the bleeding edges of the Continuum. They spoke of him in past tense, a ghost whose name was only useful for warning children away from the lower cuts.

But to me, he was still My Hero. 

In the dark of my bedroom, clutching his heavy bracewood survey slate, I did not call him a hero. I called him *Papa*. I would trace the worn oil-grooves of the wood with my thumb, whispering directly into the empty space beside my bed. 

*“I’m going to find the seam tomorrow, Papa,”* I would whisper, my voice no louder than the draft whistling through the window-slate. *“I’m going to make the cuts straight. Just like you showed me. I’ll make them look at the wall and remember who you were. I’m going to bring you home.”*

I did not accept him as dead. I never would. The village built Sills—thick, stubborn walls of stone—just to keep the wild out. They wanted to hide from the thinned places. But I knew that if I wanted to find him, I had to go where the lines went slack.

At dawn, before the first cook-smoke rose from the hamlet, I slipped out with his mending chisel and the survey slate.

The weeping seam on the lower terrace cut was a narrow, ugly break. Cold, mineral-veined water trickled from the rockface, turning the gray limestone to a slick, dark charcoal. It was an active fracture, the kind of thinned space my father would have spent three days measuring before he ever touched a hammer to the wedge.

I was fifteen, and I had no patience for three days of math. I only had five years of my mother’s silent hollowing pressing against my ribs.

I braced the survey slate against my knee, trying to calculate the angles. The numbers on the wax looked simple, but when I held the chisel to the wet stone, my hand shook. The coordinate lines seemed to shift beneath my eyes.

*Measure twice,* my father’s memory whispered.

*No,* my anger answered. *I will show them today.*

I aligned the mending chisel and struck.

The blow was too hard, the value of the mark completely unchecked. I did not establish the boundary before I invited the conduit. 

For a heartbeat, the limestone behaved. Then, the Continuum’s **Echo** violently completed my sloppy geometry.

It was not a standard rockfall. The stone did not crumble; it *negotiated* the space with terrifying speed. The shelf beneath my boots shivered, the straight voxel-edges of the crop-wall shearing downward by six clean inches as the cliffside rearranged itself to fit my wrong mark. 

My left leg caught in the sudden, snapping gap. The stone clamped tight around my boot, holding me fast as the overhead supports cracked. 

"Papa!" I screamed. 

The teenage surveyor who called Thomas "My Hero" to the village vanished in an instant. I was ten years old again, covered in lime-dust, clawing at the cold, unyielding stone as the ceiling began to rain grit. 

"Papa! Please, save me! Papa!"

But the stone did not have a father's hand. It only had weight.

"Orindessa!"

A hand caught my shoulder, wrenching me backward with a desperate, lunging violence. 

It was my mother.

She had followed me. Her dark hair was completely loose, whipped wild by the ravine draft, her gray coat stained with the wet mineral muck of the terrace. With a strength that did not belong to her frail, hollowed frame, she drove a bracewood wedge into the gap beside my foot, throwing her shoulder against the lever until the stone groaned and released my boot.

She dragged me back onto the dry path just as the upper brace sheared, sending a heavy block of limestone crashing into the very space I had occupied.

We lay on the wet stone, both of us heaving, the air thick with the sharp, chemical sting of raw lime. I looked at the collapse, my chest tight with a terror I could not put into words.

Then, Elena stood up.

The paralyzed, silent stranger of the last five years was gone. In her place stood a woman driven completely mad by the trauma of nearly watching the stone eat the last piece of her family. She did not pull me into her arms. She grabbed me by the shoulders, her fingers digging through my wool coat hard enough to leave dark, immediate bruises, shaking me until my teeth clicked.

"Are you mad?!" she shrieked, her voice breaking into a manic, aggressive register I had never heard before. "Look at it! Look at what you did! You think you are a mender? You think you can play with the thinned stone and make it obey you?!"

"I... I was only trying to fix the wall, Mama!" I sobbed, the tears burning hot through the dust on my cheeks. "Like Papa did! I wanted to show them—"

"You are just like him!" she screamed, her face pale, her eyes wide and wild with a furious, unhinged grief. "Always chasing the seams! Always looking at the cliff as if it owed you a father! He was a fool who loved the stone more than his own home, and he let it swallow him whole! And you... you come here, playing with his slate, throwing your life into the same cracks! You want to slide into the dark and leave me with absolutely nothing?!"

The comparison cut deeper than the falling stone. My grief, buried under five years of her freezing silence, finally flared into a hot, defensive rage. I wrenched my shoulders out of her grip, my voice rising to match her shriek.

"He was not a fool!" I yelled back, my fists clenched, my whole body shaking. "He was a hero! He wanted to save this village, and he wanted to save *us*! If you hate me so much for trying to find him, if you can't even stand to look at my face—why don't I just go?! Why don't I just run away and leave you alone?!"

It was a teenager's empty, dramatic threat, a desperate plea for her to hold me, to shake her head, to tell me that she needed me to stay.

But Elena’s manic frenzy was past the point of maternal instinct. Her face hardened, her expression turning into a cold, terrifying mask of absolute exhaustion.

"Then go," she whispered. 

The whisper was louder than her screams. It carried the weight of a woman who had completely run out of hope.

"Mama..." I breathed, the anger evaporating from my chest, replaced by a cold, hollow dread.

"I don't care anymore, Orindessa," she said, her voice steady now, but carrying a sharp, mean edge that felt like a blade. "I don't care. You are going to get yourself killed by the stone anyway, just like he did. You are determined to follow him into the dark. Why should I have to stay here and watch it? Why should I have to be anywhere near your recklessness?!"

"I didn't mean—"

"Go!" she shrieked again, her manic anger snapping back with sudden, physical violence. 

She dragged me by the wrist back up the switchbacks, ignoring my stumbles, ignoring the way my bruised knee struck the sharp steps of the terrace. She hauled me into our yard, slammed the door of our home, and emerged seconds later.

She threw my father’s survey slate and a single wool blanket at my feet. The dark wood of the slate clattered against the dry flagstones, its brass pins catching the morning light.

"Let the outlands have you," she spat, her eyes dead, her mouth pulled into a hard, unforgiving line. "Let the stone have its mender. I am finished paying for his mistakes."

She stepped back inside.

The heavy bracewood door shut between us. The iron latch clicked into place with a dry, final thud. 

I stood in the cold morning wind of the high terraces, fifteen years old, clutching a dusty blanket and my father’s slate. I stared at the wood of the door, waiting for it to open, waiting for her to apologize to her "little bird" as she had five years ago.

The door did not open.

I did not cry. The cold of the stone seemed to creep up through my boots, settling deep behind my ribs until my chest felt as hard and silent as the Sills. 

I turned away from the house that was no longer a home. I took the road south, toward the lawless outland routes, where the raiders walked. I did not run to be a criminal. I ran because only the bandits were brave enough to walk the thinned paths where my hero had vanished. I ran to find my Papa.

Tavin has stopped playing with his measuring cord. He looks at his own pink knuckles, then up at her, his voice very small in the quiet.

"Did you ever go back, Sister?"

Orin looks toward the dark, silent switchbacks beyond the town gate.

"No," she says softly, her voice carrying the weathered weight of centuries. "By the time I learned enough knots to mend a home, the house was already gone. But the road has a way of giving you new family to carry, if you are brave enough to keep your hands open in the dark."
