# Knot 02: The Last Departure

The embers in the pit are still holding their square orange faces, but the wind from the ravine is beginning to slide over the terrace walls. It carries the smell of cold limestone and wet grass—the kind of air that doesn't apologize for what it takes.

I watch you, Tavin, wrapping your fingers around that spare bell line. You are holding it with the same tight, impatient grip. You look at the road and you see a line you can always walk. You think that because we mended the north cut yesterday, the path has promised to remain polite tomorrow.

We had three more winters like that first one I told you about. Three whole years where the bread rose straight, the crop-wells behaved, and my father’s boots returned to our wood-box every single evening. There was no sign of the drift in our hamlet, and the high cuts of the Continuum remained quiet. We had forgotten that peace is not a permanent state. We had forgotten that safety is just a debt we haven't been asked to pay yet.

It was a crisp morning in the fourth winter when the road decided to collect.

The morning began with a clean, ordinary sun clearing the peaks. I was sitting on my bracewood stool, watching my mother punch the first rise of the root-dough. Her hands were gentle, her hair pulled back in a neat, dark wrap that caught the early light. I was ten years old then, just old enough to think I knew how the world was put together, but young enough to still keep my toy wooden hauler tucked under my arm.

Then the latch clicked.

It wasn't my father. It was a young runner from the lower terraces, his boots grey with wet lime. He didn't step over our threshold. He stood on the sill, his fingers gripping the doorframe.

"Thomas," the boy panted, his breath coming in short, excited clouds. "The lower water catch. The seam near the crop-wall is weeping. The shadow lag is five seconds wide, and the bracewood is beginning to splinter."

My father rose from the table. He didn't rush. He had mended weeping seams dozens of times before—it was routine field work for him, the kind of chore he did between breakfast and lunch. He reached for his survey slate, his chalk, and the bracewood-braced coat that hung near the hearth.

My mother stopped her kneading. Her hands stayed sunk in the grey flour, her brow furrowed in a quiet, familiar worry. 

"Thomas," she said softly. "Let the senior surveyors take the line. You've spent three nights on the high ridges already."

"The senior surveyors are down in the lower hamlet mending the cistern arches, Elena," my father answered with a reassuring smile. He checked the nick in his field knife and tucked it into his belt. "If the crop-wall slips before the dry season, the shelves will lose their balance. The seed-jars will go dry for everyone. I'll be back before the second rise is done."

I stood up from my stool, running over to hug his waist. I squeezed him hard, my face pressed against the rough wool of his coat. 

"Go fix the stone, Hero Papa," I said, looking up at him with fierce, childish pride. "You're the strongest surveyor in the whole Continuum. The bad stone doesn't stand a chance."

My father laughed—that deep, warm rumble in his chest that always made the winter draft vanish. He crouched down until he was at my height, his dark eyes catching the orange glow of the hearth-fire. He rubbed a stray bit of grey chalk dust from his thumb onto my nose, making me giggle. 

"That's my brave little bird," he whispered, kissing my forehead.

He stood, and my mother let out a tired, gentle sigh, her worry vanishing in the warmth of his smile. She stepped forward, her hands still dusty with flour, and kissed his cheek. 

"I love you, Thomas. Be careful out there," she said.

"I love you both," he replied, winking at me. "Make sure the bread behaves."

He reached for the wooden latch, stepped over the sill, and walked out into the crisp morning air. Elena watched him go, her face soft and relaxed, before turning back to her mixing bowls.

It was a completely normal morning. We went about our chores without a single thought of danger. The root-dough rose beautifully, spilling over the edges of the clay tins. I sat on my stool, dragging my toy hauler across the clean stone flags, pretending it was navigating the high ridges. Elena began preparing a clean, damp towel, leaving it by the wood-box so she could wipe the limestone dust from his sleeves the moment he stepped through the door.

At noon, the latch clicked.

My mother called out happily, not even looking up from her ovens: "Thomas! You're early. I was just about to pull the loaves..."

But the man who stepped over the threshold was not my father.

It was Harlan, one of the other surveyors. He stood in the doorway, white-faced, trembling, his clothes covered in a fine, ash-like stone dust. He didn't step into the kitchen. He just stood there, breathing in short, shallow gasps, looking around our warm room as if he had forgotten what a house looked like.

"Harlan?" my mother asked, her smile faltering as she set the bread-paddle down. "What is it? Did Thomas finish the brace? Where is he?"

Harlan looked down at his hands, which were shaking so hard he had to press them against his thighs. When he spoke, his voice was flat, dry, and entirely empty.

"Elena... the line," he whispered. "It's clean."

My mother frowned, taking a step toward him. "Clean? What do you mean, clean? Did the seam stop weeping?"

"There is no seam," Harlan said, his eyes unfocused, staring past her at our hearth. "There is no crop-wall. It... it resolved. It's just flat. The whole ridge... it completed itself. There's nothing to mend anymore."

My mother let out a nervous, breathless laugh, her hand finding the edge of the table. "Harlan, you're not making sense. If the wall is fixed, then why are you covered in lime? Why isn't Thomas with you?"

Harlan looked up, his hollow eyes finally meeting hers, and the absolute blankness in his gaze made the warmth of our kitchen evaporate in an instant. 

"Elena, you don't understand," Harlan whispered, his voice cracking. "The slate... the chalk... they're gone. We dropped the measuring line, and it didn't find the bottom. It didn't find the stone. It didn't find... anything. There is no path left to walk. We can't even salt the place. There is nothing to burn. The Continuum just... took the end of the road."

I stepped forward, my toy hauler slipping from my fingers and clattering against the stone floor. I reached out, tugging hard on the dusty hem of Harlan's coat.

"Where is Papa?" I asked, my voice rising, suddenly small and terrified. "Where is my Hero Papa? Did he stay to help the crop hands?"

Harlan looked down at me, his mouth twitching, but no words came out. He couldn't look me in the eye. He simply closed his eyes and let his head drop, his silent, shaking shoulders delivering the terrible, unthinkable truth.

My father was gone. 

He had been caught in an active overworld Knot—a sudden, catastrophic collapse where the geometry of the route simply simplified itself, closing like a fist and erasing everything within its coordinates. It had left the stone perfectly flat, pristine, and empty. There were no boots left by the path. No survey slate. No scrap of wool from his coat. There was nothing left to bury, and nothing left to salt and burn.

I remember looking from Harlan's dusty boots to my mother's face. 

Elena didn't scream. She didn't weep. She stood frozen beside the flour-bin, her hands still white with the dust of the bread he had told us to watch. The loaves in the oven began to burn, the bitter, black smoke beginning to thick the air, but neither of us moved to save them. 

The silence that settled over our kitchen that afternoon was not the quiet of a sleeping house. It was the heavy, suffocating silence of the stone.
