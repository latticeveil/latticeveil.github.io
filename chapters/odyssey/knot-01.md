# Knot 01: Before the Cord Went Tight

The embers in the pit have a polite way of dying. If you watch them closely, the blocky edges of the Coal Chunks do not crumble into chaotic, drifting ash like the wild brush of the ridge. They simply shrink, row by clean row, their geometric orange faces dimming until they match the dark stone of the floor.

I watch you, Tavin, holding your measuring cord. Your fingers are twitching near the brass slide, eager to show me how quickly you can drop a line. You have the impatient look of a boy who thinks speed is the same thing as safety.

You are wrong. But I cannot teach you that with an order. I can only show you where the thread began.

You sit here on these squared benches, under stone arches that stay where we put them, and you think the world was always this structured. You think the rules we write on our slates are as natural as the rain. Some of you even talk about *The Sill*, *The Path*, and *The Latch* as if they were built into the clay when the Continuum was first smoothed out.

But they weren't.

In my grandfather’s time, before the drift started, there were no surveyors. There were no routekeepers, no sealwrights, and certainly no raiders waiting in the dark cuts of the rock. The Continuum was an unbroken sheet of honest stone. If you walked east, the road stayed east. If you built a wall, the corners didn't negotiate their angles when you turned your back. The world was normal, peaceful, and entirely free of danger. No one had ever heard of a thinned place, and the idea of a path repeating itself would have sounded like a child's fairy tale.

My grandfather lived his entire life without ever needing to worry. He never carried a surveyor's slate, because there was nothing to measure but the distance to his crops.

It was my father, Thomas, who was the first of us to see the world begin to slip. He was a *new* surveyor—one of the very first. He didn't have senior navigators to teach him, and he didn't have manuals written by men who had died proving the limits. He had only his eyes, a piece of dry chalk, and a growing dread that the stone beneath our feet was beginning to forget its own shape.

But long before he was a surveyor, long before the Continuum turned rude, he was just my father.

My earliest memories are wrapped in the smell of rising root-bread.

Our kitchen was small, but it was always warm. My mother kept the clay hearth sealed with a double-paste of lime and ash to keep the draft from stealing the heat, and on cold Continuum afternoons, the steam from her baking would thick the air until the window-slates went white with condensation. I would sit on a low bracewood stool near her flour-bin, my knees pulled up to my chin, watching her hands.

She was beautiful then. Her hair was still the dark, rich color of damp loam, and her fingers, though calloused from the crop lattice, were light when they touched my cheek.

"Keep your feet out of the flour-path, little bird," she would say, her voice carrying the low, comforting hum of water moving through a clean, unchoked cistern. "Your father will be home before the bread has finished its second rise, and he doesn't need to find our home white with your mischief."

I would laugh and pull my toes back, but my eyes always tracked toward the heavy wooden door.

My father was a caring husband and a gentle father, but to our community, he was also a genuine hero. He didn't wear a uniform, and he didn't carry a weapon—weapons solve the wrong problems in a world like this—but when the water in the lower well began to lag mid-breath, or when a crop lattice began to sag because the gravity under it was losing its grip, it was Thomas they called.

"He mends the bones of the terrace," my mother told me once as she wiped flour from her apron. She said it with a quiet pride that sat behind her eyes like a lamp. "Without his chalk, the crops would forget which way to grow."

When the latch finally clicked on those cold evenings, I would be off my stool before the door had even finished its swing.

He always smelled of cold limestone and fresh spruce resin. He would stand in the doorway for three measured breaths—never rushing, even when the wind was biting at his back—and count on his fingers. *One. Two. Three.* He did it as a silent safety-check of our threshold, ensuring the road had not followed him through the door.

It was my father who coined the terms we still use, though back then, we used the "Old Way" of speaking. He didn't talk about Frames, Conduits, or Limiters. Those are clinical, sterile words born of the real-world visitors and their cold machines. My father used the language of the home.

He called the boundary **The Sill**. To him, a frame was like the literal wooden threshold of our house—the thing that declared where the inside began and the outside was forced to stop.

He called the passage **The Path**. A conduit was just a road between the crop terraces, a channel meant to let people pass through without letting the drift settle.

And he called the ending **The Latch**. A limiter was just the wooden bar dropped across the door at night to remind the cold night wind that its permission had ended.

*The Sill. The Path. The Latch.*

He would check them on his knuckles before he stepped over our threshold. Only then would he look at me and smile.

"Well, Orindessa," he would say, his voice deep and dry, but carrying a warmth that made the winter draft vanish. "Is the bread still behaving, or have you and your mother decided to let the kitchen drift?"

"It's rising straight, Father," I would tell him, reaching for his hand. His palms were wide and rough, the skin at the side of his thumb stained grey with survey chalk.

He would lift me in one easy motion, his shoulder solid beneath my knees, and carry me toward the hearth.

My parents loved each other deeply. You could see it in the way my mother would set his cup down—not quickly, but pausing long enough for her hand to rest on his shoulder. You could see it in the way he would look up from his survey slate, his serious eyes softening the moment her shadow fell across his page. They did not have the loud, bitter shouting matches that some of the neighbors kept behind their shutters. Their lives were balanced, braced against one another like two timbers holding up a shared roof.

When they disagreed, it was always peaceful. My mother would lean against the table's edge, her fingers tracing the old grain of the wood, her voice soft with worry.

"The lower cut is shifting again, Thomas," she would say gently. "The crop hands said the shadow lag is three seconds wide near the salt-wells. Let the older surveyors take the line. You've spent three nights on the high ridges already, and the wind is coming from the wrong side of the ravine."

My father would look at her hand, then up at her face. He did not get angry. He did not tell her she was foolish.

"The village needs the wall straight, Elena," he would answer softly, his fingers closing over hers. "If the crop-wall slips, the seed-jars go dry for everyone. I'll be careful. I promise."

My mother would look at him for a long moment, her sigh like the rustle of dry Rootvine leaves, and then she would nod. She would not turn away in anger. She would simply go back to her ovens, her fingers working the dough with a little more urgency, as if she could knead her worry into the bread and make it harmless.

And I would sit beside him, my head resting against his sleeve, watching him mend my toys with his field knife.

I had a small toy hauler then, carved from soft pine. One of its wheels—a perfect, straight-cut square of timber—had split along the grain when I let it drop from the crop terrace. My father did not throw it away. He did not tell me I was reckless.

He took his field knife—the one with the small nick in the blade that left a double-trail on the wood—and sat with me by the hearth.

"A broken sill doesn't mean the journey is finished, Orindessa," he said, his thumb smoothing the split pine. "It only means the weight has to be redistributed. You see the grain here? If you cut across it, the wood will always find a way to argue with you. But if you follow the line the tree chose, the join will hold even when the road gets rude."

He worked slowly, his knife shaving the pine in thin, curling ribbons that smelled of summer resin. I watched his hands—the grey chalk dust on his knuckles, the steady, unhurried pressure of his blade. To me, he was the strongest thing in the Continuum. He was my hero, and I believed that as long as his boots sat by our wood-box, the Continuum could never find a way to hurt us.

When the toy was whole again, he did not give it back immediately. He set it on his slate, exactly where his three survey marks met.

"Measure twice, Orin," he murmured, his dark eyes catching the orange glow of the embers. "And cut once. The stone doesn't care about your hurry, and it never apologizes when you make a mistake."

I nodded, though I was too young to know what the stone’s silence cost. I only knew the kitchen was warm, the bread smelled of sweet starch, and my father was sitting beside me.

Before we went to our beds, he would always lift me onto his shoulders. It was our ritual. He would walk to the small shuttered window at the back of the house, push the slate aside, and let the cold Continuum wind touch our faces.

"Look there, Orindessa," he whispered, pointing his grey-stained finger toward the high, dark ridges.

From his shoulders, the village looked like a constellation of small, steady lanterns hung against the black cliff-face. I could see the crop lattices, the square cistern roofs, and the narrow switchbacks running down toward the valley. It all looked so straight, so quiet.

"You see where the timber meets the stone arch at the edge of the crop wall?" he asked, his hand steadying my ankle.

"Yes," I breathed, my fingers digging into his dark hair.

"That is the **Brace-line**," he said, his voice dropping into the slow, patient cadence he used when the road was listening. "That is where the weight of the mountain decides to rest. As long as that joint stays true, the people below can sleep without keeping one eye on the ceiling. It's a heavy debt, Orin, but someone has to hold the line so the bread can rise in peace."

I leaned my cheek against his head, my eyes tracing the clean, straight edge where our roof-beam met the terrace rock.

"I want to hold the line too, Father," I whispered.

He laughed—a soft, low rumble in his chest that I could feel through my boots. He did not tell me I was too small. He only pressed his hand against my knee and held me closer.

"Then we will have to teach you how to measure, little bird," he said. "But not tonight. Tonight, the joint is true, and the bread is warm, and the road is sleeping."

He closed the slate shutter, blocking out the wind, and carried me back toward the hearth.

That was the last winter we had together. That was the last season my home smelled of rising bread, and the last time I went to sleep believing the world was an unsplit slab of honest stone.

***

Old Sister Orin pauses, her hand moving to her wrapped wrist, her thumb tracing the linen where her weighted cord lies still. She looks at the young surveyors around her, their eyes wide in the steady, orange ember-light.

“You look at me now,” she says quietly, her voice weathered as the cliff-side. “And you see a woman who knows every wrong turn between here and the Void. But remember this: before the stone taught me how to bleed, it taught me how to love. And if my father had not been the man he was, I would have died a raider, with nothing but Vey’s blood on my hands.”

She leans back in her rocking chair, the bracewood giving a soft, compliant creak.

“Sleep now,” she commands, her eyes dropping back to the embers. “Tomorrow, the work begins. And tomorrow, I will tell you about the morning the quiet took him.”
